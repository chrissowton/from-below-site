Civil rights & anti-racism

The Independent Party of Colour and the Massacre of 1912

Black veterans of Cuba's independence war founded a party to demand equality. When it was banned, they protested — and thousands of Afro-Cubans were massacred.

When
1908 – 1912
Where
Oriente Province, Cuba

01

What is the background to this event?

Afro-Cubans made up a large part of the Liberation Army that fought for Cuba's independence from Spain between 1868 and 1898. The independence leader José Martí promised a nation "with all and for the good of all", without racial divisions.

  • But after independence in 1902, under strong US influence, Afro-Cubans were excluded from good jobs and public office, and faced racism.
  • Most Afro-Cubans lived and worked in poverty, many on the sugar plantations of eastern Cuba.1
  • In 1908, veterans led by Evaristo Estenoz and Pedro Ivonnet founded the Independent Party of Colour (Partido Independiente de Color, PIC) to demand equal rights.1

03

What happened?

The PIC quickly gained support among Afro-Cubans.1 In 1910, Congress passed the Morúa law, which banned political parties based on race, and the party was outlawed.1

On 20 May 1912, the party's leaders launched an armed protest to demand that the ban be lifted.1

  • Several hundred rebels, mainly peasants and lightly armed, rose in Oriente Province.1
  • Some rebels burned property, including the town of La Maya, and attacked stores and estates.
  • The United States sent Marines to protect American-owned mines, railways and plantations.1
  • The Cuban government and press responded with racist propaganda, portraying the rebels as savages threatening "civilisation".1

The army, rural police and white volunteer militias then carried out massacres of Afro-Cubans, many of them not involved in the rising.1 Estenoz was shot on 27 June and Ivonnet killed soon after.1 Between 3,000 and 6,000 people were killed.1

04

What happened next?

  • Silence: the massacre was largely erased from Cuban history for decades.
  • Fear: it discouraged independent Afro-Cuban political organisation for a generation.
  • Revolution: after 1959, the revolutionary government declared racism abolished, but historians and Afro-Cuban activists argue that racial inequality persisted.
05

What do others say?

At the time

Critics argued that a party based on race would divide the nation, and that the rebels were a threat to order. The PIC argued that Afro-Cubans were already excluded, and that they needed their own voice.

Historians' debates

  • A "race war"? Aline Helg and others show that the rebellion was a limited armed protest, and that the "race war" was mainly the killing of Afro-Cubans by the state and white militias.
  • The Morúa law: historians debate whether it was a genuine attempt at racial unity or a tool to exclude Black political organisation.
  • Rebel violence: rebels destroyed property, but caused few deaths; the scale of the government's response was out of all proportion.
06

The evidence

  • Cuban newspapers of the time, which reveal racist attitudes.
  • US military and diplomatic records.

07

How is it remembered?

  • Afro-Cuban activists and historians have worked to recover the memory of 1912, especially around its centenary in 2012.
  • The filmmaker Gloria Rolando's documentary 1912: Breaking the Silence (2010–12) helped bring the story to light.
08

Why does it matter?

The 1912 massacre is one of the darkest episodes in Cuban history. It shows how the promise of a nation without racism was betrayed, and how Black political organisation was met with extreme violence.

  • It raises questions about whether banning race-based parties promotes equality or silences discrimination.
  • It connects to wider histories of racial violence in the Americas.
