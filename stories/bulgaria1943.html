Stopping the Trains: Bulgaria 1943

When the Bulgarian government secretly agreed to deport the country's Jews to Nazi death camps, townspeople, MPs, church leaders and ordinary citizens protested. Bulgaria's 48,000 Jews survived — but the 11,343 Jews in Bulgarian-occupied lands did not.

When
March – May 1943
Where
Kyustendil and Sofia, Bulgaria

01

What is the background to this event?

Bulgaria was allied with Nazi Germany. From 1941 it passed anti-Jewish laws and took over Greek and Yugoslav territories.1

  • About 48,000 Jews lived in Bulgaria, more than half in Sofia.1
  • In early 1943, the government agreed with the SS to deport 20,000 Jews, starting with those in the occupied territories.12
  • The Jews of Kyustendil and Plovdiv were among the first to be targeted in Bulgaria itself.1

    The king, whose decisions remain debated.

In their own words

03

What happened?

In early March 1943, the Jews of Kyustendil were ordered to leave their homes with only a few belongings. Citizens of the town appointed a delegation to go to Sofia and ask the government to cancel the order.2

  • On 9 March, the delegation met Dimitar Peshev. Together they confronted the interior minister, who cancelled the deportation from Bulgaria proper.12
  • Peshev then gathered the signatures of 43 government MPs on a letter of protest. He was removed as deputy speaker.1
  • The Holy Synod of the Orthodox Church and leading writers protested; unions, professional bodies and citizens sent petitions.1
  • In Plovdiv, Metropolitan Kiril is said to have threatened to lie down on the railway tracks.
  • In May 1943, the government expelled Sofia's 25,743 Jews to the countryside and seized their property, but did not deport them.1

Those who were not saved

In March 1943, the Bulgarian authorities rounded up and deported 11,343 Jews from occupied Macedonia, Thrace and Pirot. They were sent to Treblinka, where almost all were murdered.1

04

What happened next?

  • Survival: Bulgaria's Jews survived the war — one of the highest survival rates in Axis Europe.1
  • Peshev was tried by the communist People's Court in 1945 and imprisoned. He died in obscurity in 1973 and was later recognised by Yad Vashem as Righteous Among the Nations.
  • Emigration: most of Bulgaria's Jews emigrated to Israel after 1948.
05

What do others say?

At the time

The government kept the deportation plans secret and presented the expulsion from Sofia as a labour measure.1

Debates since

  • The king's role: some credit Tsar Boris III with saving the Jews; others stress that his government organised the deportations from the occupied lands.3
  • The communists later claimed the credit for the party, which historians reject.3
  • Memory: Greek and North Macedonian historians and Jewish organisations have urged Bulgaria to acknowledge its responsibility for the deported Jews.

07

How is it remembered?

  • A monument to Peshev stands in Kyustendil.
  • Bulgaria marks 10 March as a day of remembrance of the rescue and of the victims of the Holocaust.
08

Why does it matter?

Bulgaria's story shows that public protest could stop deportations — and that the same state could at the same moment send thousands of other Jews to their deaths.

  • The rescue began with ordinary townspeople.

What other events are like this?

  • 1941The February Strike — After German police seized 425 Jewish men in Amsterdam, the city's tram drivers, dockers and factory workers walked out. It was the first mass public protest against the persecution of Jews in Nazi-occupied Europe.
