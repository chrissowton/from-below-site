The Barricades of Riga

Fearing a Soviet attack, Latvians built barricades of concrete, logs and farm tractors around their parliament and key buildings — and kept watch around bonfires for two weeks in the January cold.

When
13 – 27 January 1991
Where
Riga and other towns, Latvia

See it on the map →

01

What is the background to this event?

The Soviet Union occupied Latvia in 1940 and again in 1944.1

  • Under Gorbachev's reforms, the Popular Front of Latvia and other groups campaigned openly for independence.1
  • In March 1990, independence supporters won the elections to the Supreme Council, which declared the restoration of independence in May 1990.1
  • Soviet troops, KGB and the OMON special police remained in Latvia.1
  • On 2 January 1991, the OMON seized the Press House in Riga.1
02

Who were the key people?

  • The Popular Front of Latvia

    The mass movement that called people to the barricades.

  • Farmers and lorry drivers

    Who brought tractors, trucks and heavy machinery to block the streets.

  • Andris Slapiņš and Gvido Zvaigzne

    Film-makers killed while filming the attack of 20 January.

  • The OMON

    Soviet special police loyal to Moscow.

03

What happened?

After the Soviet attack in Vilnius on 13 January 1991, the Latvian government called on people to build barricades to protect key buildings in Riga and other towns.1

  • Tens of thousands of people came from across the country. They blocked streets with concrete blocks, logs, farm machinery and lorries.
  • Volunteers kept watch around bonfires day and night, around the parliament, the television and radio buildings and the telephone exchange.
  • Six people were killed in OMON attacks. Most were shot on 20 January 1991, when the OMON attacked the Latvian Ministry of the Interior; among them were the film-makers Andris Slapiņš and Gvido Zvaigzne.1
  • Another person died in an accident while reinforcing the barricades.1
  • By about 27 January the barricades were dismantled. The Soviet army did not attack the parliament.1

04

What happened next?

  • Independence: Latvia declared full independence on 21 August 1991, during the failed coup in Moscow.
  • Recognition: about 32,000 people later received a medal for taking part in or supporting the barricades.1
05

What do others say?

At the time

Pro-Soviet groups called for Moscow's direct rule; independence supporters saw the OMON attacks as an attempted coup.1

Debates since

  • Violence: the defenders were unarmed civilians; the deaths were caused by OMON fire, and the number of casualties among Soviet loyalists is unknown.1
  • Russian speakers: some Russian-speaking residents joined the barricades; others opposed independence.
06

The evidence

  • Film shot by Slapiņš and Zvaigzne and other camera crews.
  • The Museum of the Barricades of 1991 in Riga.
07

How is it remembered?

  • Each January, bonfires are lit in Riga in memory of the barricades.
08

Why does it matter?

The barricades were a mass act of non-violent defence that helped protect Latvia's restored independence.

  • They united people from the city and countryside.

Questions to think about

  1. Why did Latvians build barricades rather than take up arms?
  2. Why were farmers and their tractors so important?
  3. Why did the Soviet army not attack the parliament?

09

What other events are like this?

  • 1991The January Events in Vilnius — When Soviet tanks rolled towards Lithuania's television tower and parliament, tens of thousands of unarmed people came out to defend them. Fourteen civilians were killed — but the parliament was never taken.
  • 1989The Baltic Way — Around two million people joined hands in a human chain 675 kilometres long, across Estonia, Latvia and Lithuania, to demand freedom from the Soviet Union.
  • 1988Estonia's Singing Revolution — Night after night in June 1988, crowds gathered at Tallinn's Song Festival Grounds to sing forbidden songs. The artist Heinz Valk gave the movement its name: the Singing Revolution.
