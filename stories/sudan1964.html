Sudan's October Revolution

When police killed a student at a university debate about the war in the south, Sudanese professionals, workers and students launched a general strike that brought down a military dictatorship in days.

When
21 October – 15 November 1964
Where
Khartoum, Sudan
Facts checked
2026-11-16

01

What is the background to this event?

Sudan became independent from Anglo-Egyptian rule in 1956. Two years later, in November 1958, General Ibrahim Abboud led a military coup, dissolved parliament and banned political parties, saying he would end "the state of degeneration, chaos, and instability of the country".1

  • Abboud's government settled the dispute with Egypt over the Nile waters and improved the economy for a time, but it built no political support outside the army.1
  • In the south, where most people were neither Arab nor Muslim, the government pursued a policy of Arabisation and Islamisation, expelling Christian missionaries and taking over schools.1
  • This provoked strikes in schools and open revolt. By 1963 a full civil war was under way; Abboud's forces were responsible for many deaths, and large numbers of southerners fled as refugees.1

In August 1964, Abboud set up a commission to study the "southern problem". When it called for public debate, students at the University of Khartoum organised a series of debates that quickly became a forum for open criticism of the regime. The government banned them.1

In their own words

03

What happened?

On the night of 21 October 1964, students at the University of Khartoum defied the ban and held a meeting to discuss the war in the south. Police stormed the campus to break it up, firing on the students. One student, Ahmed al-Qurashi Taha, was killed, and others were wounded.12

His funeral the next day became a huge demonstration. Over the following days, Sudanese society rose against the regime:2

  • University teachers resigned, and lawyers, judges, doctors and engineers formed a Professionals' Front, which joined with trade unions and the banned political parties.
  • A general strike paralysed Khartoum and spread across the country. Railways, offices and public services stopped.
  • Women took part prominently in the demonstrations and in the professional and union organisations.

Rather than suppress the opposition by armed force, Abboud dissolved his government on 26 October 1964, and allowed a provisional cabinet to be formed under Sirr al-Khatim al-Khalifa. On 15 November, Abboud himself resigned, ending Sudan's first period of military rule.1

04

What happened next?

  • Women's vote: the transitional government gave women the right to vote, and in the 1965 elections Fatima Ahmed Ibrahim became the first woman elected to Sudan's parliament.
  • The south: a Round Table Conference on the south was held in March 1965, but it failed to reach agreement, and the civil war continued until 1972.
  • Parliamentary rule: elections in 1965 restored party government, but it was unstable. In May 1969, Colonel Jaafar Nimeiri seized power in another coup.
  • A tradition of uprising: Sudanese people overthrew military rulers again in the April 1985 uprising and in the revolution of 2018–19.
05

What do others say?

At the time

Supporters of the regime blamed communists and party politicians for exploiting the student death. The revolutionaries presented the movement as the will of the whole Sudanese people.

Historians' debates

  • Why did Abboud step down? Historians point to divisions within the army, the strength of the general strike, and Abboud's own unwillingness to order mass bloodshed.
  • The south: southern Sudanese leaders welcomed the fall of Abboud but were disappointed that the new governments did not end the war. Historians debate how far the revolution addressed the concerns of the south.
  • A model for later uprisings: Willow Berridge compares the uprisings of 1964 and 1985, showing how professionals' unions and the "October" tradition shaped Sudanese politics, including in 2019.

07

How is it remembered?

  • 21 October is remembered in Sudan as the anniversary of the revolution, and protesters returned to the streets on that date in 2021 and 2022.
  • Songs of the October Revolution, such as those of the singer Mohammed Wardi, are still sung at demonstrations.
  • The University of Khartoum remembers Ahmed al-Qurashi Taha as a martyr of the revolution.
08

Why does it matter?

Sudan's October Revolution was one of the first times in Africa or the Arab world that an unarmed popular movement brought down a military government. Students, professionals and workers, using strikes and demonstrations, forced a dictator to step down in less than a month.

  • It created a model of civil disobedience — led by professionals' and workers' organisations — that Sudanese people used again in 1985 and 2019.
  • It linked the struggle for democracy in the north with the war in the south.
  • It opened the way for women's political rights in Sudan.

What other events are like this?

  • 2010The Tunisian Revolution — A street vendor's desperate protest in a small town sparked a month of uprising that toppled a dictator of 23 years and began the Arab Spring.
  • 1919Egypt's 1919 Revolution — When Britain exiled the nationalist leader Saad Zaghlul, Egyptians of every class and religion rose up in strikes and demonstrations — and women marched in public for the first time.
  • 1973The Thai Student Uprising of 14 October 1973 — When thirteen activists were arrested for handing out leaflets calling for a constitution, half a million people filled the streets of Bangkok and brought down a military dictatorship.
