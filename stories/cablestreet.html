The Battle of Cable Street

When the British Union of Fascists tried to march through the Jewish East End, tens of thousands of local people blocked the way.

When
4 October 1936
Where
Cable Street and Gardiner's Corner, East London
Facts checked

01

What is the background to this event?

Sir Oswald Mosley, a former Conservative and Labour MP, founded the British Union of Fascists (BUF) in 1932 after visiting Mussolini's Italy. Its members wore black uniforms and held large rallies. From around 1934 the BUF increasingly adopted open antisemitism and concentrated its efforts on areas with large Jewish populations, above all the East End of London.

The East End had been the centre of Jewish life in Britain for generations. Around 150,000 Yiddish-speaking Jews had come to Britain at the end of the nineteenth century, many settling in Whitechapel, Stepney and Spitalfields. In the mid-1930s these were poor, overcrowded districts with high unemployment, which the BUF sought to exploit with marches, street meetings and intimidation.

In September 1936 the BUF announced a march through the East End on Sunday 4 October, with four open-air meetings. Local people tried to stop it peacefully:

  • The Jewish People's Council organised a petition calling for the march to be banned, signed by around 100,000 people, including the mayors of five East London boroughs.
  • The petition was presented to the Home Office on 2 October by a broad delegation including Labour MPs and local representatives.
  • The Home Secretary, Sir John Simon, declined to ban the march, and the Metropolitan Police prepared to protect its route.

Who were the key people?

  • Oswald Mosley

    Leader of the British Union of Fascists.

  • Sir Philip Game

    Commissioner of the Metropolitan Police, who ultimately ordered the march to be abandoned.

  • The Jewish People's Council against Fascism and Anti-Semitism

    An umbrella body of local Jewish organisations that led the petition and the call to resist.

  • Phil Piratin

    A local Communist organiser, later elected MP for Mile End in 1945, who wrote an account of the day in Flame Can't Be Hidden (1948).

  • Max Levitas

    A young Jewish Communist from Stepney, described in 2018 as the last survivor of the battle.

  • Irish dockers and local residents

    Non-Jewish East Enders, including many Irish Catholic dock workers, who joined their Jewish neighbours on the barricades.

  • The women of Cable Street

    Residents who threw rubbish, rotten vegetables and the contents of chamber pots from upper windows onto the police below.

03

What happened?

The BUF planned to assemble at Royal Mint Street, near Tower Hill, where Mosley would review his forces before they marched east in four columns.2 Thousands of police, including mounted officers, were deployed to keep the route open.

Anti-fascists organised under the slogan "They shall not pass" — the cry associated with the defence of Verdun in 1916 and, at that moment, with the defence of Madrid in the Spanish Civil War.2 Their strategy was to occupy the key junctions through which the march would have to pass.

  • A vast crowd — estimates vary widely, but most exceed 100,000 — blocked Gardiner's Corner, the junction of Whitechapel High Street, Commercial Street, Commercial Road and Leman Street.23
  • When police attempted to clear an alternative route along Cable Street, residents and anti-fascists built barricades from furniture, paving stones and an overturned lorry.3
  • Police repeatedly charged the barricades with batons and were repeatedly forced back.

Faced with the impossibility of forcing a passage without very serious bloodshed, Sir Philip Game instructed Mosley to abandon the march. The fascists marched west instead, through the deserted City of London to the Embankment, where they dispersed. Mounted police moved through the waiting crowds in the East End announcing: "All over, there will be no march, you can go home."2

Many of those arrested on the day were anti-fascists. Some were subsequently imprisoned for their part in the confrontations with the police.3

04

What happened next?

  • New law: Parliament passed the Public Order Act 1936, which banned political uniforms and gave the police powers to impose conditions on, or ban, processions. Sir Philip Game believed the uniform ban would reduce the BUF's "spectacular appeal to the young and foolish".
  • The far right did not vanish: antisemitic attacks continued in the East End in the following weeks, and the BUF campaigned on until it was banned and Mosley interned in 1940.
  • Memory: a large mural depicting the battle was painted on the side of St George's Town Hall in Cable Street between 1979 and 1983, and a commemorative plaque was erected in 2008.
05

Why does it matter?

Cable Street has become a symbol of community solidarity against fascism: Jewish and Irish, religious and secular, communist and Labour, standing together in the streets where they lived. Historians debate how decisive it was in the BUF's decline, but its significance as an example of collective, local resistance is beyond doubt.

  • It demonstrated that a community could refuse to allow its streets to be used for intimidation.
  • It is frequently invoked in modern mobilisations against the far right in Britain and beyond.
  • It raises enduring questions about free speech, policing and the state's duty to protect minorities.

What do others say?How it was seen at the time, and where historians disagree

At the time

The British Union of Fascists claimed that the government had surrendered to "Red" violence and that its right to free speech had been denied. Some newspapers condemned both sides for disorder. Within the anti-fascist movement there was disagreement too: the Jewish Board of Deputies and the Jewish Chronicle urged Jews to stay away, and the Labour Party leadership discouraged confrontation, while the Communist Party and local organisations called for mass resistance.

Historians' debates

  • Did it defeat fascism? Some historians argue that Cable Street was a turning point; others point out that BUF support in parts of the East End actually rose in the months that followed, and that the Public Order Act did more damage to the movement.
  • The Communist Party's role: historians debate how far the Communist Party led the mobilisation and how far it followed the lead of local Jewish and labour activists.
  • The numbers involved on all sides remain contested.
How do we know?The evidence, and a source from the time
  • Police reports and Home Office files, now in the National Archives.
  • Newspaper coverage and newsreel footage.
  • Memoirs, especially Phil Piratin's Flame Can't Be Hidden, written from a Communist perspective.
  • Oral histories recorded with participants, many of them in the 1980s and 1990s.

How is it remembered?Memorials, anniversaries and arguments over memory
  • The large mural on St George's Town Hall was painted between 1979 and 1983 and restored several times after vandalism.
  • Anniversary marches, notably the 80th in 2016, have drawn thousands of people.
  • Cable Street is regularly invoked by anti-racist and anti-fascist movements in Britain.
