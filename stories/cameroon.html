Cameroon's Hidden War

When France banned Cameroon's main independence party, its supporters took to the forests. The war that followed killed tens of thousands — and France only acknowledged it in 2025.

When
1955 – 1971
Where
Sanaga-Maritime and the Bamileke region, Cameroon
Facts checked

01

What is the background to this event?

After the First World War, the former German colony of Kamerun was divided between France and Britain as League of Nations mandates, later United Nations trust territories. Unlike ordinary colonies, these were supposed to be prepared for self-government.

  • In 1948, Ruben Um Nyobè, a trade unionist and civil servant, and Félix-Roland Moumié founded the Union of the Peoples of Cameroon (UPC), which demanded independence and the reunification of the French and British territories.1
  • Um Nyobè took the UPC's case to the United Nations in New York. The UPC grew into a mass movement.1
  • The French administration worked to block the UPC: in the 1951 elections, it delayed accepting Um Nyobè's candidacy and placed few polling stations in areas where he was popular.1

In their own words

03

What happened?

On 22 April 1955, the UPC issued a "Joint Proclamation" demanding independence. In May 1955, pro-independence riots broke out in Douala, Yaoundé and other cities; they were put down by the new French High Commissioner, Roland Pré.1 In July 1955, the French authorities banned the UPC. Um Nyobè and other leaders went into hiding.1

The conflict grew into an armed insurgency:1

  • UPC fighters formed a guerrilla movement in the forests of the Sanaga-Maritime region, among the Bassa people, and later in the Bamileke region of the west.
  • From December 1957, Lieutenant Colonel Jean Lamberton directed a "pacification zone" (ZOPAC), in which villagers were forcibly regrouped into guarded camps, using methods developed in Indochina and Algeria.
  • On 13 September 1958, Um Nyobè was tracked down and killed in the forest. His body was dragged through villages and buried in concrete.

The war did not end with independence. Cameroon became independent on 1 January 1960 under President Ahmadou Ahidjo, who signed a military agreement with France. With French help, the Cameroonian army put down the revolt in the Bamileke region, using heavy artillery and napalm and destroying hundreds of villages. Heads of people killed were publicly displayed.1 Most estimates put the death toll in the tens of thousands; in 1964 the British embassy estimated 61,300 to 76,300 civilian deaths, 80 per cent of them in the Bamileke region.1

04

What happened next?

  • Assassination abroad: in November 1960, Félix-Roland Moumié died after being poisoned with thallium in Geneva.
  • The end of the revolt: the rebellion was largely defeated by 1964, though clashes continued. Ernest Ouandié was captured in 1970 and publicly executed in Bafoussam in January 1971.
  • Silence: under Ahidjo and his successor Paul Biya, the UPC's history was suppressed in Cameroon, and in France the war was hardly known.
  • Acknowledgement: a joint commission of French and Cameroonian historians reported in January 2025, and later that year President Emmanuel Macron acknowledged that France had waged a war of repression in Cameroon.
05

What do others say?

At the time

The French authorities and, after 1960, the Cameroonian government presented the UPC as communist terrorists and bandits. The UPC presented itself as a national liberation movement forced into armed struggle by the banning of peaceful politics.

Historians' debates

  • Violence on all sides: historians document massive violence by French and Cameroonian forces, including napalm, torture, forced regroupment and summary executions. They also record that UPC fighters killed political opponents, chiefs, civil servants and civilians they considered collaborators, and that the war became, in places, a brutal civil conflict between Cameroonians.
  • How many died? Estimates range from tens of thousands to, according to some writers, several hundred thousand. The British embassy's 1964 estimate of 61,300–76,300 civilian deaths is often cited.
  • Who was responsible after 1960? Historians such as Thomas Deltombe and his co-authors argue that France continued to direct the war after independence; others emphasise the responsibility of the Ahidjo government.
06

The evidence

  • French military and diplomatic archives, partly opened to the joint commission.
  • British diplomatic reports, including the 1964 casualty estimate.
  • The UPC's own documents, and Um Nyobè's writings and speeches to the United Nations.
  • Oral testimonies collected in the Bassa and Bamileke regions.

07

How is it remembered?

  • For decades the UPC's leaders were officially ignored in Cameroon; in 1991 a law rehabilitated them as national heroes.
  • Statues and memorials to Um Nyobè have been erected, including at Eséka.
  • The 2011 book Kamerun! and the 2025 report brought the war to public attention in France.
08

Why does it matter?

The Cameroon War was one of the bloodiest wars of decolonisation in Africa, yet it was so little known that it has been called the "hidden war". It shows how a colonial power could shape a country's independence by destroying the movement that had demanded it.

  • Um Nyobè's campaign at the United Nations was an early example of anti-colonial diplomacy.
  • The war shaped Cameroon's political system and its relationship with France for decades.
  • Recent acknowledgement shows how historical research and public pressure can bring hidden histories to light.
