The Namibian Contract Workers' Strike

About 25,000 migrant workers struck against a contract-labour system many compared to slavery. It was the largest strike in Namibia's history, and a turning point in the fight against South African rule.

When
December 1971 – March 1972
Where
Windhoek, Walvis Bay and Tsumeb, Namibia
Facts checked
2026-11-16

See it on the map →

01

What is the background to this event?

Namibia, then called South West Africa, was ruled by South Africa under apartheid.1

  • Black people in the northern reserves could only leave by signing 12–18-month labour contracts with a state-linked agency, SWANLA.1
  • Workers could not bargain, had to carry passes, and faced criminal penalties for quitting or organising.1
  • They lived in compounds controlled by employers, and women were barred from signing contracts.1
  • Many described the system as close to slavery or serfdom.1

In their own words

03

What happened?

The strike began on 13 December 1971 in Windhoek and on 14 December in Walvis Bay.1

  • It spread to the US-owned Tsumeb mine and beyond.1
  • About 25,000 workers took part, most from Ovamboland in the north.1
  • The strikers demanded an end to the contract-labour system.1
  • In January 1972, people in Ovamboland tore down more than 100 km of border fence, and there were attacks on officials and informers.1
  • Police killed three men at Ondobe and five workers at Epinga.1

04

What happened next?

  • Reform: the strike ended in March 1972; SWANLA was abolished, though contract labour continued in a new form.
  • Independence: the strike strengthened the movement that won Namibia's independence in 1990.
  • A hidden grave: a mass grave of five workers killed at Epinga became publicly known only in 2008.1
05

What do others say?

At the time

The South African government blamed agitators; workers described an unjust system.1

Debates since

  • Labour or nation? The strike was about working conditions, but also promoted independence under SWAPO.1
  • Violence on both sides: some protesters attacked officials, headmen and informers; police killed strikers and others in Ovamboland.1

07

How is it remembered?

  • The strike is remembered in Namibia as a turning point in the liberation struggle.
08

Why does it matter?

The 1971–72 strike was the largest in Namibia's history, and a key moment in the struggle against apartheid there.

What other events are like this?

  • 1973The Durban Strikes of 1973 — Under apartheid it was illegal for Black workers to strike. In 1973, around 60,000 of them did so anyway, at more than 100 firms in Durban — reviving the Black trade union movement that would help bring down apartheid.
  • 1935The Copperbelt Strike of 1935 — When the colonial government raised the tax on African mineworkers, they walked out at three of the Copperbelt's great mines. Police shot six people dead — but the strike is remembered as the birth of Zambian nationalism.
  • 1904The Herero and Nama Uprisings and Genocide — The Herero and Nama rose against German settlers who had taken their land and cattle. Germany's response was the first genocide of the twentieth century.
