The Eritrean People's Liberation Front

For almost twenty years, fighters in the mountains of northern Eritrea — about a third of them women — held out against one of Africa's largest armies. They won independence in 1991. The state they built became one of the world's most repressive.

When

Where

01

What is the background to this event?

Eritrea was an Italian colony until 1941, then under British administration. In 1952, the UN federated it with Ethiopia; in 1962, Emperor Haile Selassie annexed it.

  • The Eritrean Liberation Front (ELF) began an armed struggle in 1961.
  • Young, mostly left-wing dissidents broke away from the ELF, which was divided by feuds; some who stayed were killed.1
  • In 1973, they formed the Eritrean People's Liberation Front (EPLF).1

03

What happened?

The EPLF built its base in the Sahel mountains of northern Eritrea.1

  • By 1977, it had driven the Ethiopian army out of much of Eritrea.1
  • After the Soviet Union began supporting Ethiopia's Derg regime, Ethiopian forces pushed the EPLF back to its mountain base around Nakfa.1
  • The EPLF defeated repeated major offensives, including the Red Star Campaign of 1982.1
  • In its liberated areas, it ran schools, clinics, workshops and land reform, and encouraged women to fight and take leadership roles.
  • In 1988 it won a major victory at Afabet, and in 1990 took the port of Massawa.1
  • In May 1991, with the Tigray People's Liberation Front, it helped bring down the Derg.1

04

What happened next?

  • Independence: in a UN-monitored referendum in 1993, Eritreans voted overwhelmingly for independence.1
  • One-party rule: the EPLF became the People's Front for Democracy and Justice, the only legal party.1
  • Repression: in 2001, leading EPLF veterans who called for reform, and independent journalists, were arrested and have not been seen since. Indefinite national service has driven hundreds of thousands to flee.
05

What do others say?

At the time

Ethiopian governments saw Eritrean independence as secession and fought to keep the territory. The EPLF saw it as an anti-colonial struggle.1

Debates since

  • Violence: the war killed tens of thousands of fighters and civilians on all sides; Ethiopian forces bombed villages, and the EPLF also fought a civil war with the ELF in 1980–81.1
  • Women's liberation: historians debate how far gains for women survived after the war.
  • Legacy: some former fighters now say the movement's discipline and secrecy paved the way for dictatorship.

07

How is it remembered?

  • The trenches at Nakfa are a national monument, and Eritrea's currency is named after the town.
08

Why does it matter?

The EPLF's war is one of the most remarkable stories of resistance in modern Africa — and a warning about what can happen to liberation movements in power.

  • It gave women an unusually large role.
  • Its later history raises hard questions about armed struggle and democracy.

What other events are like this?

  • 1960The Mueda Massacre — Makonde farmers gathered peacefully to ask the Portuguese for better prices and independence. Soldiers opened fire. The massacre convinced many that only armed struggle could end colonial rule.
  • 1965The Dhofar Revolution — In one of the most isolated places on earth, mountain tribes rose against a sultan who banned radios, bicycles and girls' schools. The revolution opened schools for girls — and used terror against its opponents.
  • 1974Ethiopia 1974: 'Land to the Tiller' — Students demanded land for the peasants; taxi drivers, teachers, workers, Muslims and soldiers joined them. The emperor fell — but the revolution was seized by a military junta that turned its terror on the very students who had begun it.
