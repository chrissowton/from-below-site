The Algerian War of Independence

After 124 years of French rule, the National Liberation Front launched a war for independence. France answered with torture, camps and the destruction of thousands of villages; the FLN also killed civilians. In 1962, Algeria became independent — at the cost of hundreds of thousands of lives.

01

What is the background to this event?

France began its conquest of Algeria in 1830, and ruled it as part of France, with a large European settler population.1

  • Algerian nationalism grew in the 20th century: Messali Hadj and others founded the North African Star in 1926 and the Algerian People's Party in 1937.1
  • On 8 May 1945, French forces and settler militias killed thousands of Algerians around Sétif and Guelma after a nationalist demonstration and riots.2

03

What happened?

On 1 November 1954, FLN militants carried out 70 coordinated attacks across Algeria, beginning the war.3

  • Escalation: in August 1955, FLN-led attacks around Philippeville killed European and Algerian civilians; French reprisals killed far more Algerians.1
  • The Battle of Algiers: in 1957, French paratroopers broke the FLN in Algiers using torture, forced disappearances and illegal executions.1
  • The countryside: the French army destroyed over 8,000 villages and moved more than 2 million Algerians into camps.1
  • Towards independence: in September 1959, de Gaulle spoke of 'self-determination'. Settler and army opponents formed the OAS, which waged a campaign of bombings and murders. The Évian Accords of March 1962 agreed a ceasefire, and Algeria became independent on 5 July 1962.1

04

What happened next?

  • The cost: the war caused the deaths of between 400,000 and 1.5 million Algerians, 25,600 French soldiers and 6,000 Europeans.1
  • Exodus: about 900,000 European Algerians fled to France within a few months of independence.1
  • A new state: the FLN ruled independent Algeria as a single party.
05

What do others say?

At the time

The French government presented the war as a police operation to keep order in part of France; the FLN presented it as a war of national liberation.1

Debates since

  • The death toll: estimates range from 400,000 to 1.5 million Algerian deaths.1
  • Torture: in 2001, General Paul Aussaresses confirmed that torture had been used on a large scale.4
  • Memory: France and Algeria still remember the war very differently.
06

The evidence

  • Military and police archives, testimony from soldiers and victims, Henri Alleg's account of his torture (La Question, 1958), photographs and the press.4

07

How is it remembered?

  • 1 November and 5 July are national days in Algeria.
  • In 2024, President Macron acknowledged that Larbi Ben M'hidi was 'assassinated by French soldiers'.5
08

Why does it matter?

Algeria's war was one of the bloodiest wars of decolonisation, and it inspired anti-colonial movements around the world. It also forced France to confront the use of torture by its own army — a debate that continues.1

Questions to think about

  1. Why did Algerian nationalists turn to armed struggle in 1954?
  2. Why did the French army use torture, and why was it denied for so long?
  3. How should we judge the FLN's attacks on civilians?
  4. Why was the war so divisive in France itself?
09

What other events are like this?

  • 1945The Sétif and Guelma Massacres — On the day the Second World War ended in Europe, Algerians marched for independence in Sétif. The colonial response killed thousands and set the course towards war.
  • 1952The Mau Mau Uprising — An armed rebellion over land and freedom was met with mass detention and torture. Decades later, the British government paid compensation to survivors.
  • 1947The Malagasy Uprising of 1947 — Armed mostly with spears, Malagasy nationalists rose against French colonial rule. The repression killed tens of thousands — and France kept silent about it for more than half a century.
