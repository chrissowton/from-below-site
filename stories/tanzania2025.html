Tanzania's Election Protests

With the main opposition parties barred and their leader in prison, protests broke out on election day. Under an internet blackout and curfew, security forces opened fire. A government commission later counted 518 dead; the opposition says the true toll is far higher.

When
29 October – November 2025
Where
Dar es Salaam, Arusha, Mwanza and towns across Tanzania

Ongoing situationLast reviewed 17 November 2026

The government commission's report of April 2026 counted 518 deaths but did not say who planned the violence it described; the opposition rejected it as a whitewash and says the true number is far higher.1 We have not found reports that anyone has been convicted for the killings.

Still unresolved:

  • Whether an independent investigation takes place, as the UN has called for
  • What happens to the people charged with treason, and to Tundu Lissu
  • Whether the true number of dead is ever established

This is recent history. Accounts, casualty figures and outcomes may change as more evidence emerges.

01

What is the background to this event?

Tanzania had held multi-party elections since 1995, and the main opposition parties had taken part in every one.2

  • On 9 April 2025, Tundu Lissu, chairman of the main opposition party Chadema, was arrested on charges of incitement and treason after calling for electoral reform at a rally.2
  • Days later, Chadema was disqualified from the election. In September, the candidate of the second opposition party, ACT-Wazalendo, was also barred.2
  • Human rights organisations and foreign observers had raised concerns about shrinking civic space and media restrictions.1

In their own words

03

What happened?

On election day, 29 October 2025, protests broke out in Dar es Salaam, with demonstrators burning tyres and blocking roads and denouncing a "sham election". A curfew was imposed and an internet blackout was reported.1

  • Protests spread to Arusha, Mwanza and other cities. Security forces used tear gas and live ammunition; the army set up roadblocks across the country.1
  • On 1 November, President Samia Suluhu Hassan was declared the winner with 98% of the vote. She was sworn in on 3 November at a military parade ground.1
  • On 7 November, prosecutors charged at least 240 people with treason and criminal conspiracy.1
  • A CNN investigation found that security forces shot at protesters, many of them unarmed or carrying only stones and sticks, and found indications that victims had been buried in mass graves. Amnesty International also documented widespread use of lethal force.1

The death toll is fiercely disputed. On 31 October, the UN human rights office reported at least 10 confirmed deaths. Chadema said it had documented 2,000 deaths. In April 2026, a government-appointed commission reported that 518 people had been killed; the opposition called the report a whitewash.1

04

What happened next?

  • International concern: the UN human rights office and the African Commission on Human and Peoples' Rights expressed alarm. The Catholic archbishop of Dar es Salaam said, "The punishment for protests is not to shoot and kill."1
05

What do others say?

At the time

Chadema called the results "completely fabricated"; the government rejected the claims, and the police chief blamed illegal immigrants for inciting the protests.1

Debates since

  • How many died? Figures range from the UN's early count of at least 10 confirmed deaths to the government commission's 518 and Chadema's 2,000 or more.1
  • Who was responsible? The commission said the protests were planned and organised, without saying by whom; investigations by CNN and Amnesty International point to lethal force by the security forces.1
06

The evidence

  • Videos smuggled out despite the internet blackout, a CNN investigation using video, sound analysis and satellite images, reports by Amnesty International and the UN, and the government commission's report.1
07

How is it remembered?

  • This is recent history. How the events of October 2025 are remembered in Tanzania is still being decided.
08

Why does it matter?

The 2025 election was by far the deadliest in Tanzania's history.2 It broke with three decades in which the major opposition parties had taken part in every election.2
