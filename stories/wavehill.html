The Wave Hill Walk-Off

Gurindji stockmen and their families walked off a British-owned cattle station. What began as a strike over pay became the foundation of the Aboriginal land rights movement.

When
23 August 1966 – 16 August 1975
Where
Wave Hill station, Northern Territory, Australia
Facts checked

01

What is the background to this event?

The cattle industry of northern Australia depended heavily on the labour of Aboriginal stockmen and domestic workers, who worked on stations established on their own traditional country. Wave Hill station, covering a vast area of Gurindji land, was granted to a pastoralist in 1883 and bought in 1914 by the British meat company owned by the Vestey family.

Aboriginal workers on the station were paid far less than white workers, often partly or wholly in rations, and lived in very poor conditions:

  • A Northern Territory government inquiry in the 1930s found that the company had been "quite ruthless in denying their Aboriginal labour proper access to basic human rights".
  • A 1945 inquiry found that Vesteys was not even paying the minimum wage of five shillings a day then set for Aboriginal workers.
  • Regulations of 1959 set wages for Aboriginal "wards" at rates up to 50 per cent lower than those for non-Aboriginal workers in similar jobs.

In March 1966 the Commonwealth Conciliation and Arbitration Commission agreed that Aboriginal pastoral workers should receive equal wages, but, in response to the pastoralists' concerns, delayed implementation for three years. For the Gurindji, the wait was too long.

Who were the key people?

  • Vincent Lingiari

    A Gurindji elder and head stockman who led the walk-off and became the public voice of the Gurindji claim to their land.

  • Mick Rangiari

    A Gurindji leader who travelled to the cities of the south-east to build support for the claim.

  • Dexter Daniels

    An Aboriginal organiser with the North Australian Workers' Union who helped to initiate the strike.

  • Frank Hardy

    A writer who supported the Gurindji and publicised their struggle in his book The Unlucky Australians (1968).

  • Billy Bunter Jampijinpa

    A sixteen-year-old stockman at the time of the walk-off, whose recollections are among the oral histories of the strike.

  • Gough Whitlam

    Prime Minister of Australia, who handed back part of the land in 1975.

  • The Gurindji families

    Some 200 stockmen, house servants and their families who chose years of hardship on their own country over a return to the old terms.

In their own words

03

What happened?

On 23 August 1966, led by Vincent Lingiari, about 200 Gurindji stockmen, domestic workers and their families walked off Wave Hill station.2 They first camped in the dry bed of the Victoria River, near the welfare settlement at Wave Hill.

It quickly became clear that the dispute was about more than wages. Lingiari told Frank Hardy:

"This bin Gurindji country long time before them Vestey mob."Vincent Lingiari to Frank Hardy, 19662

The company offered improved wages and conditions if the workers returned. They refused. In March 1967 the Gurindji moved to Wattie Creek, known to them as Daguragu, a place of deep cultural significance, and began to build a settlement there.2 At the same time they drafted a petition to the Governor-General, Lord Casey, asking for a lease of about 1,300 square kilometres around Daguragu, to be run cooperatively by the Gurindji. It stated:

"We feel that morally the land is ours and should be returned to us."Gurindji petition to the Governor-General, 19672

The petition was rejected, but the Gurindji stayed. Over the following years:

  • Unions, students, churches and Aboriginal advancement organisations across Australia raised funds and campaigned in their support.
  • Gurindji leaders travelled to Sydney, Melbourne and other cities to speak at public meetings.
  • The strike lasted for more than seven years, in difficult conditions and with little income.2

On 16 August 1975, after an agreement was brokered with the Vestey company, Prime Minister Gough Whitlam travelled to Daguragu. In a ceremony captured in Mervyn Bishop's famous photograph, he poured a handful of soil into Vincent Lingiari's hand and handed over a lease to part of the Gurindji's land.23

04

What happened next?

  • Land rights legislation: the Aboriginal Land Rights (Northern Territory) Act 1976, passed by the Fraser government on the basis of work begun under Whitlam, allowed Aboriginal people in the Northern Territory to claim land on the basis of traditional occupation. The Gurindji received inalienable freehold title to their land in 1986.
  • Unfinished business: Lingiari's vision of a separate but equal settlement with land, cultural and political autonomy was only partly realised, and the community continued to face economic hardship.
  • Native title: in 2020 the Federal Court recognised native title over the area, acknowledging Indigenous connection to the land "at least since European settlement and probably for millennia".
  • Memory: in 1991 Paul Kelly and Kev Carmody told the story in the song "From Little Things Big Things Grow", which was added to the National Film and Sound Archive's Sounds of Australia registry in 2010.
05

Why does it matter?

The Wave Hill walk-off began as an industrial dispute and became one of the founding events of the Aboriginal land rights movement. It showed how a struggle over wages and conditions could grow into a claim about justice, sovereignty and belonging, and it brought the question of land to the centre of Australian politics.

  • It led directly to the first Australian legislation recognising Aboriginal land rights.
  • It was led by Aboriginal workers themselves, supported by — but not directed from — the labour movement.
  • It remains a powerful example of the connection between labour rights and Indigenous rights in settler-colonial societies.

What do others say?How it was seen at the time, and where historians disagree

At the time

The Vestey company and many pastoralists argued that Aboriginal workers received housing and rations in addition to wages, and that equal pay would make stations unviable. Some officials and commentators blamed the strike on communist union organisers and on writers such as Frank Hardy.

Historians' debates

  • Wages or land? Historians debate when the land claim became central — whether it was the Gurindji's aim from the start or emerged during the strike.
  • The role of outsiders: some accounts emphasise unions and southern supporters; others, such as Charlie Ward, insist that the Gurindji themselves directed the struggle.
  • The aftermath: the handover is often presented as a triumphant ending, but historians have highlighted the economic difficulties the community faced and the limits of the land eventually returned.
How do we know?The evidence, and a source from the time
  • Government files, inquiry reports and parliamentary debates.
  • Frank Hardy's The Unlucky Australians — a participant's account written to win support.
  • Oral histories recorded with Gurindji participants, including in Gurindji language.
  • Mervyn Bishop's photographs of the 1975 handover.

Mervyn Bishop, Daguragu, 16 August 1975

Prime Minister Gough Whitlam pours soil into the hand of the Gurindji leader Vincent Lingiari, nine years after the walk-off began. The photographer, Mervyn Bishop, was the first Aboriginal person to work on a metropolitan daily newspaper in Australia. His photograph became one of the best-known images in Australian history.

Reading it

Gough Whitlam pours soil into Vincent Lingiari's hand, nine years after the walk-off began. The photographer, Mervyn Bishop, was the first Aboriginal person to work on a metropolitan daily paper in Australia. The image can look like a gift from the government; it was in fact the result of nine years of Gurindji struggle.

How is it remembered?Memorials, anniversaries and arguments over memory
  • The Freedom Day Festival is held at Kalkarindji every August.
  • The federal electorate covering much of the Northern Territory was named Lingiari in 2001.
  • The song "From Little Things Big Things Grow" has become one of Australia's best-known protest songs.
  • The walk-off route has been heritage-listed.
