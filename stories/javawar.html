Diponegoro and the Java War

A Javanese prince with visions of a holy war led peasants and religious teachers against Dutch rule. The war lasted five years, and at least 200,000 Javanese civilians died.

When
1825 – 1830
Where
Yogyakarta, Java, Indonesia
Facts checked
2026-11-16

See it on the map →

01

What is the background to this event?

In the early nineteenth century, the Dutch and, for a time, the British strengthened colonial control over Java at the expense of its princely states.1

  • The British introduced a land tax payable in cash, which caused hardship for peasants.1
  • Prince Diponegoro, eldest son of a sultan of Yogyakarta, had been passed over for the throne and became estranged from the court and the colonial government.1
  • A millenarian movement was growing, and Diponegoro claimed visions calling him to holy war.1

03

What happened?

In July 1825, tensions over a government road project sparked Diponegoro's rebellion.1

  • The rebels besieged Yogyakarta, and defeated Dutch forces several times in 1826.1
  • Other leaders rose on Java's north coast and in East Java.1
  • The war became a guerrilla war, and Diponegoro's popular support made it hard for the Dutch to win.1
  • From 1827, the Dutch built networks of small forts that gradually hemmed the rebels in.1
  • In 1830, Diponegoro was captured at Magelang while meeting de Kock for negotiations.1

04

What happened next?

  • Exile: Diponegoro was exiled to Manado and then Makassar, where he died in 1855.1
  • Colonial control: the princely states lost most of their power, and the Dutch ruled Java unchallenged.1
  • The Cultivation System: the Dutch then forced Javanese peasants to grow export crops, earning huge revenues.1
05

What do others say?

At the time

The Dutch saw a rebellious prince; Diponegoro saw himself as a just ruler fighting for Islam and Java.2

Debates since

  • Anti-colonial hero? Indonesia honours him as a national hero; historians such as Peter Carey stress the war's roots in Javanese religion and court politics.2
  • Violence on all sides: about 15,000 Dutch troops and 20,000 rebels died; at least 200,000 Javanese civilians died from violence, disease and famine.1
06

The evidence

  • Diponegoro's autobiography (Babad Diponegoro), written in exile.2
  • Dutch military records.

Primary source: Painting

What is it?
Painting
Made by, and when
Nicolaas Pieneman, about 1835

A Dutch painting of Prince Diponegoro's capture in 1830, showing it as a calm 'submission'. In fact Diponegoro had come to negotiate with General de Kock and was arrested. In 1857 the Javanese painter Raden Saleh painted his own version of the scene, which presents it very differently.

Questions to ask of this source

  1. How does Pieneman show the Dutch and the Javanese?
  2. Why call the event a 'submission' rather than an arrest?
  3. Why might Raden Saleh have painted his own version?
07

How is it remembered?

  • Raden Saleh's painting The Arrest of Pangeran Diponegoro (1857) presents the prince with dignity, answering Dutch versions.
  • The Babad Diponegoro is listed in UNESCO's Memory of the World Register.
08

Why does it matter?

The Java War was the last major armed resistance to Dutch rule in Java until the Indonesian revolution a century later.1

  • It was led by a prince, but fought and suffered by peasants.

Questions to think about

  1. Why did peasants follow a prince into war?
  2. How did religion shape the rebellion?
  3. Was Diponegoro's capture during negotiations a betrayal?
  4. Why was the death toll among civilians so high?
