Positive Action and Ghana's Independence

A campaign of strikes and boycotts landed Kwame Nkrumah in prison — from where he won an election and led the first sub-Saharan African colony to independence.

When
January 1950 – 6 March 1957
Where
Accra, Gold Coast (now Ghana)
Facts checked
2026-11-15

01

What is the background to this event?

The Gold Coast was a British colony in West Africa, rich in cocoa, gold and timber. After the Second World War, demands for self-government grew rapidly:

  • Thousands of soldiers returned from fighting for Britain in Burma and elsewhere to find poor prospects and unpaid pensions.
  • Post-war inflation and high import prices caused widespread anger; in January 1948 a boycott of foreign-owned shops began.
  • On 28 February 1948 police fired on a march of ex-servicemen in Accra, killing three. Riots followed across the country.

Kwame Nkrumah had returned in 1947 after twelve years studying and organising in the United States and Britain, to become secretary of the United Gold Coast Convention, a party led by lawyers and businessmen. Impatient with its cautious approach, he founded the Convention People's Party (CPP) in June 1949, with the slogan "Self-Government Now". It quickly attracted support from young people, market women, workers and ex-servicemen.

In their own words

03

What happened?

In late 1949 the colonial government published proposals for constitutional reform that fell well short of the CPP's demand for immediate self-government. On 8 January 1950 Nkrumah declared "Positive Action": a campaign of non-violent strikes, boycotts and non-cooperation with the colonial authorities, modelled partly on Gandhi's methods in India.12

  • The Gold Coast Trades Union Congress called a general strike, and shops, offices and transport stopped across the colony.
  • The Governor declared a state of emergency, imposed a curfew and banned meetings.

Nkrumah and other CPP leaders were arrested later in January 1950. Nkrumah was convicted of inciting an illegal strike and sedition and sentenced to a total of three years in prison.2 With its leader in James Fort prison, the party continued to organise under Komla Gbedemah.

In the general election of February 1951 — the first in the colony held under a new constitution — the CPP won a decisive majority of the elected seats. Nkrumah, standing from prison, won the Accra Central seat by a huge margin.2 Governor Arden-Clarke released him on 12 February 1951 and invited him to form a government as "Leader of Government Business".1

04

What happened next?

  • Self-government: Nkrumah became Prime Minister in 1952. A new constitution in 1954 created an all-African cabinet responsible for internal affairs.
  • Independence: on 6 March 1957 the Gold Coast became independent as Ghana — the first colony in sub-Saharan Africa to win independence from European rule. At midnight Nkrumah declared: "At long last, the battle has ended! And thus Ghana, your beloved country, is free forever!"
  • Pan-Africanism: Ghana became a centre for independence movements across Africa, and Nkrumah a leading advocate of African unity.
  • Authoritarianism: Nkrumah's government became increasingly authoritarian, introducing detention without trial in 1958 and a one-party state in 1964. He was overthrown by a military coup in 1966.
05

What do others say?

At the time

The colonial government saw Positive Action as an illegal attempt to bring down lawful authority and treated Nkrumah as a dangerous agitator, possibly influenced by communism. The older nationalist leaders of the United Gold Coast Convention regarded him as reckless.

Historians' debates

  • Who won independence? Some historians stress the CPP's mass mobilisation; others emphasise Britain's own decision to manage a gradual transfer of power.
  • Nkrumah's legacy: he is celebrated as a liberator and Pan-African visionary, and criticised for authoritarian rule and economic mismanagement.
  • Regional divisions: historians such as Jean Allman have shown that the National Liberation Movement in Asante opposed the CPP's vision of a centralised state, and that independence was more contested than national histories suggest.
06

The evidence

  • Nkrumah's own autobiography, published in the year of independence — an important source, but also a political document.
  • The report of the Watson Commission into the 1948 disturbances, and colonial government records.
  • CPP newspapers, such as the Accra Evening News.
  • Oral histories of party activists, including market women.
07

How is it remembered?

  • 6 March is Independence Day in Ghana, and the Independence Arch and Black Star Square in Accra commemorate it.
  • The Kwame Nkrumah Memorial Park in Accra contains his mausoleum.
  • 21 September, Nkrumah's birthday, has been a public holiday in Ghana, sometimes renamed Founders' Day — reflecting debates about whether he alone should be honoured.
08

Why does it matter?

Ghana's path to independence showed that mass political organisation — strikes, boycotts and elections — could force a colonial power to hand over power, and it inspired independence movements across Africa and the Caribbean. The CPP was built from below by market women, workers, young people and veterans, not only by an educated elite.

  • Ghana's independence in 1957 was the beginning of the wave of African decolonisation that followed in the 1960s.
  • Positive Action shows how ideas of non-violent resistance travelled from India to Africa.
  • Ghana's later history raises difficult questions about what happens to liberation movements once they are in power.

What other events are like this?

  • 1930The Salt March — A 24-day march to the sea to make salt in defiance of British law set off a campaign of civil disobedience across India.
  • 1952The Mau Mau Uprising — An armed rebellion over land and freedom was met with mass detention and torture. Decades later, the British government paid compensation to survivors.
  • 1929The Women's War (Ogu Umunwanyi) — Tens of thousands of Igbo and Ibibio women challenged British colonial rule, using their own traditions of collective protest.
