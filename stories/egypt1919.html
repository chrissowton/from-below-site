Egypt's 1919 Revolution

When Britain exiled the nationalist leader Saad Zaghlul, Egyptians of every class and religion rose up in strikes and demonstrations — and women marched in public for the first time.

When
March – April 1919
Where
Cairo and across Egypt

01

What is the background to this event?

Britain had occupied Egypt since 1882. Formally, Egypt remained part of the Ottoman Empire, ruled by a khedive, but real power lay with the British. When the Ottoman Empire entered the First World War on Germany's side in 1914, Britain declared Egypt a protectorate.

The war years brought great hardship:

  • Hundreds of thousands of Egyptian peasants were recruited — often forcibly — into the Egyptian Labour Corps to work for the British army.
  • Crops and animals were requisitioned, and prices rose sharply.
  • Martial law was imposed and political activity restricted.

At the end of the war, hopes were raised by US President Woodrow Wilson's call for national self-determination. Two days after the armistice of 11 November 1918, a delegation (wafd) of Egyptian politicians led by Saad Zaghlul asked the British High Commissioner for permission to go to London and the Paris Peace Conference to argue for independence. They were refused. The Wafd then organised a nationwide campaign, collecting signatures from Egyptians authorising it to speak for them.1

02

Who were the key people?

  • Saad Zaghlul

    A lawyer, former minister and leader of the Wafd. His arrest and exile sparked the revolution. He later became Prime Minister (1924).

  • Huda Sha'arawi

    A leading figure among the upper-class women who organised demonstrations in 1919. She later founded the Egyptian Feminist Union (1923).

  • Safiya Zaghlul

    Saad Zaghlul's wife, known as 'Mother of the Egyptians', who played a leading role in the women's movement and the nationalist cause.

  • General Edmund Allenby

    The British general sent to restore order, who became High Commissioner and later favoured negotiation.

  • The students, workers and fellahin

    Students of Al-Azhar and the secular schools, transport and other workers, and peasants (fellahin) across the countryside.

03

What happened?

On 8 March 1919, the British arrested Saad Zaghlul and three other Wafd leaders and deported them to Malta. The next day, students in Cairo began demonstrating, and within days the protests had spread across the country.1

For several weeks, demonstrations and strikes by students, civil servants, merchants, peasants, workers and religious leaders brought normal life to a halt:12

  • Lawyers, transport workers and government employees went on strike; railway and telegraph lines were cut in the countryside.
  • The movement brought together Muslims and Christians, who marched under flags combining the crescent and the cross. Coptic priests spoke at Al-Azhar mosque, and Muslim clerics spoke in churches.
  • On 16 March 1919, hundreds of women — many from upper-class families who usually lived in seclusion — marched through Cairo in protest. Their participation was reported around the world.
  • In some places, local committees took over; in the town of Mit Ghamr, nationalists declared an independent republic.

The British response was harsh. Troops fired on demonstrators and villages were burned. On 18 March, at Deir Mawas in Upper Egypt, revolutionaries attacked a train carrying British soldiers, killing eight people including three British officers.1 In the disturbances between 15 and 31 March, at least 800 Egyptians were killed; by July, the figure was 800 killed and 1,600 wounded.1

The British government sent General Edmund Allenby to restore order. Allenby concluded that force alone would not work, and on 7 April 1919 Zaghlul and his colleagues were released and allowed to travel to the Paris Peace Conference.1

04

What happened next?

  • The Milner Mission: in December 1919 Britain sent a commission of inquiry, led by Lord Milner. The Wafd organised a boycott, and the mission concluded in 1921 that the protectorate should be abandoned.
  • Independence — with conditions: on 28 February 1922 Britain unilaterally declared Egypt independent, but kept control of defence, the Suez Canal, the protection of foreign interests and Sudan.
  • A constitution: a constitution was introduced in 1923, and in the 1924 elections the Wafd won a landslide; Zaghlul became Prime Minister.
  • Women's rights: in 1923 Huda Sha'arawi founded the Egyptian Feminist Union; returning from a conference in Rome, she publicly removed her face veil at Cairo railway station.
  • Full independence: British troops remained in Egypt until 1956.
05

What do others say?

At the time

British officials blamed the unrest on agitators, Bolshevik influence or religious fanaticism, and underestimated the depth of popular feeling. Egyptian nationalists presented the revolution as the united uprising of a whole nation.

Historians' debates

  • Whose revolution? Earlier histories focused on Zaghlul and the elite politicians of the Wafd. Later historians, such as Ellis Goldberg, emphasise the role of peasants and workers, whose grievances about wartime requisitions and forced labour went far beyond the Wafd's programme.
  • Success or failure? Some historians see the 1922 independence as a real victory; others argue that it was hollow, since Britain kept control of the most important matters.
  • Women's role: historians such as Beth Baron and Margot Badran have recovered the role of women, while noting that the nationalist movement did not grant women the vote in the 1923 constitution.

07

How is it remembered?

  • Mahmoud Mokhtar's sculpture Egypt's Renaissance (Nahdat Misr), unveiled in 1928 near Cairo University, celebrates the national awakening.
  • The revolution is the setting of Naguib Mahfouz's novel Palace Walk (1956), the first volume of his Cairo Trilogy.
  • Its centenary in 2019 was marked by exhibitions and debates in Egypt.
08

Why does it matter?

The 1919 Revolution was one of the first great mass movements against colonial rule after the First World War, at the same moment as the March First Movement in Korea and protests in India and China. It united Egyptians across lines of class, religion and gender, and forced Britain to concede formal independence.

  • It created modern Egyptian nationalism as a mass movement and the Wafd as its party.
  • It marked the beginning of organised women's political activity in Egypt.
  • Its image of Muslim–Christian unity is still invoked in Egypt today, for example during the 2011 revolution.
