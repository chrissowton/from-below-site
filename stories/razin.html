Stenka Razin's Rising

A Cossack raider gathered runaway serfs and the poor of the Volga into an army against the nobles and officials of the tsar. He took city after city before being defeated, betrayed and executed in Red Square.

When
1670 – 1671
Where
Astrakhan and the Volga, Russia

See it on the map →

01

What is the background to this event?

In the mid-seventeenth century, Russia's tsars were strengthening their power, and the gap between peasants and nobles was growing.1

  • The Law Code of 1649 made serfdom part of the law.1
  • Wars with Poland and Sweden brought higher taxes and conscription.1
  • Many peasants fled south to the Don Cossacks, an independent frontier community.1
  • From 1667, Stepan (Stenka) Razin led Cossack raids on the Volga and the Caspian Sea, and his reputation grew.1
02

Who were the key people?

  • Stepan (Stenka) Razin

    Don Cossack leader of the rising; executed in 1671.

  • Frol Razin

    His brother, captured with him.

  • Runaway serfs, the urban poor and non-Russian peoples

    Who joined his army.

  • Prince Yuri Baryatinsky

    The tsar's commander who defeated him at Simbirsk.

03

What happened?

In 1670, Razin turned his raids into a rising against the nobility and the tsar's officials.1

  • He took Astrakhan, Saratov and Samara as he moved up the Volga.1
  • He aimed to set up a Cossack republic along the Volga before advancing on Moscow.1
  • He claimed to fight the "traitor-boyars", not the tsar himself.1
  • At Simbirsk, in October 1670, he was defeated by Baryatinsky's army and fled.1
  • His proclamations spread revolt further, as far as the regions around Nizhny Novgorod, Tambov and Penza.1
  • In 1671, Cossack elders handed him over. On 16 June he was quartered in Red Square.1

04

What happened next?

  • Repression: many of his followers were killed after Simbirsk.1
  • Pugachev: a century later, another Cossack, Yemelyan Pugachev, led an even larger rising.
05

What do others say?

At the time

The state and the Church condemned him; to many of the poor he was a liberator.

Debates since

  • Bandit or rebel? Paul Avrich described the rising as a "curious mixture of brigandage and revolt".12
  • Violence on both sides: his raids devastated towns and his forces killed nobles and officials; the tsar's armies in turn slaughtered his followers.1
06

The evidence

  • Razin's proclamations and government records.
  • Accounts by foreign visitors to Russia.2
07

How is it remembered?

  • Razin is the hero of the folk song "Volga, Volga, Mother Volga", and streets and a bridge in St Petersburg bear his name.
08

Why does it matter?

Razin's rising was one of the great peasant and Cossack revolts of Russian history.

  • It shows how frontier freedom attracted those fleeing serfdom.

Questions to think about

  1. Why did serfs flee to the Cossacks?
  2. Why did Razin claim to fight the boyars rather than the tsar?
  3. Was Razin a rebel, a bandit or both?
  4. Why did Razin become a hero of Russian folk songs?
09

What other events are like this?

  • 1773Pugachev's Rebellion — A Cossack deserter claiming to be the murdered Tsar Peter III proclaimed the end of serfdom. Cossacks, serfs, factory workers and Bashkirs rose in the largest peasant war in Russian history.
  • 1573The Croatian–Slovene Peasant Revolt — Serfs in Croatia and Slovenia rose against a cruel baron and planned a world without nobles, with peasant officials answerable only to the emperor. They were crushed in twelve days.
  • 1525The German Peasants' War and the Twelve Articles — Tens of thousands of peasants rose against their lords, armed with the new ideas of the Reformation and a printed manifesto of rights. It was Europe's largest popular uprising before the French Revolution.
