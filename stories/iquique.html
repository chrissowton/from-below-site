The Santa María School Massacre

Thousands of striking nitrate miners and their families were shot down in and around a school in Iquique — one of the worst massacres of workers in Latin American history.

When
21 December 1907
Where
Iquique, Chile
Facts checked

01

What is the background to this event?

After its victory in the War of the Pacific (1879–1884), Chile took control of the nitrate-rich deserts of Tarapacá and Antofagasta from Peru and Bolivia. Nitrate, used for fertilisers and explosives, became Chile's main export, and most of the mines (oficinas) were owned by British and other foreign companies.

Some 40,000 workers laboured in the nitrate fields, including around 13,000 from Bolivia and Peru.1 Their conditions were extremely harsh:

  • Many were paid not in money but in tokens (fichas) that could only be spent in the company's own shops, where prices were high.1
  • Accidents were frequent, and the open vats used to process the nitrate caused many injuries and deaths.
  • Petitions sent to the government by workers' delegations in 1901, 1903 and 1904 had produced no result.1

The governments of the time were reluctant to intervene in disputes between employers and workers, and tended to see large-scale workers' movements as a threat to public order.

In their own words

03

What happened?

In December 1907 a strike spread across the nitrate fields of Tarapacá. Thousands of workers and their families came down from the desert to the port of Iquique to press their demands on the authorities, and on 16 December workers from other industries in the city joined them.1 The strikers' demands, published on 16 December, included:1

  • the abolition of tokens and payment in legal currency, at a fixed exchange rate;
  • freedom of trade at the nitrate works, so that workers could buy from other shops;
  • iron grates over the processing vats to prevent fatal accidents;
  • schools for workers, and no dismissals without notice.

The strikers gathered at the Domingo Santa María School and in the Plaza Manuel Montt, carrying the flags of Chile, Peru, Bolivia and Argentina, and asked the government to mediate with the nitrate companies. By 21 December their number has been estimated at ten to twelve thousand.1

President Pedro Montt sent General Roberto Silva Renard to deal with the situation. Under confidential orders from the minister of the interior, he was to use all necessary means to force the miners to return to work.1 On 20 December, troops fired on a group of workers trying to leave the city near the Buenaventura nitrate works, killing several.1

On the afternoon of 21 December, Silva Renard told the strike leaders that they had one hour to disperse or be fired upon. When they refused, soldiers opened fire on the crowd with rifles and machine guns. Troops then stormed the school grounds, firing into the playgrounds and classrooms, where women and children were sheltering.1 Survivors were driven at sabre-point to the city's racecourse (the Club Hípico) and then sent back to the nitrate works.1

The number of victims has never been established. The government ordered that no death certificates be issued. General Silva Renard's official report spoke at first of 140 dead, later raised to 195. Most historians estimate that the number killed was far higher; it is often estimated at over 2,000, with the highest estimate of 3,600 considered speculative.13

04

What happened next?

  • Silence: the government blamed the strikers, and for decades official knowledge of the event was suppressed. The victims were buried in a mass grave; their remains were not exhumed until 1940.
  • Revenge: in 1914 Silva Renard was badly wounded in an assassination attempt by the Spanish anarchist Antonio Ramón, whose brother had been killed at Iquique. Silva Renard died some years later.
  • The labour movement: despite the repression, workers' organisations in the north continued to grow. The printer Luis Emilio Recabarren, who organised among the nitrate workers, founded the Socialist Workers' Party in 1912, a forerunner of the Chilean Communist Party.
  • The end of nitrate: the invention of synthetic nitrate during the First World War led to the collapse of the industry, and most of the oficinas became ghost towns.
05

What do others say?

At the time

The government and much of the press presented the strikers as a dangerous mob threatening public order, and claimed that the army had acted in self-defence. Silva Renard's report minimised his own responsibility and blamed the strike leaders. Workers' newspapers and survivors described a deliberate massacre.

Historians' debates

  • How many died? Because death certificates were not issued, estimates range from 140 or 195 (the official figures) to over 2,000 or more. Historians agree that the official figures were far too low.
  • Who was responsible? Historians debate how far the decision to open fire was taken by Silva Renard on the spot, and how far it was the result of orders from Santiago.
  • The "social question": historians see the massacre as part of a wider pattern of violent responses to strikes in Chile in this period, including in Valparaíso (1903) and Santiago (1905).

The evidence

  • Silva Renard's official report and government correspondence.
  • Newspaper reports from Iquique and Santiago.
  • Photographs of the strike and the troops, now held by the Biblioteca del Congreso Nacional and other archives.
  • Testimony of survivors and witnesses, collected by workers' newspapers and later historians.

07

How is it remembered?

  • The Cantata Santa María de Iquique (1970), composed by Luis Advis and performed by the group Quilapayún, made the massacre widely known; it was banned after the 1973 military coup.
  • For the centenary in 2007, President Michelle Bachelet declared a national day of mourning on 21 December, and a mausoleum was inaugurated in Iquique's cemetery.
  • Hernán Rivera Letelier's novel Santa María de las flores negras (2002) tells the story from the workers' perspective.
08

Why does it matter?

The massacre at the Santa María School was one of the bloodiest attacks on workers in the history of Latin America. It showed how far a government was prepared to go to protect the interests of foreign companies and the existing social order.

  • The strikers' demands — payment in money, safe workplaces, education — were basic, and most were later written into law.
  • The flags of four nations carried by the strikers are a reminder of workers' solidarity across borders that had recently been at war.
  • The struggle to remember the massacre has become part of Chile's wider debates about political violence.

What other events are like this?

  • 1819The Peterloo Massacre — Tens of thousands of working people gathered peacefully to demand the vote. Within ten minutes, cavalry had cleared the field with sabres.
  • 1888The Matchwomen's Strike — About 1,400 women and girls walked out of a match factory, won their demands in three weeks, and founded the largest union of women in Britain.
  • 1947The Dakar–Niger Railway Strike — African railway workers in French West Africa held out for more than five months to win equal treatment with their European colleagues.
