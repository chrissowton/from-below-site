Burundi's Protests Against a Third Term

When President Pierre Nkurunziza announced he would run for a third term, young people in Bujumbura's neighbourhoods took to the streets for weeks. The crisis that followed led to hundreds of deaths and drove hundreds of thousands into exile.

When
April 2015 – 2016
Where
Bujumbura, Burundi

See it on the map →

01

What is the background to this event?

Burundi's civil war of 1993–2005 killed an estimated 300,000 people. The peace agreement and the 2005 constitution shared power between Hutu and Tutsi and limited presidents to two terms.1

  • Pierre Nkurunziza, a former Hutu rebel leader, became president in 2005 and was re-elected in 2010.1
  • More than 60 per cent of Burundians did not have enough food.1
  • On 25 April 2015, the ruling party, the CNDD-FDD, announced that Nkurunziza would run again, arguing that his first term did not count because he had been chosen by parliament.1
02

Who were the key people?

  • Young protesters

    From neighbourhoods such as Musaga, Nyakabiga and Cibitoke in Bujumbura.

  • Pierre Claver Mbonimpa

    A leading human rights defender, shot and seriously wounded in August 2015.

  • Pierre Nkurunziza

    President of Burundi, 2005–2020.

  • Godefroid Niyombare

    A general who led a failed coup in May 2015.

  • The Imbonerakure

    The ruling party's youth wing, accused of violence against opponents.

03

What happened?

Protests began in Bujumbura immediately after the announcement and continued for more than three weeks.1

  • The government closed universities, cut internet and phone networks, and called the protesters "terrorists".1
  • The vice-president of the Constitutional Court fled the country, saying judges had been threatened; the court approved the third term.1
  • Several protesters and police officers were killed, and hundreds of people were arrested.1
  • On 13 May 2015, General Niyombare announced a coup while Nkurunziza was abroad. It collapsed within a day, and independent radio stations were attacked and closed.1
  • Nkurunziza was re-elected in July 2015 in an election boycotted by the main opposition.
  • Violence continued: government officials and opponents were assassinated. On 11 December 2015, armed groups attacked military bases, and almost 90 people were killed; residents and human rights groups accused the security forces of killing many people in the neighbourhoods afterwards.12

04

What happened next?

  • Exile: hundreds of thousands of Burundians fled to neighbouring countries.2
  • International justice: the International Criminal Court opened an investigation, and Burundi became the first country to leave the court, in 2017.
  • Constitution: a 2018 referendum extended presidential terms. Nkurunziza did not run in 2020 and died that year.
05

What do others say?

At the time

The government said the third term was legal and blamed the violence on insurgents. The opposition and civil society said it broke the constitution and the peace agreement.1

Debates since

  • Violence on all sides: security forces and the Imbonerakure were accused of killings, torture and disappearances; armed opposition groups also attacked soldiers, police and officials.23
  • Ethnic politics: observers debated whether the crisis risked reviving Hutu–Tutsi conflict, or was mainly political.
  • Death toll: estimates of the number killed since 2015 range from several hundred to more than a thousand.23
06

The evidence

  • Reports of the UN Commission of Inquiry.3
  • Photographs and video by local journalists, many of whom later went into exile.
07

How is it remembered?

  • Exiled Burundian journalists and activists continue to document the events of 2015.
08

Why does it matter?

The Burundi crisis was one of several struggles in Africa over presidential term limits, and shows the danger that such crises can bring back the violence of civil war.

  • It was led largely by young people in poor urban neighbourhoods.
  • It also shows the risks for journalists and human rights defenders.

Questions to think about

  1. Why do term limits matter?
  2. Why did the protests not succeed, unlike in Burkina Faso?
  3. How did the crisis affect journalists and human rights defenders?
  4. How should the international community respond to such crises?

What other events are like this?

  • 2014The Burkinabè Uprising — When Blaise Compaoré tried to change the constitution to stay in power after 27 years, hundreds of thousands took to the streets. Within three days, the parliament was on fire and the president had fled.
  • 2011Y'en a Marre — "Fed Up" — Angry about power cuts, a group of rappers and journalists launched a movement called 'Fed Up'. They registered hundreds of thousands of young voters and helped defeat President Abdoulaye Wade's bid for a third term.
  • 2007Guinea's General Strike of 2007 — When Guinea's ailing president freed two men accused of corruption, the trade unions called a general strike. Weeks of shutdown and protest, met with deadly force and martial law, forced him to share power.
