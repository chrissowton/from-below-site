The Plan of Campaign: Ireland's Rent Strike

When landlords refused to cut rents after bad harvests, tenants on hundreds of Irish estates paid what they thought fair into a common fund instead. The government answered with a Coercion Act, battering rams and prison.

When
1886 – 1891
Where
Portumna, County Galway, and estates across Ireland
Facts checked

01

What is the background to this event?

From the mid-1870s, falling prices for cattle and dairy produce left many Irish tenant farmers in arrears with their rent. Bad weather in 1885 and 1886 caused crops to fail.1

  • Many landlords lived in England and rarely saw their estates. Evictions were increasing.1
  • A Land Commission set up in 1881 could reduce unpayable rents, but many tenants still could not pay.1

In their own words

03

What happened?

On 23 October 1886, the Irish National League's newspaper published the "Plan of Campaign".1

  • On each estate, tenants would offer a reduced rent. If the landlord refused it, they would pay nothing to the landlord, and instead put the money into a fund to support any tenants who were evicted.1
  • It was put into operation on 203 estates. Sixty landlords accepted the reduced rents at once, and others gave in later.1
  • The test case was the estate of the absentee Marquess of Clanricarde at Portumna, County Galway, where 1,900 tenants asked for a 25% cut. He refused, and the rents were paid into the estate fund instead.1
  • In December 1886, the government declared the Plan "an unlawful and criminal conspiracy". Archbishops Walsh and Croke supported the tenants.1

04

What happened next?

  • Coercion: Balfour's Crimes Act of 1887 led to the imprisonment of hundreds of people, including over twenty MPs. Trial by jury was abolished for such cases, and police and soldiers used battering rams to evict tenants.1
  • Mitchelstown: in 1887, police fired on a crowd of 8,000 at Mitchelstown, County Cork, killing three people.1
  • The outcome: by 1893 the Plan was over. It had won settlements on 84 estates, but at a high price; some evicted tenants did not get their farms back until 1907.1
  • Land reform: the Land Purchase Act of 1903 allowed Irish tenant farmers to buy their land with government-backed loans.1
05

What do others say?

At the time

The government called it a criminal conspiracy, and the Vatican condemned it in 1888; supporters, including archbishops, saw it as justice for tenants who could not pay.1

Debates since

  • Success or failure? The organisers claimed victory, but the cost in money, imprisonment and evictions was high.1
  • Violence: the Plan was accompanied by intimidation and boycotting, and the state used armed force; three people were killed at Mitchelstown.1

How is it remembered?

  • Robert French's photographs of evictions on Plan of Campaign estates are held by the National Library of Ireland.
08

Why does it matter?

The Plan of Campaign was a collective rent strike: tenants decided together what was fair and backed each other up. Dramatic scenes of evictions by battering ram were reported around the world and won much sympathy in Britain for the evicted tenants.1

Questions to think about

What other events are like this?

  • 1879The Irish Land War and the First Boycott — Irish tenant farmers organised against evictions and high rents — and gave the world a new word, 'boycott'.
  • 1882The Crofters' War — Highland crofters, cleared from their ancestors' land to make way for sheep and deer, refused rent, occupied land and fought the police. Their land league won them the right not to be evicted.
  • 1907The Tenants' Strike and the March of the Brooms — When rents soared, tenants in Buenos Aires's crowded tenements refused to pay. Women and children marched with brooms to 'sweep away the landlords'.
