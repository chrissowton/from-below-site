The vote & democracy

The Dubai Majlis Movement

After the pearling trade collapsed, leading men of Deira demanded a say in how Dubai was run. For five months in 1938–39 a council of fifteen shared power with the Ruler, starting schools and a municipality — until it was ended by force.

When
June 1938 – 29 March 1939
Where
Deira, Dubai, United Arab Emirates
Facts checked

01

What is the background to this event?

Dubai was a small trading town on the Gulf, ruled by Sheikh Saeed bin Maktoum, under British protection.1

  • The pearling industry, on which the town depended, collapsed in 1929.3
  • Hard times led to growing demands that the Ruler's wealth should be shared more widely.2
  • Discontent was strongest across the creek in Deira, among the Ruler's own cousins and other leading families, the Al Bu Falasah.1

03

What happened?

In 1938, the reformers sent the Ruler a list of demands.1

  • They called for a 'civil list' (a fixed allowance for the Ruler), reforms of education, sanitation and order, an end to corruption, and justice and freedom in trade.1
  • On 18 June they threatened to use force if their demands were not met; the next day they occupied the watchtowers of Deira.1
  • In October, Dubai was split into two armed camps on either side of the creek. The reformers took the Nahar Tower and the Deira Customs House.1
  • After mediation by other Rulers and the British Political Agent, an agreement signed on 20 October set up the Majlis, a council of fifteen leading men of the town.1
  • The Ruler had to consult the Majlis before taking decisions. It set up a municipal council, a council of merchants and a director of education.1

04

What happened next?

  • Breakdown: the Ruler soon stopped attending, and the Majlis sought to limit his income. Order broke down, and the reformers were accused of appointing their own supporters and oppressing merchants.1
  • The end: during the wedding of Rashid bin Saeed in March 1939, the reformers, alarmed by armed Bedouin arriving in Deira, occupied its towers. On 29 March, Rashid's men attacked and took control of Deira.1
  • Afterwards: the Ruler set up two appointed councils, one for tribal leaders and one for merchants. Rashid later carried out many of the reforms the Majlis had asked for.1
05

What do others say?

At the time

The reformers presented themselves as fighting corruption; the Ruler's side saw a challenge from rival relatives.1

Debates since

  • Reform or family feud? The movement included members of the Maktoum family itself, so historians debate whether it was a reform movement or a power struggle.1
  • Violence on both sides: the reformers occupied towers and the customs house by force in 1938. When the Majlis ended in March 1939, ten of its fighters were killed, and around twenty-six people died in all.1 Another source states that many reform leaders were killed after the Majlis was dissolved.2
06

The evidence

  • British government records from the Political Residency in the Gulf, which reported on the events.1
07

How is it remembered?

  • The Majlis of 1938 is little remembered in public in the UAE today, but historians of the Gulf see it as an important early reform movement.
08

Why does it matter?

The Majlis was one of the earliest attempts at a representative council in the Gulf. It was led by a group of leading families rather than ordinary townspeople, but it raised questions — who should share in a ruler's wealth, and who should have a voice — that are still asked across the region.

What other events are like this?

  • 1954Bahrain's National Union Committee — After clashes between Sunni and Shia Bahrainis, reformers from both communities came together to demand an elected assembly, trade unions and an end to British control. Their leaders were exiled to a remote Atlantic island.
  • 2005The Campaign for Women's Votes in Kuwait — For more than forty years, Kuwaiti women campaigned for the vote — through petitions, a strike, mock ballots and demonstrations outside parliament. In May 2005, they won.
  • 1905Iran's Constitutional Revolution — Merchants, clerics, craftsmen and radicals forced the Shah to grant Iran a constitution and an elected parliament — the first in the Middle East.
