The Rice Riots of 1918

Fishermen's wives in a small Japanese port tried to stop rice being shipped away as prices soared. Their protest spread to nearly 500 places and brought down the government.

When
July – September 1918
Where
Uozu, Toyama, and across Japan

01

What is the background to this event?

During the First World War, Japan's economy boomed, but prices rose sharply.1

  • The price of rice, the staple food, soared, causing severe hardship.1
  • Government attempts to control prices failed, and many suspected officials of colluding with profiteering merchants.1

In their own words

03

What happened?

On 22 July 1918, women in the fishing town of Uozu decided to stop rice being shipped out of the prefecture.1

  • The first protests, led almost entirely by women, were largely non-violent appeals to community fairness.1
  • Riots spread to nearly 500 places, involving one to two million people.1
  • In cities such as Nagoya, Osaka and Tokyo, the protests became more political and violent, with attacks on rice dealers and companies.1
  • In the coalfields, they took the form of organised labour disputes.1
  • The government deployed over 100,000 troops; dozens of civilians were killed and over 25,000 arrested.1

04

What happened next?

  • New government: Prime Minister Terauchi resigned, and Hara Takashi formed Japan's first stable party cabinet.1
  • Reforms: the riots led to reforms in food supply and social welfare, and to more rice being grown in Japan's colonies.1
  • Movements: the riots spurred the growth of labour and social movements.1
05

What do others say?

At the time

A government report called the riots a "crisis in relations between Labor and Capital".1

Debates since

  • Many riots: historians such as Michael Lewis distinguish the community protests of Toyama, the urban riots and the miners' disputes.12
  • Violence on both sides: rioters burned and looted the property of merchants accused of profiteering; troops killed dozens of people, and tens of thousands were arrested.1

How is it remembered?

  • Uozu remembers the "fishermen's wives' uprising" as the start of the riots.
08

Why does it matter?

The rice riots were the largest popular uprising in modern Japanese history.1

  • They began with working women defending their families' food.

Questions to think about

  1. Why did women lead the first protests?
  2. Why did the riots spread so quickly?
  3. Why did the protests become more violent in the cities?
