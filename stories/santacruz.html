The Santa Cruz Massacre

A funeral march for a young independence supporter ended with Indonesian soldiers firing on hundreds of people in a cemetery. A British cameraman smuggled out the film — and the world finally saw East Timor.

When
12 November 1991
Where
Santa Cruz cemetery, Dili, East Timor

See it on the map →

01

What is the background to this event?

East Timor, a Portuguese colony for centuries, declared independence in November 1975. Days later, Indonesia invaded, with the tacit approval of the United States and Australia.

  • The occupation was extremely brutal. Over the following years, around 100,000 to 180,000 people died from killings, starvation and disease — a large part of the population.
  • The resistance, led by Fretilin and its armed wing Falintil, continued in the mountains, while young people organised a clandestine movement in the towns.
  • In 1991, a visit by Portuguese parliamentarians was planned, raising hopes of international attention. When it was cancelled, tensions rose.1
  • On 28 October 1991, Indonesian troops raided the Motael Church in Dili. A young independence supporter, Sebastião Gomes, was shot dead, and a pro-Indonesian activist, Afonso Henriques, was stabbed and killed.1
02

Who were the key people?

  • Sebastião Gomes

    A young independence supporter killed by Indonesian troops on 28 October 1991.

  • Max Stahl

    A British cameraman who filmed the massacre and smuggled the footage out.

  • Amy Goodman and Allan Nairn

    American journalists who witnessed the massacre and were beaten by soldiers.

  • Kamal Bamadhaj

    A New Zealand student and human rights activist killed in the massacre.

  • The young people of Dili

    Who organised the march and the clandestine resistance.

03

What happened?

On 12 November 1991, after a memorial Mass for Sebastião Gomes, several thousand men, women and children walked from the Motael Church to the Santa Cruz cemetery. Along the way they unfurled banners and East Timorese flags. By most accounts, the crowd was loud but peaceful and orderly.1 It was the largest demonstration against the occupation since 1975.1

  • During a brief confrontation on the way, an Indonesian major was stabbed.1
  • When the procession reached the cemetery, around 200 Indonesian soldiers arrived and opened fire on the unarmed crowd.1
  • At least 250 people were killed, including Kamal Bamadhaj.1 Many more were wounded, arrested or disappeared in the following days.

Max Stahl filmed the massacre undercover and hid the tapes in a grave. They were smuggled out and broadcast around the world.1 Goodman and Nairn tried to shield the Timorese; soldiers beat them, fracturing Nairn's skull.1

04

What happened next?

  • International solidarity: the footage transformed world opinion. Solidarity movements grew in Portugal, Australia, the United States and elsewhere, and some governments cut military aid to Indonesia.
  • Nobel Prize: in 1996, Bishop Carlos Filipe Ximenes Belo and José Ramos-Horta received the Nobel Peace Prize.
  • Independence: after the fall of Suharto, East Timorese voted for independence in a UN referendum in 1999. Pro-Indonesian militias backed by the army then killed around 1,400 people and destroyed much of the country. Timor-Leste became independent in 2002.
05

What do others say?

At the time

The Indonesian military claimed the soldiers acted in self-defence after an attack and gave a much lower death toll. Witnesses and the footage showed soldiers firing on an unarmed crowd.

Debates since

  • The death toll: the Indonesian government acknowledged around 50 dead; human rights groups and the truth commission documented far more.
  • Violence: the stabbing of an officer before the shooting was used by the military to justify its response; investigators concluded that the firing was a deliberate massacre of unarmed people.
  • Western responsibility: historians debate how much Western governments knew and why they supported Indonesia for so long.
06

The evidence

  • Max Stahl's film, later used in the documentary In Cold Blood: The Massacre of East Timor.
  • Testimonies collected by the truth commission (CAVR).
  • Reports by Amnesty International and other human rights organisations.
07

How is it remembered?

  • 12 November is National Youth Day in Timor-Leste, commemorating the young people who died.
  • Each year, people march from the Motael Church to the Santa Cruz cemetery.
08

Why does it matter?

The Santa Cruz massacre was a turning point in the struggle for East Timor's independence. A few minutes of film made a forgotten occupation impossible to ignore.

  • It shows the power of witnesses and images.
  • It shows how young people sustained resistance through peaceful protest.
  • It raises questions about the role of Western governments in supporting the occupation.

What other events are like this?

  • 1945The Battle of Surabaya — Weeks after Indonesia declared independence, the young people of Surabaya tore the blue from a Dutch flag and then fought British and Indian troops street by street — a defeat that became a national legend.
  • 1976The Soweto Uprising — Thousands of Black schoolchildren marched against being taught in Afrikaans. Police opened fire, and the uprising spread across the country.
  • 1977The Mothers of the Plaza de Mayo — The mothers of Argentina's 'disappeared' walked in silent circles in front of the presidential palace, in open defiance of a military dictatorship.
