The Pots and Pans Revolution

When Iceland's banks collapsed, a singer stood alone in front of parliament with a microphone. Within months, thousands were banging pots and pans, and the government fell.

When
October 2008 – January 2009
Where
Austurvöllur, Reykjavík, Iceland

See it on the map →

01

What is the background to this event?

In October 2008, Iceland's three main banks collapsed in the global financial crisis, and the economy crashed.

  • Many Icelanders blamed the government and the banks.1
02

Who were the key people?

  • Hörður Torfason

    Singer and gay rights pioneer who began the protests.

  • Raddir fólksins ('Voices of the People')

    The group that organised weekly rallies.

  • Geir Haarde

    Prime minister, later tried before a special court.

03

What happened?

In October 2008, Hörður Torfason stood in Austurvöllur square with an open microphone and invited people to speak.1

  • The next Saturday, an organised demonstration took place, and the group decided to rally every Saturday until the government stepped down.1
  • On 20 January 2009, thousands protested at the parliament, banging pots and pans — at the time, the largest protests in Icelandic history.1
  • The government resigned, and elections in April 2009 brought a new left-wing government.1

Sources

  1. Wikipedia, '2009 Icelandic financial crisis protests', on the protests, the fall of the government, the trial of Geir Haarde, the Icesave referendums and the constitutional assembly. Link ↗
04

What happened next?

  • Accountability: former Prime Minister Geir Haarde was tried before a special court.1
  • Referendums: citizens voted in referendums on whether to repay the debts of the Icesave bank.1
  • A people's constitution: 25 ordinary citizens were elected to draft a new constitution, presented to parliament in 2011, though it was never adopted.1
05

What do others say?

At the time

The government argued it was handling the crisis; protesters demanded resignations and new elections.1

Debates since

  • Success? Supporters praise Iceland for prosecuting bankers and refusing to make taxpayers pay the Icesave debts; critics note the new constitution stalled.1
  • Violence: the protests were largely peaceful, though there were clashes with police in January 2009.
06

The evidence

  • Photographs and recordings of the weekly rallies.1
07

How is it remembered?

  • The constitutional draft remains a rallying point for Icelandic reformers.
08

Why does it matter?

The Pots and Pans Revolution showed citizens holding bankers and politicians to account after the financial crisis.

Questions to think about

  1. Why did one person's protest grow into a movement?
  2. Why did protesters bang pots and pans?
  3. Should leaders be put on trial for economic failures?
  4. Why was the crowd-sourced constitution never adopted?
