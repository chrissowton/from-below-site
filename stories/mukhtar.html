Omar al-Mukhtar and the Libyan Resistance

A Quran teacher in his sixties led a guerrilla war against Fascist Italy for nearly a decade. To defeat him, Italy forced half the population of eastern Libya into concentration camps.

When
1923 – 1932
Where
Jebel Akhdar, Cyrenaica, Libya

See it on the map →

01

What is the background to this event?

Italy invaded Libya, then part of the Ottoman Empire, in 1911. Resistance was led in the east, Cyrenaica, by the Senussi order, a Muslim religious brotherhood with deep roots among Bedouin tribes.1

  • After 1922, Mussolini's Fascist government launched a campaign to reconquer the whole of Libya.1
  • Using aircraft and motor transport, Italy occupied the coast and much of Tripolitania, but met fierce guerrilla resistance in the forested hills of the Jebel Akhdar ("Green Mountain") in Cyrenaica.1
02

Who were the key people?

  • Omar al-Mukhtar

    A Senussi teacher of the Quran who led the resistance from his sixties until his capture and hanging in 1931, aged about 73.

  • The Bedouin of Cyrenaica

    Herders who supported and fed the fighters, and who suffered most from Italian repression.

  • Rodolfo Graziani and Pietro Badoglio

    The Italian commanders responsible for the concentration camps.

03

What happened?

Led by Omar al-Mukhtar, small groups of fighters on horseback attacked Italian posts and convoys, then disappeared into the hills, supported by the Bedouin population.1 Negotiations between Italy and al-Mukhtar broke down in 1929–30.1

To separate the fighters from the people who supported them, the Italian commanders decided to remove the population entirely.1

  • About 100,000 Bedouin — half the population of Cyrenaica, mostly women, children and the elderly — were forced to march across the desert to concentration camps. Those who could not keep up were shot.1
  • In the camps, people lived behind barbed wire with little food, water or medicine. By the time the camps closed in 1933, around 40,000 of the internees had died.1
  • The Italian air force used chemical weapons against fighters in the desert.1
  • Italy built a 300-kilometre barbed-wire fence along the Egyptian border to cut off supplies.1

The resistance was also accused of abuses: Senussi fighters were accused of torturing and mutilating captured Italians and of refusing to take prisoners.1

In September 1931, al-Mukhtar was wounded and captured. After a brief trial, he was hanged on 16 September 1931 in front of 20,000 Libyans brought from the camps to watch.

04

What happened next?

  • Defeat: the resistance collapsed after al-Mukhtar's execution, and Italy declared Libya "pacified" in 1932.
  • The toll: about a quarter of the people of Cyrenaica — around 56,000 of 225,000 — died during the war and in the camps.1
  • Independence: Libya became independent in 1951, after Italy's defeat in the Second World War.
  • Apology: in 2008, Italy signed a treaty with Libya apologising for colonial rule and promising $5 billion in investment.
05

What do others say?

At the time

Fascist propaganda presented the camps as "oases of civilisation" and the resistance as banditry.1 Libyans saw it as a defence of their land and faith.

Historians' debates

  • Genocide? Ahmida and others argue that the camps were genocidal; Italian historians such as Angelo Del Boca exposed the crimes after decades of silence in Italy.
  • Violence by the resistance: Italian sources accused the Senussi of atrocities against prisoners; historians weigh these claims against the vastly greater scale of Italian killing.1
  • Silence in Italy: the film Lion of the Desert (1981), about al-Mukhtar, was banned in Italy for many years.
06

The evidence

  • Italian military records and official photographs of the camps.
  • Oral histories and poems of camp survivors, such as the famous poem Ma bi marad ("I have no illness but...") by Rajab Buhwaish.
  • Records of al-Mukhtar's trial.
07

How is it remembered?

  • Omar al-Mukhtar appears on Libyan banknotes and is honoured across the Arab world.
  • The film Lion of the Desert (1981), starring Anthony Quinn, told his story.
08

Why does it matter?

The Libyan resistance and the camps are among the least known colonial atrocities of the twentieth century. Historians such as Ali Abdullatif Ahmida describe them as genocide.

  • Omar al-Mukhtar became a symbol of resistance across the Arab and Muslim world.
  • The story raises questions about how colonial violence is remembered and taught.

Questions to think about

  1. Why did Italy decide to remove the entire population of the Jebel Akhdar?
  2. Why do you think Omar al-Mukhtar became such a powerful symbol?
  3. Why were Italy's crimes in Libya forgotten for so long?
  4. Is an apology enough to address colonial atrocities?

What other events are like this?

  • 1921Abd el-Krim and the Republic of the Rif — Berber mountain tribes inflicted one of the worst defeats on a European army in colonial history and founded a republic. Spain and France needed hundreds of thousands of troops — and poison gas — to crush it.
  • 1904The Herero and Nama Uprisings and Genocide — The Herero and Nama rose against German settlers who had taken their land and cattle. Germany's response was the first genocide of the twentieth century.
  • 1945The Sétif and Guelma Massacres — On the day the Second World War ended in Europe, Algerians marched for independence in Sétif. The colonial response killed thousands and set the course towards war.
