The vote & democracy

The Eureka Stockade

Gold miners furious at licence hunts and a tax they could not afford swore an oath under a blue flag with white stars. Soldiers stormed their stockade — but within two years the miners had won the vote.

When
1851 – December 1854
Where
Ballarat, Victoria, Australia
Facts checked
2026-11-15

See it on the map →

01

What is the background to this event?

In 1851, gold was discovered in Victoria, and prospectors poured in.1

  • The colonial government imposed a mining licence of 30 shillings a month, charged whether or not miners found gold.1
  • Police carried out frequent "licence hunts", and the system was enforced harshly.1
  • Miners organised meetings and stopped paying the tax en masse.1
  • In October 1854, the killing of a miner and the acquittal of the hotel keeper accused of it led to a riot in which the Eureka Hotel was burned.1

In their own words

03

What happened?

The Ballarat Reform League demanded reforms, including the vote for all men and the abolition of the licence.

  • On 29 November 1854, about 10,000 people met at Bakery Hill, where the Eureka Flag was flown for the first time.1
  • Peter Lalor led the swearing of an oath of allegiance to the flag, and the rebels built a stockade.1
  • At dawn on 3 December, soldiers and police stormed the stockade. According to official records, 27 people died, most of them rebels.1
  • Thirteen captured rebels were tried for high treason in Melbourne, but juries acquitted them all.1

04

What happened next?

  • Democracy: reforms followed, including the vote for all adult men in elections to Victoria's Legislative Assembly and the end of property qualifications for members.1
  • Lalor: in 1856, Peter Lalor was elected to the Legislative Assembly.1
05

What do others say?

At the time

The government saw a treasonous revolt; many colonists sympathised with the miners.1

Debates since

  • Democracy for whom? The miners won the vote for men; women, Aboriginal people and Chinese miners were left out, and the same report that recommended reform called for limits on Chinese miners.1
  • Violence: most of the dead were rebels killed in the storming of the stockade; some soldiers were also killed.1
  • The flag: the Eureka Flag has been used by unions, the left and, at times, the far right.

07

How is it remembered?

  • The Eureka Flag is displayed at the Eureka Centre in Ballarat, and the anniversary is marked each December.
08

Why does it matter?

Eureka is often seen as a birthplace of democracy in Australia.1

  • Historian Clare Wright has shown the role of women on the goldfields in the rebellion.2

Questions to think about

  1. Why did the licence feel so unjust to the miners?
  2. Why did juries refuse to convict the rebels?
  3. How did a short battle lead to lasting reform?
  4. Whose story is left out of the usual account of Eureka?

What other events are like this?

  • 1819The Peterloo Massacre — Tens of thousands of working people gathered peacefully to demand the vote. Within ten minutes, cavalry had cleared the field with sabres.
  • 1839The Chartists and the Newport Rising — Millions of working people signed the People's Charter demanding the vote. In Newport, thousands of miners and ironworkers marched — and soldiers opened fire.
  • 1893Women Win the Vote in New Zealand — A nationwide petition campaign made New Zealand the first self-governing country in which all women could vote in parliamentary elections.
