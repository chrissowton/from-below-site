Black January: Soviet Troops in Baku

As the Soviet Union weakened, Azerbaijan's Popular Front took control of parts of the republic. After a week that began with a pogrom against Armenians, 26,000 Soviet troops entered Baku and fired into the crowds.

When
13 – 22 January 1990
Where
Baku, Azerbaijan
Facts checked
2026-11-18

See it on the map →

01

What is the background to this event?

In the late 1980s, Azerbaijan and Armenia were in bitter conflict over Nagorno-Karabakh, a mainly Armenian region inside Soviet Azerbaijan.1

  • In December 1989, the Popular Front of Azerbaijan took over local government in several areas near the Iranian border.1
  • On 9 January 1990, Armenia's parliament voted to include Nagorno-Karabakh in its budget. Demonstrations in Azerbaijan demanded the removal of communist officials and independence from the Soviet Union. Human Rights Watch described their rhetoric as "heavily anti-Armenian".1

In their own words

03

What happened?

On 13 January 1990, a pogrom against Armenians began in Baku. It left 48 people dead, and thousands of Armenians fled or were evacuated.1

  • Local authorities were paralysed, and Soviet troops in the city did not intervene, saying they had no orders.1
  • On 18 January, the Popular Front barricaded the main roads into Baku with cars, trucks and buses.1
  • On 19 January, Moscow declared a state of emergency. Special forces blew up the power supply to the television station, and late that night 26,000 Soviet troops entered the city, smashing through the barricades.1
  • The troops fired into the crowds, and the shooting continued for three days. The state of emergency was announced only hours after the attack began.1
  • On 22 January, almost the whole population of Baku turned out to bury the dead. For 40 days, people stayed away from work in mourning and protest.1
04

What happened next?

  • Condemnation: on 22 January 1990, Azerbaijan's Supreme Soviet declared that the military deployment was an act of aggression.1
  • Investigations: in 1991, the human rights groups Memorial and Helsinki Watch reported that Soviet troops had used unjustified force, resulting in many deaths.1
  • Independence: on 18 October 1991, Azerbaijan's parliament restored the country's independence.1
05

What do others say?

At the time

Gorbachev and Yazov said the army was needed to stop violence against Armenians and an attempt to overthrow the Soviet Azerbaijani government; Azerbaijanis saw an attack on their independence movement.1

Debates since

  • Armed resistance? Gorbachev claimed that Popular Front gunmen fired first; the Moscow organisation Shield found no evidence of armed Popular Front fighters.1
  • Violence: Azerbaijan's official figure is 147 civilians killed and 800 injured; other sources give 133–137, with unofficial estimates up to 300. Reports put Soviet military deaths at between 21 and 29. The pogrom of the previous week killed 48 people.1
06

The evidence

  • Reports by Memorial, Helsinki Watch and Shield; Radio Liberty broadcasts; and lists of the victims.1

07

How is it remembered?

  • 20 January is a day of national mourning in Azerbaijan. Commemorative stamps and memorials honour the dead.1
08

Why does it matter?

Black January turned many Azerbaijanis against Soviet rule and became a founding memory of independent Azerbaijan. It also shows how national movements could be bound up with ethnic violence.1

Open questions

  1. Why did Moscow say it had sent in the army, and why did many Azerbaijanis disbelieve it?
  2. Why did the troops wait until after the pogrom against Armenians was over?
  3. Why did the authorities cut off television and radio before the troops entered?

09

What other events are like this?

  • 19899 April 1989: The Tbilisi Massacre — Thousands of Georgians gathered peacefully in the heart of Tbilisi to demand independence. Before dawn, Soviet soldiers attacked them with spades and gas, killing 21 people, most of them young women.
  • 1991The January Events in Vilnius — When Soviet tanks rolled towards Lithuania's television tower and parliament, tens of thousands of unarmed people came out to defend them. Fourteen civilians were killed — but the parliament was never taken.
  • 1991The Barricades of Riga — Fearing a Soviet attack, Latvians built barricades of concrete, logs and farm tractors around their parliament and key buildings — and kept watch around bonfires for two weeks in the January cold.
