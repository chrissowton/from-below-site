Yaa Asantewaa and the War of the Golden Stool

When a British governor demanded to sit on the sacred Golden Stool, the queen mother Yaa Asantewaa led the Asante in a last war against colonial rule.

When
March – September 1900
Where
Kumasi, Asante (present-day Ghana)

See it on the map →

01

What is the background to this event?

The Asante Empire, centred on the city of Kumasi, was one of the most powerful states in West Africa in the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries. During the nineteenth century it fought a series of wars with the British, who were expanding their Gold Coast colony on the coast.

At the heart of Asante identity was the Golden Stool (Sika Dwa Kofi). According to tradition, it was brought down from the sky by the priest Okomfo Anokye around 1700 and contained the soul (sunsum) of the Asante nation. No one — not even the king — was allowed to sit on it.

  • In 1896 the British occupied Kumasi without a fight and exiled the king, Prempeh I, together with other members of the Asante government. They were later sent to the Seychelles.2
  • Among the exiles was the grandson of Yaa Asantewaa, the queen mother of Ejisu, whom she had nominated as chief. In his absence she became regent of the Ejisu–Juaben district.2
  • The British imposed taxes and forced labour, and gold prospectors and missionaries moved into Asante.
02

Who were the key people?

  • Yaa Asantewaa

    Queen mother of Ejisu, born around 1840. She urged the Asante chiefs to fight and became the leader of the war. She died in exile in the Seychelles in 1921.

  • Sir Frederic Hodgson

    Governor of the Gold Coast, whose demand for the Golden Stool sparked the war.

  • Prempeh I

    The Asantehene (king of Asante), exiled by the British in 1896. He was allowed to return in 1924.

  • Colonel James Willcocks

    The British officer who led the relief force to Kumasi in July 1900.

  • The Asante fighters

    Thousands of soldiers from Ejisu and other Asante states who besieged the British fort.

03

What happened?

On 25 March 1900, Governor Frederic Hodgson arrived in Kumasi and summoned the Asante chiefs. He demanded that they hand over the Golden Stool, telling them:1

"Where is the Golden Stool? Why am I not sitting on the Golden Stool at this moment? I am the representative of the paramount power; why have you relegated me to this chair? Why did you not take the opportunity of my coming to Kumasi to bring the Golden Stool for me to sit upon?"Governor Frederic Hodgson, Kumasi, 28 March 19001

The demand was a deep insult. At a secret meeting of the remaining Asante leaders, the chiefs disagreed about what to do. According to Asante tradition, Yaa Asantewaa rose and challenged the men: if they would not fight, she said, the women would — and the men could exchange their loincloths for her undergarments. To show her determination, she seized a gun and fired a shot.2

War broke out at the end of March:12

  • Asante forces besieged the British fort at Kumasi, where Hodgson, his wife, officials, soldiers and many African civilians had taken refuge. Stockades were built to cut off the roads.
  • In June, with food running out, Hodgson and part of the garrison broke out of the fort and escaped to the coast, leaving a small force behind.
  • On 15 July, a relief force under Colonel James Willcocks reached Kumasi and relieved the fort.
  • Fighting continued in the forest for several more months, until the last Asante forces were defeated in September 1900.

Yaa Asantewaa was captured and, with other leaders, exiled to the Seychelles. The British annexed Asante as a Crown colony in 1901. But the Golden Stool itself was never captured: it had been hidden, and the British never found it.12

04

What happened next?

  • Exile: Yaa Asantewaa died in exile in the Seychelles on 17 October 1921.
  • The stool survives: in 1921 road builders accidentally uncovered the stool's hiding place, and some Asante men stripped it of gold ornaments; they were tried by an Asante court. The British authorities now declared that they would not interfere with the stool.
  • Restoration: Prempeh I was allowed to return in 1924. In 1935 the British restored the Asante Confederacy and the office of Asantehene.
  • Independence: the Gold Coast, including Asante, became independent as Ghana in 1957 — the first sub-Saharan African colony to do so.
05

What do others say?

At the time

British accounts presented the war as a rebellion by "savage" subjects and celebrated the heroism of the relief column. Asante accounts, preserved largely in oral tradition, presented it as a defence of national sovereignty and the soul of the nation.

Historians' debates

  • What did she say? Yaa Asantewaa's famous speech survives in several versions, passed down in oral tradition and later written down. Historians treat the exact words with caution, while accepting that she played a leading role.
  • Leader or symbol? Some historians, notably A. Adu Boahen, argue that she was the real war leader; others suggest the British exaggerated her role, or that later nationalists turned her into a symbol.
  • Why did the war happen? Historians point not only to the stool, but also to the exile of the king, taxation, forced labour and the activities of gold-mining companies.
06

The evidence

  • British military and colonial records, and memoirs such as Lady Hodgson's The Siege of Kumassi (1901).
  • Photographs taken by British officers and journalists during the campaign.
  • Asante oral traditions, including songs about Yaa Asantewaa.
07

How is it remembered?

  • The Yaa Asantewaa Museum at Ejisu was opened in 2000 for the centenary of the war; it was badly damaged by fire in 2004.
  • The Yaa Asantewaa Girls' Senior High School in Kumasi and many other institutions bear her name.
  • Songs about her are still sung in Ghana, and she is honoured in the Ghanaian diaspora.
08

Why does it matter?

The War of the Golden Stool was the last of the Anglo-Asante wars. It was lost on the battlefield, but it won something important: the British never again attempted to seize the stool, and the Asante kingdom and its institutions survived colonial rule.

  • Yaa Asantewaa is one of the best-known women leaders of resistance to colonialism in Africa.
  • The war shows how colonial rulers' disregard for the sacred and political meaning of objects could provoke resistance.
  • She remains a symbol of courage and of women's leadership in Ghana and across the African diaspora.

Questions to think about

  1. Why do you think Hodgson's demand caused such outrage?
  2. How reliable is oral tradition as a historical source? How does it compare with colonial records?
  3. Can a war that is lost on the battlefield still be a success?
  4. Why might a woman leader have been especially significant in this war — both to the Asante and to the British?
