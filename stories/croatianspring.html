The Croatian Spring

A campaign for economic autonomy and the Croatian language drew in reform communists, writers and tens of thousands of students. Tito crushed it in December 1971 — and stirred tensions that would return two decades later.

When
1967 – 1971
Where
Zagreb, Croatia
Facts checked
2026-11-16

See it on the map →

01

What is the background to this event?

Yugoslavia was a federation of six republics under Josip Broz Tito. In the 1960s, economic reforms led republics to defend their own interests.1

  • Croatian reformers wanted Croatian firms to keep more of their hard-currency earnings.1
  • A central issue was whether Croatian was a language in its own right, rather than a variant of Serbo-Croatian.1
  • The cultural society Matica hrvatska became a centre of the movement.1

In their own words

03

What happened?

From the late 1960s, grievances were aired through Matica hrvatska. In the early 1970s, they were taken up by reformers in the Croatian party led by Savka Dabčević-Kučar and Miko Tripalo.1

  • Demands grew from economic reform to political autonomy, the Croatian language, and complaints about the over-representation of Serbs in the police and politics in Croatia.1
  • Hrvatski tjednik, edited by Vlado Gotovac, became the best-selling paper in Croatia.1
  • Students elected Dražen Budiša to lead their federation. Tito asked Dabčević-Kučar to arrest activists; she refused.1
  • In November 1971, students went on strike.
  • In December 1971, Tito forced the reformist leadership to resign. Matica hrvatska was banned and hundreds were arrested; thousands were expelled from the party.1

04

What happened next?

  • Prison: leaders including Budiša, Gotovac and the historian Franjo Tuđman were jailed.
  • The 1974 constitution gave the republics more power, granting some of the movement's demands.
  • Later: Tuđman became Croatia's first president in 1990; Croatia declared independence in 1991, followed by war.
05

What do others say?

At the time

Tito and conservatives accused the movement of nationalism and counter-revolution; supporters saw a demand for fair treatment within Yugoslavia.1

Debates since

  • Reform or nationalism? Historians still disagree about whether it was a liberal reform movement or a nationalist one.2
  • Serbs in Croatia: some of its demands alarmed Croatia's Serbs, increasing tensions.1
  • Non-violence: the movement was peaceful; the repression used arrests and purges.

How is it remembered?

  • In Croatia the movement is widely seen as a forerunner of independence.
08

Why does it matter?

The Croatian Spring was the largest challenge to Tito's Yugoslavia, combining demands for reform, language rights and national autonomy.

  • It shows how national grievances can unite people — and divide them from neighbours.
