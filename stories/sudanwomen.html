Fatima Ahmed Ibrahim and the Sudanese Women's Union

A schoolgirl who led a strike for science lessons founded one of Africa's largest women's movements. In 1965 she became the first woman elected to Sudan's parliament.

When
1952 – 1965
Where
Omdurman and Khartoum, Sudan

See it on the map →

01

What is the background to this event?

Under Anglo-Egyptian rule, few Sudanese girls received formal education. The British authorities opposed formal schooling for girls, and women had no vote.2

  • Fatima Ahmed Ibrahim was born in Khartoum around 1930. Her father, a teacher, was expelled from a government school for refusing to teach in English.1
  • At Omdurman Girls' Secondary School, she ran a wall newspaper on women's rights and led what is described as Sudan's first women's strike, after the school replaced science lessons with "family science". The strike succeeded.1
  • The struggle for Sudanese independence from Britain was gathering pace.
02

Who were the key people?

  • Fatima Ahmed Ibrahim

    Founder member and later president of the Sudanese Women's Union, and the first woman elected to Sudan's parliament (1965). She died in 2017.

  • Fatima Talib Ismail

    The first president of the Sudanese Women's Union.

  • Khalida Zahir

    One of Sudan's first women doctors and a founder of the union.

  • Suad Ibrahim Ahmed

    A writer and activist who argued for a more secular women's movement.

03

What happened?

On 17 January 1952, a group of women founded the Sudanese Women's Union (SWU), with Fatima Talib as president and Fatima Ahmed Ibrahim and Khalida Zahir on the executive committee.2

  • The union was open to all women and set up branches across Sudan.1
  • Its demands included the vote, the right of women to stand for all legislative and administrative bodies, equal pay and training, and an end to illiteracy among women.1
  • It set up schools and evening classes for women, campaigned against forced and child marriage, and called for women's right to consent to marriage.2
  • From 1955, Fatima Ahmed Ibrahim edited the union's magazine, Sawt al-Mar'a (Woman's Voice), which played an important part in the movement that overthrew the military government of Ibrahim Abboud in October 1964.1

After the October 1964 revolution, Sudanese women won the vote. In 1965, Fatima Ahmed Ibrahim was elected to parliament — the first woman in Sudan, and according to some accounts the first in any African parliament.2

04

What happened next?

  • Repression: after President Jaafar Nimeiry banned the union, Fatima Ahmed Ibrahim was placed under house arrest.2 Her husband, the trade union leader Al-Shafi Ahmed al-Sheikh, was executed after a failed coup in 1971.
  • Family law: the union's campaigns helped win reforms in family law and equal pay in the 1970s.2
  • Recognition: in 1993 she received the UN Prize in the Field of Human Rights.
  • New generations: women were at the forefront of the 2018–19 revolution that overthrew Omar al-Bashir.
05

What do others say?

At the time

Islamist groups opposed the union, arguing that its demands were against religion.1 Fatima Ahmed Ibrahim argued that Islam could be a progressive force for women.1

Debates

  • Religion and feminism: within the movement, Suad Ibrahim Ahmed argued for grounding women's rights in secular ideas; Fatima Ahmed Ibrahim preferred to argue within an Islamic framework.1
  • Communist links: Fatima Ahmed Ibrahim was a member of the Sudanese Communist Party, but sought to keep the union independent of it.1

07

How is it remembered?

  • Tens of thousands attended Fatima Ahmed Ibrahim's funeral in Khartoum in 2017.
  • Women in the 2019 revolution invoked the memory of earlier women activists.
08

Why does it matter?

The Sudanese Women's Union was one of the largest women's movements in post-independence Africa.2 It linked women's rights to education, work, independence and democracy.

  • It challenges the idea that feminism was imported into Africa from the West.
  • It shows how women's movements can contribute to national revolutions.
