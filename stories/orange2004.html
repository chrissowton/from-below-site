The vote & democracy

The Orange Revolution

When a presidential run-off was rigged, hundreds of thousands of Ukrainians camped in the snow in central Kyiv for weeks. The Supreme Court annulled the result, and the election was held again.

When
22 November 2004 – 23 January 2005
Where
Maidan Nezalezhnosti (Independence Square), Kyiv, Ukraine
Facts checked

01

What is the background to this event?

Leonid Kuchma had been president of Ukraine since 1994, and his rule had been shaken by scandal.1

  • In 2000, the journalist Georgiy Gongadze, founder of a newspaper that exposed corruption, was kidnapped and murdered. Persistent rumours suggested that Kuchma had ordered the killing, and a movement against him began.1
  • Kuchma did not stand again in 2004. He backed his prime minister, Viktor Yanukovych, against the opposition leader Viktor Yushchenko.1
  • In September 2004, Yushchenko suffered dioxin poisoning in mysterious circumstances. He survived and returned to the campaign.1
  • Both East and West took sides. Russia's president, Vladimir Putin, visited Ukraine several times, and Kremlin political advisers ran a campaign for Yanukovych. The United States spent $65 million over two years to support political organisations in Ukraine.1

In their own words

03

What happened?

In the first round on 31 October 2004, the two leading candidates were almost level, each with about 39% of the vote.1

  • After the run-off on 21 November, the authorities declared Yanukovych the winner. Domestic and foreign election monitors reported widespread fraud.1
  • From 22 November, protests began in cities across Ukraine. The largest, on Kyiv's Independence Square, drew an estimated 500,000 people; on some days up to a million were on the streets, in freezing weather. Protesters wore orange, the colour of Yushchenko's campaign.1
  • The opposition called for civil disobedience, sit-ins and general strikes. Pora! and other activists used mobile phones and the internet to coordinate election monitoring and protests.12
  • On 28 November, according to The New York Times, more than 10,000 interior ministry troops were mobilised to clear the square. Security chiefs warned their commander to pull back, and he did, preventing bloodshed.1
  • On 3 December, the Supreme Court annulled the run-off and ordered a new vote. On 26 December, under intense scrutiny, Yushchenko won with about 52% to Yanukovych's 44%.1
  • Yushchenko was inaugurated on 23 January 2005.1

The revolution was largely peaceful. About 18% of Ukrainians later said they had taken part, but support was strongest in the west and centre of the country; many in the south and east backed Yanukovych.1

04

What happened next?

  • Disappointment: the 'Orange' coalition split, and a 2007 study found Ukrainians viewed the revolution less positively than in 2005.1
  • Reversal: in 2010, Yanukovych won the presidency in an election that international observers judged fair.1
  • Fraud: no officials involved in the 2004 fraud were convicted.1
05

What do others say?

At the time

Supporters saw a democratic revolution; Yanukovych's supporters and the Russian government saw a Western-backed coup.1

Debates since

  • From below? Western organisations funded election monitoring and civil society, while Russia funded and advised Yanukovych's campaign. Historians debate how much either decided the outcome compared with the hundreds of thousands who came out.1
  • Violence: the protests ended without deaths; the decision to pull back the troops on 28 November is disputed in its details.1
  • A divided country: the revolution's support came mainly from western and central Ukraine.1

07

How is it remembered?

  • In 2005, President Yushchenko declared 22 November, the day the protests began, a 'Day of Freedom'; in 2011 President Yanukovych moved it to another date. Opinion about the revolution has remained divided, largely along regional lines.1
08

Why does it matter?

The Orange Revolution showed that mass, peaceful protest could overturn a rigged election in a large post-Soviet state. It alarmed governments in Russia and Belarus, which came to use the word 'orange' as a warning.1

Questions to think about

  1. Why did a rigged run-off bring so many people onto the streets?
  2. Why did the troops sent on 28 November pull back?
  3. How much did foreign money matter — from the West and from Russia?
  4. Why was the country so divided between west and east?
09

What other events are like this?

  • 2003The Rose Revolution — After a rigged election, Georgians filled the streets for three weeks. Protesters carrying roses entered parliament, and the president resigned without a shot being fired.
  • 2000Otpor! and the Fall of Milošević — A handful of students with a clenched-fist logo and a sense of humour built a movement of 70,000 young people. Their non-violent campaign helped bring down Slobodan Milošević.
  • 2006The Jeans Revolution — When Alexander Lukashenko claimed 83% of the vote, young Belarusians pitched tents in central Minsk and wore denim as a symbol of freedom. After a week, riot police cleared the camp — this colour revolution failed.
