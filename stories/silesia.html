The Silesian Weavers' Uprising

Hand weavers starving on falling piece rates marched on the mansions of the merchants who paid them. Prussian troops shot eleven of them dead, and the rising became a symbol for poets, playwrights and Karl Marx.

When
4 – 6 June 1844
Where
Peterswaldau (Pieszyce), Lower Silesia, Poland
Facts checked
2026-11-16

See it on the map →

01

What is the background to this event?

Silesia was a centre of handloom weaving, now in south-west Poland but then part of Prussia.1

  • After 1815, machine-made British cloth and the loss of markets ruined the Silesian linen trade.1
  • Weavers worked at home for contractors, who supplied raw material and paid for finished cloth — and kept cutting their pay.1
  • In the "Hungry Forties", many weavers could not cover even their basic needs.1

03

What happened?

On 4 June 1844, weavers in Peterswaldau marched to the mansion of their contractors to demand higher pay.1

  • They were met with scorn and threats, and broke into the house, smashing machinery and property.1
  • The revolt spread to nearby towns, especially Langenbielau (now Bielawa).1
  • On 6 June, Prussian troops fired on the weavers, killing 11.1
  • Others were arrested and put on trial.1

04

What happened next?

  • 1848: the uprising is seen as a forerunner of the revolutions of 1848.1
  • The labour movement: it was one of the first major labour revolts in the German-speaking world.1
05

What do others say?

At the time

The authorities saw a riot; writers such as Heine saw the beginnings of a revolution.1

Debates since

  • Class struggle? Marx saw the uprising as a sign of working-class consciousness; historians note that it targeted particular contractors.12
  • Violence: the weavers destroyed property; soldiers shot 11 of them dead.1
06

The evidence

Primary source: Painting

What is it?
Painting
Made by, and when
Carl Wilhelm Hübner, Düsseldorf, 1844

A painting from the year of the uprising showing weavers handing over their cloth to a merchant who sets the price. When it was exhibited in Germany it caused a stir, because it showed the poverty of the weavers to a wider public.

07

How is it remembered?

  • Gerhart Hauptmann's play The Weavers (1892) and Käthe Kollwitz's prints A Weavers' Revolt made the rising famous.1
08

Why does it matter?

The weavers' uprising showed the human cost of industrialisation, and made the "social question" a central issue in Germany.

Questions to think about

  1. Why were hand weavers so badly hit by industrialisation?
  2. Why did the weavers attack the contractors' houses?

What other events are like this?

  • 1830The Swing Riots — Farm labourers across southern England smashed the threshing machines that took their winter work, and sent threatening letters signed by a mythical 'Captain Swing'. The state answered with hangings and transportation.
  • 1378The Revolt of the Ciompi — The wool workers of Florence, excluded from the guilds that ran the city, seized the government — and for a few weeks a wool-carder sat at the head of the republic.
  • 1888The Matchwomen's Strike — About 1,400 women and girls walked out of a match factory, won their demands in three weeks, and founded the largest union of women in Britain.
