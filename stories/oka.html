The Kanehsatà:ke Resistance (Oka Crisis)

When a town planned to extend a golf course over a Mohawk burial ground and forest, Kanien'kehá:ka people barricaded the road — beginning a 78-day standoff with police and the Canadian army.

When
11 July – 26 September 1990
Where
Kanehsatà:ke and Oka, Quebec, Canada
Facts checked
2026-11-16

01

What is the background to this event?

The Kanien'kehá:ka (Mohawk) community of Kanehsatà:ke, near Montreal, has lived on the land around the Lake of Two Mountains since the eighteenth century. Their claim to the land has a long and painful history:1

  • In 1717 the French king granted the land to a Catholic religious order, the Sulpicians, for a mission to the Indigenous people. The Kanien'kehá:ka say that the land was meant to be theirs.
  • For more than two centuries, the Kanien'kehá:ka petitioned British and Canadian authorities for recognition of their land rights, without success. The Sulpicians sold much of the land to settlers.
  • In 1959 the town of Oka approved a private nine-hole golf course on part of the disputed land, next to a forest known as the Pines and a Kanien'kehá:ka cemetery.1
  • In 1977 the community filed a land claim with the federal government, which was rejected in 1986.1

In March 1989, the golf club announced plans to expand the course by another nine holes, into the Pines and next to the cemetery, and to build luxury housing. The town did not consult the Kanien'kehá:ka.1

03

What happened?

In March 1990, after a court allowed the golf course expansion to go ahead, members of the Kanien'kehá:ka community erected a barricade on a small dirt road leading into the Pines.1 On the morning of 11 July 1990, at the request of the mayor of Oka, the provincial police — the Sûreté du Québec (SQ) — raided the barricade with a tactical team, using tear gas and concussion grenades.1 A firefight broke out, and Corporal Marcel Lemay was shot and killed. The coroner's inquiry could not determine who fired the fatal shot.1

The crisis spread quickly:1

  • In solidarity, Kanien'kehá:ka from Kahnawà:ke blocked the Honoré Mercier Bridge, a major route into Montreal, and the highways around their territory.
  • For weeks, the blockade caused enormous disruption. Crowds of non-Indigenous residents in nearby Châteauguay rioted, burned effigies of Mohawks and attacked police.
  • Indigenous people across Canada held solidarity protests and blockades.
  • In August, the Quebec government asked for the Canadian army. Thousands of soldiers, with armoured vehicles, replaced the police around Kanehsatà:ke and Kahnawà:ke.
  • On 28 August, when families with children and elderly people tried to leave Kahnawà:ke by car, a crowd at the Mercier Bridge pelted the cars with stones while police stood by.

The Mercier Bridge blockade was lifted in early September after negotiations. At Kanehsatà:ke, the remaining defenders — surrounded by soldiers in the treatment centre — walked out on 26 September 1990. In the confusion, a fourteen-year-old girl, Waneek Horn-Miller, was stabbed with a bayonet by a soldier.12 The crisis had lasted 78 days.1

04

What happened next?

  • The golf course: the expansion was cancelled, and the federal government bought the disputed land — but did not transfer it to the community. The land claim remains unresolved.
  • A Royal Commission: in 1991 the federal government set up the Royal Commission on Aboriginal Peoples. Its 1996 report called for a fundamental change in the relationship between Indigenous peoples and Canada.
  • Prosecutions: dozens of people were charged after the crisis; most were acquitted.
  • A new generation: the crisis inspired a generation of Indigenous activists and artists, and influenced later movements such as Idle No More (2012).
05

What do others say?

At the time

Many politicians and media outlets described the armed Mohawk Warriors as criminals and terrorists, and emphasised the death of Corporal Lemay. Indigenous people and their supporters described a community defending its land and its dead against an aggressive police raid.

Historians' and commentators' debates

  • Weapons: the presence of armed Warriors divided opinion, including within Kanien'kehá:ka communities. Some argue that it attracted attention and deterred attack; others that it allowed governments to frame the crisis as a security problem.
  • Who was responsible? A coroner's report criticised the planning of the police raid and noted that police communications that should have been recorded were not provided.
  • What did it achieve? The golf course was stopped and a Royal Commission created, but the underlying land claim remains unresolved more than thirty years later.

How is it remembered?

  • Indigenous communities and activists mark the anniversary of 11 July each year.
  • Alanis Obomsawin's Kanehsatake: 270 Years of Resistance (1993) is one of the most important documentaries in Canadian history.
  • The Pines still stand, and the community continues to press its land claim.
08

Why does it matter?

The Oka Crisis was a turning point in relations between Indigenous peoples and the Canadian state. A local dispute over a golf course exposed centuries of unresolved land claims and broken promises, and forced the whole country to pay attention.

  • It showed the depth of Indigenous anger at the failure to recognise land rights.
  • It led to the Royal Commission on Aboriginal Peoples, a landmark in Canadian Indigenous policy.
  • It remains a powerful symbol of Indigenous resistance — and of the racism that Indigenous people faced.
