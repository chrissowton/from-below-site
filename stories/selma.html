Civil rights & anti-racism

Bloody Sunday at the Edmund Pettus Bridge

Around six hundred marchers set out from Selma to demand the right to vote. On the Edmund Pettus Bridge, state troopers attacked them with clubs and tear gas.

01

What is the background to this event?

The Fifteenth Amendment to the US Constitution, ratified in 1870, prohibited denying the vote on grounds of race. Across much of the South, however, Black citizens were systematically prevented from voting through poll taxes, literacy tests applied at the registrar's discretion, restricted office hours, economic retaliation and violence.

Dallas County, Alabama, of which Selma is the county seat, was a stark example. Black residents made up roughly half the population, yet only about 2 per cent of eligible Black voters in Selma — some 300 out of 15,000 — had managed to register.

  • Local people had been campaigning for years through the Dallas County Voters League, led by figures such as Amelia Boynton and Frederick Reese.
  • From 1963 organisers from the Student Nonviolent Coordinating Committee (SNCC) worked in Selma; in January 1965 Martin Luther King Jr. and the Southern Christian Leadership Conference (SCLC) joined the campaign.
  • On 18 February 1965, at a night march in nearby Marion, a state trooper shot Jimmie Lee Jackson, a young deacon who was trying to protect his mother. He died eight days later.

In response, activists planned a march of 54 miles from Selma to the state capitol in Montgomery. Governor George Wallace declared: "There will be no march between Selma and Montgomery."

02

Who were the key people?

  • John Lewis

    Chairman of SNCC, aged 25, who led the march at the front of the column. His skull was fractured. He later served as a US congressman for more than thirty years.

  • Hosea Williams

    An SCLC organiser who led the march alongside Lewis.

  • Amelia Boynton

    A long-standing Selma voting-rights organiser. She was beaten unconscious on the bridge, and a photograph of her lying injured was published around the world.

  • Jimmie Lee Jackson

    A 26-year-old church deacon and voting-rights activist whose killing prompted the march.

  • Diane Nash and James Bevel

    SCLC strategists who conceived the idea of a march to Montgomery.

  • Sheriff Jim Clark and Major John Cloud

    Clark commanded the Dallas County posse; Cloud commanded the state troopers on the bridge.

  • James Reeb and Viola Liuzzo

    Supporters from the North who were murdered during the campaign — Reeb, a Unitarian minister, in Selma on 9 March, and Liuzzo, a mother of five from Detroit, on 25 March.

03

What happened?

On Sunday 7 March 1965, an estimated 525 to 600 marchers left Brown Chapel AME Church and walked in double file through Selma towards the Edmund Pettus Bridge, which carries US Route 80 over the Alabama River.1 The bridge was named after a Confederate general who later became a leader of the Ku Klux Klan in Alabama.

At the far side of the bridge, a line of Alabama state troopers and members of Sheriff Jim Clark's mounted posse was waiting. Major John Cloud ordered the marchers to disperse and gave them two minutes to do so.2 Moments later the troopers advanced:

  • Marchers were knocked to the ground and beaten with clubs; mounted men rode into the column.
  • Troopers fired tear gas, and the posse pursued marchers back across the bridge and into the Black neighbourhood around Brown Chapel.
  • In all, 17 marchers were hospitalised and around 50 more were treated for lesser injuries.1

John Lewis later recalled that day on the bridge: "I thought I saw death."3 That evening, television networks broadcast footage of the attack; ABC interrupted its showing of the film Judgment at Nuremberg to show it. President Lyndon Johnson issued a statement "deploring the brutality with which a number of Negro citizens of Alabama were treated."1

On 9 March, Martin Luther King Jr. led around 2,500 marchers back to the bridge, where they knelt to pray and then turned around, in deference to a federal court order — an event remembered as "Turnaround Tuesday".4 That night, the Rev. James Reeb was beaten by white segregationists in Selma; he died two days later. On 15 March, President Johnson addressed a joint session of Congress:

"At times history and fate meet at a single time in a single place to shape a turning point in man's unending search for freedom. So it was at Lexington and Concord. So it was a century ago at Appomattox. So it was last week in Selma, Alabama."President Lyndon B. Johnson, 'The American Promise', 15 March 19655

He ended his appeal for a voting rights bill with the words of the movement's anthem: "And we shall overcome."5 The bill was sent to Congress on 17 March.2 On 21 March, under the protection of federalised National Guard troops, the march set out again, reaching Montgomery on 25 March, where a crowd of around 25,000 gathered at the state capitol.4

04

What happened next?

President Johnson signed the Voting Rights Act on 6 August 1965. It outlawed literacy tests, authorised federal examiners to register voters, and required states and counties with a history of discrimination to obtain federal approval ("preclearance") before changing their voting rules.

  • Black voter registration across the South rose sharply within a few years, and federal examiners began registering voters in Dallas County that summer.
  • In 2013, in Shelby County v. Holder, the US Supreme Court struck down the formula used to determine which areas required preclearance, effectively suspending that part of the Act.
  • When John Lewis died in 2020, his coffin was carried across the Edmund Pettus Bridge one final time. Calls to rename the bridge after him continue.
05

What do others say?

At the time

Governor George Wallace and Alabama officials described the marchers as "outsiders" and agitators and justified their actions as necessary to protect traffic and public safety. Some white Southerners and newspapers accused civil rights leaders of deliberately provoking violence for publicity. Within the movement, SNCC activists were critical of Martin Luther King Jr.'s decision to turn back on 9 March.

Historians' debates

  • Leaders and local people: older accounts centred on King; historians such as Charles Payne and Clayborne Carson have emphasised the years of grassroots work by local people and SNCC organisers.
  • Strategy: some historians argue that the movement deliberately sought confrontation to win national attention; others stress that the violence was the responsibility of those who inflicted it.
  • Johnson's role: historians debate how far the President was a reluctant or a committed supporter of voting rights.
  • The film Selma (2014) was criticised by some historians for its portrayal of Johnson as an obstacle.

The evidence

  • President Johnson's recorded telephone conversations.

07

How is it remembered?

  • The Selma to Montgomery National Historic Trail was established in 1996.
  • Anniversary marches across the bridge have been attended by Presidents Clinton, Obama and Biden.
  • The bridge still bears Edmund Pettus's name; campaigns to rename it after John Lewis have so far been unsuccessful.
08

Why does it matter?

Selma demonstrates that democratic rights in the United States were won by the people who were denied them. The march itself lasted only minutes before it was attacked, but it rested on years of patient local organising, much of it by people whose names rarely appear in films and textbooks. Their persistence, and their refusal to respond to violence with violence, turned a local struggle into a national crisis that the federal government could no longer ignore.

  • The Voting Rights Act of 1965 is widely regarded as one of the most effective civil rights laws in American history.
  • The events showed the power of television in exposing state violence to a national audience.
  • The continuing debate over voting access in the United States shows that these rights must still be defended.

What other events are like this?

  • 1819The Peterloo Massacre — Tens of thousands of working people gathered peacefully to demand the vote. Within ten minutes, cavalry had cleared the field with sabres.
  • 1930The Salt March — A 24-day march to the sea to make salt in defiance of British law set off a campaign of civil disobedience across India.
  • 1976The Soweto Uprising — Thousands of Black schoolchildren marched against being taught in Afrikaans. Police opened fire, and the uprising spread across the country.
