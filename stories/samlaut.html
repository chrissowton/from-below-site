The Samlaut Uprising

Cambodian rice farmers, forced to sell their harvest to the army at gunpoint and pushed off their land, rose against Prince Sihanouk's government. The brutal repression that followed helped drive many into the arms of the Khmer Rouge.

When
1967 – 1968
Where
Samlaut, Battambang Province, Cambodia
Facts checked
2026-11-15

See it on the map →

01

What is the background to this event?

In the 1960s, Cambodia was ruled by Prince Norodom Sihanouk through his political movement, the Sangkum. He tried to keep Cambodia neutral as war raged in neighbouring Vietnam.

  • Thousands of Khmer Krom families fleeing the war in South Vietnam were resettled in Battambang Province, and many local people found themselves displaced from their land.1
  • In Pailin, land was bought up for plantations, and Khmer peasants were reduced to labourers.1
  • Much of Cambodia's rice was being sold illegally to the Viet Cong and on the Saigon black market, depriving the government of tax revenue.1
  • In early 1967, Prime Minister Lon Nol introduced the ramassage du paddy: the army bought and collected rice from peasants at low prices, often at gunpoint.1
02

Who were the key people?

  • The peasants of Samlaut and Battambang

    Rice farmers who rose against the government.

  • Khieu Samphan, Hou Yuon and Hu Nim

    Leftist politicians who were accused of stirring up the revolt and fled to the jungle. Two later became Khmer Rouge leaders.

  • Lon Nol

    The prime minister who introduced the rice collection and directed the repression.

  • Prince Norodom Sihanouk

    The head of state, who blamed foreign communists for the revolt.

03

What happened?

On 2 April 1967, angry peasants in Samlaut attacked soldiers collecting rice, killing two of them and seizing rifles.1

  • In the following weeks, the revolt spread, with more attacks on government property and personnel.1
  • By June 1967, four thousand or more villagers had fled their homes into the forest to join the rebels and escape the troops sent by Sihanouk.1
  • The army responded with great brutality, burning villages and killing suspected rebels; estimates of the dead range from hundreds to many thousands.

In early 1968, a second, more organised uprising spread more widely across several provinces and was more destructive than the first.1

04

What happened next?

  • The Khmer Rouge: some historians see the Samlaut rebellion as the beginning of the Cambodian revolutionary movement that led to the Khmer Rouge victory in 1975.1
  • War: in 1970, Lon Nol overthrew Sihanouk. Cambodia was drawn into the Vietnam War, and US bombing devastated the countryside.
  • Genocide: the Khmer Rouge regime of 1975–79 killed around 1.5 to 2 million Cambodians — many of them peasants like those of Samlaut.
05

What do others say?

At the time

Sihanouk blamed foreign — mainly Vietnamese — communists and leftist politicians. Peasants were driven by their own grievances.

Historians' debates

  • Spontaneous or communist-led? David Chandler and Milton Osborne see the revolt as mainly a local, spontaneous response to injustice; Ben Kiernan sees it as the "baptism of fire" of the Cambodian revolutionary movement.1
  • Political memory: both the later republican government and the Khmer Rouge's allies had reasons to tell the story in different ways.1
  • Violence: rebels killed soldiers and officials; the army's repression killed far more, including many uninvolved villagers.
06

The evidence

  • Cambodian government reports and press of the time.
  • Interviews with survivors, collected by historians such as Ben Kiernan.
07

How is it remembered?

  • The uprising is little remembered publicly in Cambodia, overshadowed by the later war and genocide.
08

Why does it matter?

The Samlaut uprising shows how peasant grievances — over land, prices and the abuse of power — can explode into revolt. It also shows how such revolts can be captured by movements that later commit terrible crimes.

  • It is a reminder that history from below includes tragedies as well as triumphs.
  • It raises questions about how state repression can drive people towards extremism.

Questions to think about

  1. Why did the ramassage du paddy cause such anger?
  2. Why do historians disagree about who led the uprising?
  3. How can state repression push people towards extremist movements?
  4. Why is it important to study revolts that led to tragic outcomes?
09

What other events are like this?

  • 1930The Nghệ-Tĩnh Soviets — Peasants and workers in central Vietnam rose against French taxes, landlords and mandarins, and set up their own village councils. The French bombed them from the air.
  • 1894The Donghak Peasant Revolution — Korean peasants inspired by a new religion rose against corrupt officials, briefly governed their province through peasant councils, and then fought the Japanese army.
  • 1932The 1932 Uprising and La Matanza — Indigenous peasants and communists rose against a military government in coffee country. The army's revenge killed thousands and nearly wiped out a language.
