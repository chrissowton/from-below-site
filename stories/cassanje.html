The Baixa de Cassanje Revolt

Angolan farmers forced to grow cotton for a colonial company refused to work and burned their identity cards. Portugal answered with air raids — and within weeks Angola's war of independence had begun.

When
January 1961
Where
Baixa de Cassanje, Malanje, Angola

2026-11-16

01

What is the background to this event?

Portugal had claimed Angola since the sixteenth century, but its control of the interior was established only in the early twentieth century. Under the dictatorship of António de Oliveira Salazar, Portugal insisted that its colonies were "overseas provinces" of a single nation, and refused to consider independence.

  • Most Africans were classified as indígenas (natives), without civil rights, subject to a special labour code and to forced labour.
  • In the fertile Baixa de Cassanje, in the district of Malanje, African farmers were compelled to grow cotton for Cotonang, a company with Portuguese and Belgian capital, which bought the crop at low fixed prices. Farmers had to neglect their own food crops to meet their quotas.12
  • Across Africa, colonies were becoming independent. In June 1960, the neighbouring Belgian Congo became independent, and news spread across the border.

    Another leader of the protest.

  • The cotton farmers of the Baixa de Cassanje

    Thousands of Mbundu farmers who refused to cultivate cotton.

  • Cotonang

    The cotton company that held the monopoly on buying the crop.

03

What happened?

In the closing weeks of 1960, discontent among the cotton farmers of the Baixa de Cassanje boiled over. On 4 January 1961, workers refused to work and rose in protest, led by two previously unknown Angolans, António Mariano and Kulu-Xingu.1

  • The workers burned their identification cards, which marked their status as colonial subjects.1
  • Some protesters attacked Portuguese traders on company premises, and destroyed seed, property and equipment.1
  • The protest became a general uprising across the area.1

The Portuguese authorities responded with overwhelming force. The next day, the air force bombed some twenty villages in the area, killing large numbers of villagers, and troops moved in to crush the revolt. Many survivors fled into the bush or across the border.1 The number of deaths has never been established: the MPLA independence movement later claimed some 10,000; most estimates range from 400 to as many as 7,000.1

04

What happened next?

  • Luanda, 4 February 1961: a month later, nationalists attacked prisons and a police station in the capital, Luanda, to free political prisoners. Reprisals by settlers and police killed many Africans in the city's musseques (shanty towns).
  • The north, 15 March 1961: an uprising in the north, linked to the UPA (later FNLA), killed some 1,000 white settlers and many Africans, including contract workers from the south. Portugal's counter-insurgency campaign that followed destroyed villages and killed many thousands of Africans.
  • War of independence: the fighting became a thirteen-year war between Portugal and three rival nationalist movements (the MPLA, FNLA and UNITA). Forced cotton cultivation was abolished in 1961, and the indigenato system in 1961–62.
  • Independence and civil war: after the 1974 Carnation Revolution in Portugal, Angola became independent in November 1975 — but the rival movements were already fighting a civil war that lasted, with interruptions, until 2002.
05

Why does it matter?

The Baixa de Cassanje revolt is remembered in Angola as the first great act of resistance in the struggle that led to independence. It began as a protest by farmers against forced cotton cultivation, and showed how economic exploitation lay at the heart of colonial rule.

  • Its brutal suppression helped to convince Angolans that independence could only be won by force.
  • It drew international attention to Portugal's colonial policies at the United Nations.
  • 4 January is a national holiday in Angola: the Day of the Martyrs of Colonial Repression.

What do others say?How it was seen at the time, and where historians disagree

At the time

The Portuguese authorities claimed that the revolt was the work of foreign agitators from the Congo and of a millenarian sect. Angolan nationalists presented it as a protest against forced labour that was answered with massacre.

Historians' debates

  • How many died? Estimates vary from a few hundred to 10,000; Portuguese records are incomplete and the figure remains disputed.
  • Religion and politics: some historians emphasise the influence of prophetic religious movements from the Congo; others stress the economic grievances over cotton prices and forced labour.
  • The violence that followed: historians of the 1961 uprisings document atrocities by all sides: the killing of settler families and southern contract workers in the north in March 1961, and the much larger number of Africans killed by the Portuguese army, air force and settler militias.
How do we know?The evidence, and a source from the time
  • Portuguese military and administrative records.
  • Reports by Protestant missionaries, which reached the international press.
  • Oral histories collected in Malanje and among refugees.
How is it remembered?Memorials, anniversaries and arguments over memory
  • 4 January is commemorated each year in Angola as the Day of the Martyrs of Colonial Repression.
  • Memorials to the victims have been erected in Malanje province.
