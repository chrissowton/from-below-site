The Gdańsk Shipyard Strike and Solidarity

A strike over the sacking of a crane operator grew into the first independent trade union in the Soviet bloc, with ten million members.

When
14 – 31 August 1980
Where
Lenin Shipyard, Gdańsk, Poland
Facts checked
2026-11-15

See it on the map →

01

What is the background to this event?

After the Second World War Poland became a communist state under the influence of the Soviet Union. The ruling Polish United Workers' Party claimed to govern in the name of the working class, but independent trade unions, strikes and free speech were not permitted.

Polish workers had protested several times before:

  • In December 1970, sudden food price rises provoked strikes in the shipyards of the Baltic coast. Troops and police fired on workers in Gdańsk, Gdynia and Szczecin, killing dozens of people.
  • In 1976 strikes at Radom and Ursus were again met with repression. Dissident intellectuals formed the Workers' Defence Committee (KOR) to support persecuted workers and their families.
  • By the late 1970s Poland's economy, fuelled by Western loans, was in deep crisis, with shortages of food and goods.

In 1978 Karol Wojtyła, Archbishop of Kraków, became Pope John Paul II. His visit to Poland in 1979 drew millions of people and gave many a new sense of collective strength.

In their own words

03

What happened?

In July 1980 the government raised food prices, triggering a wave of strikes across Poland.2 On 14 August, workers at the Lenin Shipyard in Gdańsk went on strike after the sacking of Anna Walentynowicz, five months before she was due to retire.2 Lech Wałęsa, who had himself been dismissed, joined the strikers — by his own account, by climbing over the shipyard wall — and was elected to lead the strike.2

On 16 August, the third day, management agreed to the shipyard workers' own demands on pay and the reinstatement of Walentynowicz and Wałęsa. Wałęsa and others announced the end of the strike — but Anna Walentynowicz and Alina Pienkowska persuaded workers to stay and continue in solidarity with other striking workplaces, transforming a local dispute into a regional movement.2

An Inter-Enterprise Strike Committee, chaired by Wałęsa, represented hundreds of workplaces and issued 21 demands to the government, including:23

  • The right to form free trade unions, independent of the Communist Party.
  • The right to strike.
  • Freedom of expression and an end to censorship.
  • The release of political prisoners.
  • A memorial to the workers killed in December 1970.

Thousands of Gdańsk residents gathered at the shipyard gates, bringing food and flowers; priests celebrated mass inside the shipyard. With strikes spreading across the country, the government gave way. On 31 August 1980, Wałęsa and Deputy Prime Minister Mieczysław Jagielski signed the Gdańsk Agreement, using a large souvenir pen bearing the image of Pope John Paul II.2 The agreement stated that the new unions would be "intended to defend the social and material interests of the workers, and not to play the role of a political party".2

04

What happened next?

  • A mass movement: the independent union Solidarity (Solidarność) was founded in September 1980 and within months claimed around ten million members — about a quarter of Poland's population.
  • Martial law: on 13 December 1981 General Wojciech Jaruzelski declared martial law. Solidarity was banned, thousands of activists were interned, and the movement was driven underground.
  • Victory: new strikes in 1988 forced the government into Round Table talks. In partially free elections in June 1989, Solidarity candidates won almost every seat they were allowed to contest, and Poland's first non-communist prime minister in the Soviet bloc took office. Within months, communist regimes across Eastern Europe had fallen.
  • Afterwards: Wałęsa became President in 1990. The rapid market reforms of the 1990s, introduced by Solidarity-led governments, caused high unemployment among the very shipyard and industrial workers who had built the movement.
05

What do others say?

At the time

The Polish government and the Soviet leadership portrayed the strikers as tools of anti-socialist forces and Western interests, and feared that the movement threatened the entire communist system. Soviet leaders put heavy pressure on the Polish government to suppress Solidarity.

Historians' debates

  • Workers or intellectuals? Some historians emphasise the role of dissident intellectuals in KOR; others, such as Lawrence Goodwyn, argue that the workers themselves developed Solidarity's strategy and ideas.
  • The Church and the Pope: historians debate how decisive the Catholic Church and John Paul II were in the movement's success.
  • Lech Wałęsa: documents released in 2016 by Poland's Institute of National Remembrance suggested that Wałęsa had collaborated with the secret police under the codename "Bolek" in the early 1970s. Wałęsa has denied this; the issue remains politically divisive.
  • The women of the strike: recent historians have argued that the roles of Walentynowicz, Pienkowska and other women were long underplayed in favour of Wałęsa.
  • The legacy: some see Solidarity's later embrace of free-market reform as a betrayal of the workers who created it.
06

The evidence

  • The original 21 demands, written on wooden boards and hung at the shipyard gate, now preserved and inscribed on UNESCO's Memory of the World Register.
  • Recordings of the negotiations, which were broadcast over loudspeakers to the strikers.
  • Photographs, films and leaflets from the strike.
  • Secret police and Communist Party archives, opened after 1989.

07

How is it remembered?

  • The Monument to the Fallen Shipyard Workers of 1970, demanded by the strikers, was unveiled in December 1980 outside the shipyard gate.
  • The European Solidarity Centre, a museum and library, opened beside the shipyard in 2014.
  • Anna Walentynowicz died in the Smolensk air disaster of 2010, which also killed the Polish president.
  • Andrzej Wajda's film Man of Iron (1981) and Wałęsa: Man of Hope (2013) dramatised the movement.
08

Why does it matter?

The Gdańsk strike created the first independent, mass trade union in the Soviet bloc and began the process that led to the fall of communism in Eastern Europe. It showed that a workers' movement could challenge a state that claimed to rule on the workers' behalf.

  • The decision of Walentynowicz and Pienkowska to continue the strike in solidarity with others turned a local victory into a national movement.
  • The 21 demands linked workers' economic grievances with civil liberties such as free speech and freedom of religion.
  • Solidarity's emphasis on non-violent, disciplined mass action influenced democracy movements elsewhere.

Open questions

  1. Why was the decision to continue the strike after 16 August so important?
  2. Why might a communist government find independent trade unions so threatening?
  3. How should we weigh the role of individual leaders like Wałęsa against that of ordinary workers?
  4. Did Solidarity's later economic policies betray the workers who created it?
