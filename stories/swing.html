The Swing Riots

Farm labourers across southern England smashed the threshing machines that took their winter work, and sent threatening letters signed by a mythical 'Captain Swing'. The state answered with hangings and transportation.

When
Summer – December 1830
Where
Elham Valley, Kent, and southern England
Facts checked

01

What is the background to this event?

Between 1770 and 1830, about six million acres of common land in England were enclosed.1

  • Farmworkers lost the land where they had grazed animals and grown food, and depended entirely on wages.1
  • After 1815, grain prices collapsed and there were too many workers for the work available.1
  • Threshing machines took away the winter work of hand threshing.1
  • Labourers also resented the tithes paid to the Church of England and the power of the Poor Law guardians.1

In their own words

03

What happened?

In the summer of 1830, labourers in the Elham Valley in East Kent began breaking threshing machines.1

  • By early December, the riots had spread across southern England and East Anglia.1
  • Protesters demanded higher wages, broke machines, burned hayricks and attacked workhouses and tithe barns.1
  • Farmers, magistrates and parsons received threatening letters signed "Swing".1
  • It was the largest movement of social unrest in nineteenth-century England.1

04

What happened next?

  • Punishment: nearly 2,000 protesters were tried; 252 were sentenced to death, 19 were hanged, 644 were imprisoned and 481 were transported to Australia.1
  • New Poor Law: the government went on to reform poor relief in 1834, introducing the harsh workhouse system.
  • Tolpuddle: four years later, Dorset farmworkers who formed a union were transported.
05

What do others say?

At the time

The authorities feared revolution; some politicians admitted that labourers lived in terrible poverty.1

Debates since

  • Causes: historians such as J. F. C. Harrison see fifty years of impoverishment as the main cause; a 2020 study found rioting was worst where threshing machines were common and other jobs were scarce.1
  • Violence: the protesters destroyed property, burned ricks and maimed animals, but in Hobsbawm and Rudé's account killed no one; the state hanged 19 of them.12

07

How is it remembered?

  • Hobsbawm and Rudé's Captain Swing (1969) made the riots a classic of history from below.
08

Why does it matter?

The Swing Riots were a revolt of the rural poor against machines, low wages and a system that had taken their land.

  • They show how new technology can hit workers hardest.

Questions to think about

  1. Why did labourers target threshing machines?
  2. Why did the protesters invent 'Captain Swing'?
  3. Were the punishments fair?
  4. What do the Swing Riots tell us about the effects of new technology on workers?
09

What other events are like this?

  • 1834The Tolpuddle Martyrs — Six farm labourers formed a society to resist wage cuts and were sentenced to transportation to Australia.
  • 1549Kett's Rebellion — Villagers tearing down a landlord's fences asked a farmer to join them. He pulled down his own fences and led 16,000 people to camp outside Norwich, where they ran their own affairs for six weeks.
  • 1844The Silesian Weavers' Uprising — Hand weavers starving on falling piece rates marched on the mansions of the merchants who paid them. Prussian troops shot eleven of them dead, and the rising became a symbol for poets, playwrights and Karl Marx.
