Anti-colonial & freedom

The Cabanagem

The poorest people of the Amazon — Indigenous people, freed slaves and mixed-race river dwellers who lived in huts called cabanas — seized the capital of Pará and held it for months.

When
1835 – 1840
Where
Belém, Pará, Brazil
Facts checked
2026-11-16

See it on the map →

01

What is the background to this event?

After Brazilian independence in 1822, the province of Grão-Pará was ruled by governors appointed by the emperor, and local leaders were pushed aside.1

  • Most of its people were very poor.1
  • In 1833, the province had about 120,000 people: about 33,000 Indigenous people, 30,000 enslaved Black people, 42,000 people of mixed race and 15,000 white people.1
  • "Cabanagem" comes from the huts of the poorest people along the rivers.1
  • In 1834, the provincial president launched a wave of repression against political opponents.1

03

What happened?

On 6 January 1835, rebels captured Belém and killed the provincial president and the army commander.1

  • Malcher became president, but tried to keep the province loyal to the empire. Rival rebels killed him.1
  • Imperial forces retook the city, but the rebels attacked again and recaptured it in August 1835, after nine days of fighting.1
  • Eduardo Angelim became president, and the rebels held the province for ten months.1
  • In 1836, the empire sent a new commander, mercenaries and a naval blockade. The rebels left Belém and Angelim was captured.1
  • Resistance continued in the interior until an amnesty in 1839; the last group surrendered in 1840.1
04

What happened next?

  • Death toll: it is estimated that the population of Pará fell from about 100,000 to 60,000 during the conflict.1
  • Brazil: the empire went on to suppress other regional revolts of the 1830s and 1840s.
05

What do others say?

At the time

The imperial government saw the rebels as a threat to the unity of Brazil and to the social order.1

Debates since

  • Separatism or social revolution? Some stress the regional elite's demands; others, such as Mark Harris, stress the role of the poor, Indigenous and Black people.2
  • Violence on all sides: rebels killed officials and rivals, including their own first president; the imperial repression, with mercenaries and naval bombardment, caused most of the deaths.12

07

How is it remembered?

  • The Memorial da Cabanagem, designed by Oscar Niemeyer, stands at the entrance to Belém, inaugurated on the 150th anniversary in 1985.1
08

Why does it matter?

The Cabanagem was one of the few times in Brazilian history when poor and non-white people took power in a whole province.

  • It was also one of the bloodiest conflicts in Brazil's history.

  • 1605Palmares: The Free Kingdom in the Hills — For almost a century, people who had escaped slavery built a free society in the hills of north-east Brazil and resisted repeated attempts to destroy it.
  • 1835The Malê Revolt — During Ramadan, enslaved and freed African Muslims rose up in Salvador — the most significant urban revolt by enslaved people in the history of the Americas.
