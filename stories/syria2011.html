The Syrian Revolution: From Daraa to Homs

When schoolboys in Daraa were arrested for anti-government graffiti, peaceful protests spread across Syria. The government answered with live fire, sieges and mass arrests — and the uprising turned into a civil war.

When
March – July 2011
Where
Daraa, Homs, Hama, Damascus and towns across Syria

01

What is the background to this event?

From 1971, Syria was ruled as a military dictatorship by Hafez al-Assad, and from 2000 by his son Bashar.1

  • An uprising in 1982 was crushed in the Hama massacre, in which over 40,000 civilians were killed.1
  • A brief period of debate after Bashar took power, the 'Damascus Spring', ended in arrests by late 2001.1
  • Syria had been under an emergency law for 48 years.1 In 2010, Reporters Without Borders ranked it among the worst countries in the world for press freedom.1
  • In early 2011, uprisings in Tunisia and Egypt inspired calls for protest in Syria.1

In their own words

03

What happened?

In early March 2011, security forces in Daraa arrested and tortured 15 young students for writing anti-government graffiti, including the Arab Spring slogan "The people want the fall of the regime".1

  • Protests began in Daraa. According to interviewees cited by Human Rights Watch, demonstrators carried olive branches, opened their shirts to show they were unarmed, and chanted "peaceful, peaceful".1
  • On 15 March, hundreds marched in Damascus; on 18 March, the 'Friday of Dignity', protests broke out in several cities. In Daraa, security forces opened fire on protesters at the Omari Mosque, killing four.1
  • On 20 March, a crowd in Daraa burned the Ba'ath Party headquarters; in two days of fighting, seven police officers and fifteen protesters were killed.1
  • On 25 March, protests spread nationwide after Friday prayers, and more than 70 protesters were reported killed. Crowds chanted, "We do not want your bread, we want dignity".1
  • On 30 March, Assad called the protests a "foreign plot". From April, the army besieged towns, including Homs, and tens of thousands of people were arrested.1
  • Soldiers who refused to fire began to defect. On 29 July 2011, the Free Syrian Army was formed, marking the start of armed rebellion.1
04

What happened next?

  • Civil war: as opposition militias captured territory, the United Nations declared the conflict a civil war in June 2012. The war caused hundreds of thousands of deaths.1
  • Crimes against humanity: in August 2011, a UN Human Rights Council report said the government's atrocities amounted to crimes against humanity.1
  • The fall of Assad: in December 2024, after a series of rebel offensives, Assad fled to Moscow and his government fell.1
05

What do others say?

At the time

Protesters called for freedom, dignity and an end to emergency rule. The government called the protests a foreign conspiracy, and some Syrians rallied in support of Assad.1

Debates since

  • Peaceful or armed? Historians debate when and why the uprising became armed; the government's violence and the defections of soldiers who refused to fire both played a part.1
  • Violence: in the first months most of the dead were protesters killed by security forces, though police and soldiers were also killed. The war that followed killed hundreds of thousands.1
06

The evidence

  • Videos filmed by protesters on mobile phones, reports by Human Rights Watch and the UN, and the testimony of defectors and detainees.1

07

How is it remembered?

  • Daraa became known as the "Cradle of the Syrian Revolution".1
08

Why does it matter?

The Syrian uprising began as one of the peaceful protest movements of the Arab Spring. The government's violent response helped turn it into one of the deadliest wars of the century.1

Open questions

  1. Why did the arrest of a group of schoolboys spark a national uprising?

  • 2011Tahrir Square and the Egyptian Revolution — Weeks after Tunisia's president fell, young Egyptians called a protest on National Police Day. Millions followed, Tahrir Square became a city within a city, and after eighteen days Hosni Mubarak resigned after thirty years in power.
  • 1925The Great Syrian Revolt — Druze villagers, Damascus craftsmen and peasants from across Syria rose against French rule. France answered by shelling Damascus.
  • 2010The Tunisian Revolution — A street vendor's desperate protest in a small town sparked a month of uprising that toppled a dictator of 23 years and began the Arab Spring.
