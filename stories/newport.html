The vote & democracy

The Chartists and the Newport Rising

Millions of working people signed the People's Charter demanding the vote. In Newport, thousands of miners and ironworkers marched — and soldiers opened fire.

When
1838 – 1848 (Newport Rising: 4 November 1839)
Where
Newport, Monmouthshire, Wales
Facts checked

01

What is the background to this event?

In the 1830s, only a minority of men in Britain could vote — roughly one in five adult men in England and Wales after the Reform Act of 1832, which excluded almost all working people. The new industrial towns were growing rapidly, conditions in mines, ironworks and mills were harsh, and the New Poor Law of 1834 forced the destitute into workhouses.

In 1838, the London Working Men's Association published the People's Charter, which demanded six reforms:2

  • a vote for every man aged twenty-one and over;
  • a secret ballot;
  • no property qualification for Members of Parliament;
  • payment of MPs, so that working men could afford to serve;
  • equal constituencies;
  • annual elections for Parliament.

Chartism became a mass national movement, with its own newspapers — the Northern Star was the best-selling provincial newspaper in Britain in 1839 — and huge open-air meetings. Its first great petition was rejected by the House of Commons in July 1839.2 In south-east Wales, among the coal miners and ironworkers of the valleys, Chartism was especially strong; the arrest and imprisonment of the popular speaker Henry Vincent in Monmouth inflamed feelings.1

02

Who were the key people?

  • John Frost

    A draper and former mayor of Newport, who led the march. He was sentenced to death for treason; the sentence was commuted to transportation to Van Diemen's Land (Tasmania).

  • Zephaniah Williams and William Jones

    A mineral agent and a watchmaker who led columns from Blackwood and Pontypool, and shared Frost's sentence.

  • Henry Vincent

    A Chartist orator whose imprisonment in Monmouth gaol helped to provoke the rising.

  • Thomas Phillips

    The mayor of Newport, who was wounded at the Westgate Hotel.

  • The marchers

    Several thousand miners and ironworkers from the valleys of Monmouthshire, some armed with pikes, clubs and guns.

03

What happened?

Over the weekend of 2–3 November 1839, Chartists gathered in the mining valleys north of Newport. On the night of 3–4 November, around 4,000 people marched through heavy rain in three columns led by John Frost, Zephaniah Williams and William Jones. Some of the miners carried home-made pikes, clubs and firearms; one whole chapel congregation is said to have joined the march.1

The authorities had been warned. The mayor had sworn in special constables and placed 32 soldiers of the 45th Regiment of Foot in the Westgate Hotel, where Chartists had been held prisoner.1 At about 9.30 a.m. on Monday 4 November, the marchers arrived in front of the hotel:1

  • Shots were exchanged, and the marchers tried to force their way into the hotel.
  • The soldiers, firing from the windows, opened fire on the crowd. In about twenty-five minutes the rising was over.
  • Between 10 and 24 Chartists were killed and perhaps 50 more were wounded. Four soldiers and the mayor of Newport were wounded.

Frost, Williams and Jones were arrested, tried for high treason, and sentenced to be hanged, drawn and quartered. After a national campaign, the sentences were commuted to transportation for life.1

04

What happened next?

  • More petitions: the Chartists presented a second petition in 1842, with over three million signatures, during a wave of strikes (the "Plug Plot" riots). It too was rejected.
  • 1848: a third petition was presented after a huge meeting on Kennington Common in London on 10 April 1848. The government prepared for revolution, but the meeting passed off peacefully. Parliament dismissed the petition, claiming that many signatures were false.
  • Exile and return: Frost was pardoned in 1854 and returned to Britain in 1856.
  • The Charter achieved: the movement faded after 1848, but five of its six points eventually became law: the secret ballot in 1872, the abolition of property qualifications in 1858, payment of MPs in 1911, and votes for all men — and women — over 21 in 1918 and 1928. Only annual parliaments were never adopted.
05

What do others say?

At the time

The government and most of the press presented the Newport Rising as a treasonous insurrection, and used it to justify repression of the whole Chartist movement. Chartists themselves were divided between "moral force" supporters, who believed in peaceful persuasion, and "physical force" supporters, who argued that the authorities would only give way to the threat of violence.

Historians' debates

  • What was the plan? Historians such as David J. V. Jones argue that the march was intended as the start of a wider rising across Britain; others see it as a mass demonstration to free prisoners that turned violent. Frost's own intentions remain unclear.
  • Who fired first? Accounts disagree about whether the Chartists or the soldiers fired first at the Westgate.
  • Why did Chartism fail? Historians debate whether the movement was defeated by repression, divided by its own leaders, or weakened by rising living standards after the 1840s.
06

The evidence

  • The trial records of Frost, Williams and Jones.
  • Chartist newspapers, especially the Northern Star, and the petitions themselves.
  • Bullet holes in the pillars of the Westgate Hotel, which can still be seen.
  • William Edward Kilburn's photographs of the Kennington Common meeting in 1848, among the earliest photographs of a crowd.

07

How is it remembered?

  • The Westgate Hotel still stands in Newport. A large mosaic mural commemorating the rising, made in 1978, was demolished in 2013 to make way for a shopping development, provoking protests.
  • The Chartists killed at Newport were buried in unmarked graves at St Woolos; a memorial stone now marks the site.
  • Events in Newport commemorate the rising each November.
08

Why does it matter?

Chartism was the first mass working-class political movement in the world. Millions of people who had no vote organised, petitioned and demonstrated for democracy, and almost all of their demands later became the foundations of British democracy.

  • The Newport Rising was the last large-scale armed rising in Britain against the state.
  • Chartism shaped later movements for the vote, including the campaign for women's suffrage.
  • It showed working people creating their own newspapers, schools, chapels and organisations.

Questions to think about

  2. Was the Newport Rising a planned insurrection or a protest that went wrong?
  3. Is it ever justified to use force to win the right to vote?
  4. Why did it take so long for the Chartists' demands to become law?

What other events are like this?

  • 1819The Peterloo Massacre — Tens of thousands of working people gathered peacefully to demand the vote. Within ten minutes, cavalry had cleared the field with sabres.
  • 1834The Tolpuddle Martyrs — Six farm labourers formed a society to resist wage cuts and were sentenced to transportation to Australia.
  • 1647The Putney Debates — Ordinary soldiers and their elected representatives argued with Cromwell and the army's generals about who should have the right to vote.
