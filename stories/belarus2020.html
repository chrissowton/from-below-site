The Belarusian Protests of 2020

When Alexander Lukashenko claimed 80 per cent of the vote after 26 years in power, Belarusians came out in the largest protests in their history. Three women led the challenge. Tens of thousands were arrested.

When
August 2020 – March 2021
Where
Minsk and across Belarus
Facts checked

01

What is the background to this event?

Alexander Lukashenko had ruled Belarus since 1994. In 2020 he sought a sixth term.1

  • Leading opposition candidates were arrested or barred, including the blogger Siarhei Tsikhanouski and the banker Viktar Babaryka.1
  • Tsikhanouski's wife, Sviatlana Tsikhanouskaya, a former teacher, registered as a candidate in his place.1
  • She was joined by Maria Kalesnikava, from Babaryka's campaign, and Veronika Tsepkalo, wife of another barred candidate.1
02

Who were the key people?

  • Sviatlana Tsikhanouskaya

    The opposition candidate, forced into exile in Lithuania.

  • Maria Kalesnikava

    A musician and campaign leader who tore up her passport rather than be deported, and was imprisoned.

  • Veronika Tsepkalo

    The third of the women who led the campaign.

  • Alexander Taraikovsky and Raman Bandarenka

    Protesters who died in August and November 2020.

  • Alexander Lukashenko

    President of Belarus since 1994.

In their own words

03

What happened?

On 9 August 2020, officials announced that Lukashenko had won about 80 per cent of the vote. Tsikhanouskaya rejected the result as falsified.1

  • Protests broke out that night across the country. Riot police used stun grenades, rubber bullets and beatings; thousands were detained in the first days.1
  • On 10 August, a protester, Alexander Taraikovsky, died in Minsk.1
  • The United Nations cited more than 450 documented cases of torture and ill-treatment of detainees, including reports of sexual abuse.1
  • Women dressed in white formed human chains with flowers. Workers at state factories went on strike.
  • On 16 August, the largest demonstration in Belarusian history filled central Minsk.1
  • Tsikhanouskaya, forced to leave for Lithuania, set up a Coordination Council.1
  • Sunday marches continued for months, despite mass arrests.

The protests were overwhelmingly peaceful. The authorities said some police officers were injured in the first nights. Several protesters died, and by the end of 2020 the human rights centre Viasna had recorded 1,000 testimonies of torture.12

04

What happened next?

  • Repression: more than 30,000 people were detained. Independent media and hundreds of organisations were closed, and many thousands of people left the country.2
  • Prison: Maria Kalesnikava was sentenced to 11 years in 2021. Ales Bialiatski, founder of Viasna, was jailed and shared the Nobel Peace Prize in 2022.
  • Releases: some prominent prisoners, including Tsikhanouski, Kalesnikava and Bialiatski, were freed and sent abroad in 2025 after negotiations with the United States, while many others remained in prison.
  • Lukashenko stayed in power, more dependent than before on Russia.
05

What do others say?

At the time

Lukashenko said the protests were directed from abroad. The European Union and the United States refused to recognise the result; Russia backed him.1

Debates since

  • Non-violence: some argue that strict non-violence limited the movement's power; others that violence would have brought a bloodbath or Russian intervention.
  • The vote: no independent count exists; Tsikhanouskaya claimed 60–70 per cent.1

07

How is it remembered?

  • The white-red-white flag became the symbol of the protests, and is now treated as extremist by the government.
  • Belarusians in exile mark 9 August each year.
08

Why does it matter?

The protests showed a society that had changed: peaceful, self-organised and led in large part by women — and a state willing to use mass violence to survive.

  • They raise the question of what peaceful protest can achieve against a government that will not yield.

Questions to think about

  1. Why did three women come to lead the opposition?
  2. Why did the protesters insist on non-violence?
  3. Why did the protests not bring down the government?
  4. Were the protests a failure?

What other events are like this?

  • 1980The Gdańsk Shipyard Strike and Solidarity — A strike over the sacking of a crane operator grew into the first independent trade union in the Soviet bloc, with ten million members.
  • 1989The Baltic Way — Around two million people joined hands in a human chain 675 kilometres long, across Estonia, Latvia and Lithuania, to demand freedom from the Soviet Union.
  • 1956The Hungarian Revolution of 1956 — Students, workers and soldiers rose against Hungary's Soviet-backed dictatorship and pulled down Stalin's statue. For twelve days they won — until Soviet tanks returned.
