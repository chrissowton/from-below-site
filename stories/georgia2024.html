Georgia's Nightly Protests for Europe

After a disputed election and the government's decision to suspend talks on joining the European Union, Georgians gathered outside parliament every night. Their protest has continued for more than six hundred days, in the face of arrests, new laws and prison sentences.

Ongoing situationLast reviewed 16 November 2026

Daily protests were still taking place outside parliament at the time of writing. The Constitutional Court was hearing Georgian Dream's case to ban five opposition parties, which its authors hope to complete by January 2027.6

Still unresolved:

  • The Constitutional Court's ruling on banning opposition parties
  • The fate of protesters and journalists in prison
  • Georgia's relationship with the EU

This is recent history. Accounts, casualty figures and outcomes may change as more evidence emerges.

01

What is the background to this event?

Georgia applied to join the European Union in March 2022 and became a candidate country in December 2023. In a 2023 survey, 85% of Georgians said they supported joining.8

  • The ruling Georgian Dream party, founded by the billionaire Bidzina Ivanishvili, had governed since 2012.1
  • In the parliamentary election of 26 October 2024, Georgian Dream was declared the winner with about 54% of the vote. The opposition and President Salome Zourabichvili said the election had been rigged, and observers reported intimidation and other violations.1

03

What happened?

Protests against the election results began on 28 October 2024.1

  • On 28 November, Prime Minister Kobakhidze announced that Georgia would not pursue EU accession talks until the end of 2028. That night, large protests began in Tbilisi and other cities.1
  • Police used water cannon and tear gas, and many protesters and journalists were beaten. The Public Defender of Georgia found that injuries deliberately inflicted on protesters amounted to torture. The government said that 113 police officers had been injured and blamed the protesters for the violence.1
  • In December 2024, an electoral college controlled by Georgian Dream chose Mikheil Kavelashvili as president. Zourabichvili left the presidential palace but insisted she remained the only legitimate president.1
  • From then on, protesters gathered every evening on Rustaveli Avenue, blocking the road for 343 consecutive days, until November 2025.2
  • The government passed new laws restricting protest, and courts sentenced opposition politicians, activists and journalists. The journalist Mzia Amaglobeli received a two-year prison sentence; in March 2026 the Supreme Court refused to hear her appeal.34
  • On 4 October 2025, the day of local elections, part of the opposition tried to storm the presidential palace and was stopped by security forces.1

Georgian Dream says the protests are funded from abroad and aimed at overthrowing the government. Western governments have sanctioned many of its officials, and the European Parliament has refused to recognise the 2024 elections.13

04

What happened next?

  • The protest continues: by mid-2026, protesters had gathered outside parliament for more than 600 consecutive days, though in much smaller numbers than at the start.7
  • Party bans: Georgian Dream asked the Constitutional Court to ban the main opposition parties, and hearings began in October 2026.56
05

What do others say?

At the time

Protesters saw a stolen election and a turn away from Europe towards Russia. Georgian Dream said it was defending Georgia's sovereignty and peace against foreign interference and a planned revolution.1

Debates since

  • The election: international observers reported serious violations but did not declare the result fraudulent as a whole; the opposition says it was stolen.1
  • Tactics: the attempt to storm the presidential palace on 4 October 2025 divided the movement, and was used by the government to justify a crackdown.1

How is it remembered?

  • Protesters mark each day of the protest by number, and the anniversaries of 28 November with mass rallies.2
08

Why does it matter?

The Georgian protests show the persistence of a movement that has kept going for years without winning, and the tools a government can use to wear down protest without mass killing: arrests, fines, new laws and prosecutions.

Questions to think about

  1. Why did the EU decision bring so many people onto the streets?
  2. How can a protest movement keep going for years?
  3. Why might a government prefer arrests and fines to mass violence?
  4. Is banning political parties ever compatible with democracy?

  • 2003The Rose Revolution — After a rigged election, Georgians filled the streets for three weeks. Protesters carrying roses entered parliament, and the president resigned without a shot being fired.
  • 2024Serbia's Student Revolt after Novi Sad — When a newly renovated station canopy collapsed in Novi Sad and killed sixteen people, students blamed corruption. Their blockades and silent vigils grew into the largest protests in Serbia's history and, two years later, an election that removed the ruling party.
  • 2020The Belarusian Protests of 2020 — When Alexander Lukashenko claimed 80 per cent of the vote after 26 years in power, Belarusians came out in the largest protests in their history. Three women led the challenge. Tens of thousands were arrested.
