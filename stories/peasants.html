The Peasants' Revolt

Tens of thousands of villagers and townspeople marched on London to demand an end to serfdom. For a few days, the government of England was at their mercy.

When
May – June 1381
Where
Essex, Kent and London, England
Facts checked

01

What is the background to this event?

The Black Death of 1348–49 killed perhaps a third or more of England's population. With fewer people to work the land, surviving labourers could demand higher wages and better terms. Landowners and the Crown responded by trying to hold wages down and keep peasants tied to the land:

  • The Ordinance of Labourers (1349) and the Statute of Labourers (1351) fixed wages at pre-plague levels and restricted workers' freedom to move in search of better pay.
  • Many peasants were still villeins (serfs), legally bound to their lord's manor, owing him labour services and unable to leave without permission.
  • The long and costly war with France required heavy taxation.

Between 1377 and 1380 Parliament imposed three poll taxes. The third, in 1380, was a flat rate of twelve pence on every person over the age of fifteen — three times the first — falling hardest on the poor. Many people evaded it by refusing to register, and in March 1381 the government sent commissioners to investigate. Radical preachers such as John Ball had for years been attacking the wealth of the Church and the injustice of social inequality.

In their own words

03

What happened?

At the end of May 1381, an attempt by the tax commissioner John Bampton to collect unpaid poll tax at Brentwood in Essex ended in violent confrontation. The rising spread rapidly through Essex and Kent.1 Rebels attacked manor houses and burned records of serfdom and taxation. In Kent, they freed John Ball from prison in Maidstone and chose Wat Tyler as their leader.

Ball's preaching gave the rebellion its most famous words:

"When Adam delved and Eve span, who was then a gentleman?"John Ball, as recorded by contemporary chroniclers, 13811

The rebels declared their loyalty to "King Richard and the true commons of England", blaming the king's councillors rather than the king himself.1 On 13 June they entered London, where many townspeople joined them:1

  • They destroyed the Savoy Palace, home of the hated John of Gaunt, the king's uncle. The rebels declared themselves "zealots for truth and justice, not thieves and robbers", and almost nothing was stolen.
  • They opened prisons and burned legal records at the Temple.
  • Some rebels also attacked Flemish immigrants, killing dozens of people.

On 14 June Richard II met the rebels at Mile End and agreed to most of their demands, including the abolition of serfdom. The same day, rebels entered the Tower of London and killed Archbishop Sudbury and Treasurer Hales.1 On 15 June the king met Wat Tyler and the rebels at Smithfield. During the meeting, Mayor William Walworth attacked Tyler, who was then killed. Richard rode forward to calm the crowd while Walworth gathered a militia from the city, and the rebels were dispersed.1

04

What happened next?

  • Repression: the king revoked the charters he had granted. According to the chronicler Thomas Walsingham, he told rebels from Essex: "Rustics you were and rustics you are still; you will remain in bondage, not as before, but incomparably harsher."
  • Executions: royal judges toured the rebel counties. John Ball was hanged, drawn and quartered at St Albans on 15 July 1381. By November, at least 1,500 rebels had been killed.
  • Longer-term change: no further poll tax was attempted for centuries, and over the following decades serfdom declined across England, driven by labour shortages and peasant resistance, until it had largely disappeared by the sixteenth century.
05

What do others say?

At the time

The chroniclers who recorded the revolt were almost all monks or clergy hostile to the rebels. They described them as a mindless, drunken mob and blamed Ball's heretical preaching. The poet John Gower portrayed the rebels as wild animals.

Historians' debates

  • Was it a "peasants'" revolt? The name was only applied in the nineteenth century. Many participants were artisans, townspeople and even minor local officials, not simply peasants.
  • Organised or spontaneous? Historians such as Rodney Hilton and Juliet Barker have emphasised the planning and coordination behind the rising, and the rebels' political aims.
  • What did they want? Historians debate how far the rebels sought to overturn the social order, and how far they wanted to restore what they saw as traditional rights.
  • Violence against immigrants: the killing of Flemish people in London is a reminder that popular movements can also turn on outsiders.
06

The evidence

  • Chronicles by Froissart, Walsingham, Knighton and the anonymous author of the Anonimalle Chronicle — all written by people hostile to the rebels.
  • Legal records of the trials that followed, which name thousands of individual participants and are valuable for "history from below".
  • Tax records, manorial court rolls and royal letters.
  • The rebels' own voices survive only in fragments, such as letters attributed to John Ball.

07

How is it remembered?

  • William Morris's A Dream of John Ball (1888) made the revolt a founding story for British socialism.
  • Opponents of the Community Charge, popularly known as the "poll tax", invoked 1381 in 1990.
  • Local commemorations are held, including at Fobbing in Essex, one of the villages where the rising began.
08

Why does it matter?

The Peasants' Revolt was the largest popular rising in medieval England. Ordinary people put forward a remarkably radical demand — the end of serfdom and of inequality based on birth — and for a few days held the capital and the king in their hands.

  • John Ball's question about Adam and Eve became one of the most quoted statements of human equality in English history.
  • The revolt showed the limits of what the Crown could impose through taxation, and no comparable poll tax was attempted until 1990.
  • It became a lasting symbol for later radicals, from William Morris to the opponents of the "poll tax" in the 1990s.

What other events are like this?

  • 1649The Diggers of St George's Hill — In the year the king was executed, a small group of poor men and women began to dig and plant common land, declaring the earth 'a common treasury for all'.
  • 1647The Putney Debates — Ordinary soldiers and their elected representatives argued with Cromwell and the army's generals about who should have the right to vote.
  • 1834The Tolpuddle Martyrs — Six farm labourers formed a society to resist wage cuts and were sentenced to transportation to Australia.
