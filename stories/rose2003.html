The vote & democracy

The Rose Revolution

After a rigged election, Georgians filled the streets for three weeks. Protesters carrying roses entered parliament, and the president resigned without a shot being fired.

When
3 – 23 November 2003
Where
Rustaveli Avenue, Tbilisi, Georgia
Facts checked

01

What is the background to this event?

Eduard Shevardnadze, a former Soviet foreign minister, had ruled Georgia since 1992.1

  • His government was seen as corrupt and ineffective.1
  • The parliamentary elections of 2 November 2003 were widely seen as rigged.1

03

What happened?

From 3 November, protests filled central Tbilisi and spread to most towns in Georgia.1

  • The youth group Kmara and civil society organisations took part in all the protests.1
  • An independent television channel played an important role.1
  • On 22 November, as the new parliament opened, protesters led by Saakashvili entered the chamber carrying red roses.1
  • On 23 November, Shevardnadze resigned.1

04

What happened next?

  • New government: new elections brought Saakashvili's movement to power.1
  • Reforms and criticism: the new government cut corruption sharply, but was later accused of authoritarian behaviour.12
05

What do others say?

At the time

Supporters called it a peaceful revolution; Shevardnadze's allies called it a coup.1

Debates since

  • From below? Western organisations funded civil society groups and election monitoring; critics, including Russia, saw foreign interference, while supporters stress that ordinary Georgians filled the streets.12
  • Violence: the revolution was bloodless.1

How is it remembered?

  • The Rose Revolution is remembered in Georgia with pride and with argument about what followed.
08

Why does it matter?

The Rose Revolution was one of the first of the non-violent "colour revolutions" that followed rigged elections.1
