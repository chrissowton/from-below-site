The Pueblo Revolt

Runners carrying knotted cords told more than two dozen Pueblo towns when to rise. Together they drove the Spanish out of New Mexico for twelve years — the most successful Indigenous revolt against Spain in North America.

When
August 1680
Where
Santa Fe and the Rio Grande pueblos, New Mexico, United States

See it on the map →

01

What is the background to this event?

From 1540, the Pueblo peoples of the Rio Grande faced waves of Spanish soldiers, missionaries and settlers.1

  • In 1599, after a Spanish attack on Acoma Pueblo, about 600 people were seized and enslaved, and many were mutilated as punishment.1
  • The Pueblos were forced to work for the colonists, and lost access to farmland and water.1
  • Franciscan missionaries outlawed kachina dances and burned sacred masks and objects.1
  • In 1675, 47 Pueblo religious leaders were arrested for "sorcery"; three were hanged and the rest whipped. One of them was Po'pay of Ohkay Owingeh.1
02

Who were the key people?

  • Po'pay (Popé)

    Tewa religious leader who planned and organised the revolt.

  • Pueblo leaders

    From more than two dozen towns speaking six languages.

  • Antonio de Otermín

    Spanish governor of New Mexico.

  • Diego de Vargas

    Who led the Spanish return in 1692.

03

What happened?

Po'pay spent five years at Taos Pueblo winning support among the 46 Pueblo towns.1

  • He sent runners with knotted cords: each morning a knot was untied, and the last knot was the signal to rise.1
  • The Spanish learned of the plan, so the revolt began early, on 10 August 1680.
  • About 400 people were killed, including men, women, children and 21 of the 33 Franciscan missionaries in New Mexico. Churches were destroyed.1
  • The Pueblos besieged Santa Fe and cut off its water. On 21 August, Governor Otermín led the surviving colonists, about 2,000, south to El Paso.1

04

What happened next?

  • Independence: the Pueblos were free of Spanish rule for twelve years.
  • Return: in 1692, Diego de Vargas retook Santa Fe. A second revolt in 1696 was crushed.1
  • Survival: afterwards, Spanish rule was less harsh in some ways, and Pueblo religion survived.
05

What do others say?

At the time

The Spanish saw the revolt as an attack on God and the Crown.1

Debates since

  • Religion and freedom: the revolt was both a political rising and a religious revival.12
  • Violence on both sides: the rebels killed about 400 colonists and missionaries; Spanish rule before it included massacre, enslavement and mutilation, and the reconquest brought further executions and enslavement.1
  • Unity: after the Spanish left, the Pueblo alliance divided.
06

The evidence

  • Spanish records, including testimony taken from Pueblo prisoners.2
  • Pueblo oral traditions.
07

How is it remembered?

  • In 2005, a statue of Po'pay by the Pueblo sculptor Cliff Fragua was placed in the US Capitol as one of New Mexico's two statues.1
08

Why does it matter?

The Pueblo Revolt was one of the most successful Indigenous uprisings in North American history.

  • Scholars see it as the first Native American movement to revive traditional religion.1

Questions to think about

  1. Why was religion at the heart of the revolt?
  2. How did the knotted cords help people speaking six languages act together?
  3. How should we judge the killing of priests and settlers?
  4. Why did the Spanish return, and what changed when they did?

What other events are like this?

  • 1780The Rebellion of Túpac Amaru II — An Indigenous leader and his wife, Micaela Bastidas, led the largest rebellion in Spain's American empire, demanding an end to forced labour and abuse.
  • 1598Curalaba and the Mapuche Uprising — A Mapuche ambush killed the Spanish governor of Chile and set off a general uprising that destroyed seven Spanish cities. The Mapuche kept their independence south of the Biobío River for nearly three centuries.
  • 1969The Occupation of Alcatraz — Calling themselves 'Indians of All Tribes', Native American students and activists occupied the abandoned prison island for nineteen months — and changed federal policy.
