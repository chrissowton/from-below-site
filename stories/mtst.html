Housing & community

Brazil's Homeless Workers' Movement (MTST)

In a country with millions of families in inadequate housing and millions of empty homes, the MTST occupies unused land and buildings to demand housing — sometimes in tent cities of thousands of families.

When
2002 – present
Where
São Paulo, Brazil
Facts checked
2026-11-16

See it on the map →

01

What is the background to this event?

Brazil's cities grew rapidly in the twentieth century, and millions of people live in favelas or overcrowded housing. Estimates of the housing shortage range from 6 to 8 million homes.1

  • At the same time, many buildings and plots stand empty.1
  • The MTST grew out of the Landless Rural Workers' Movement (MST), which occupies unused farmland.1
  • Its first occupation as an independent movement was in Guarulhos, São Paulo, in 2002.1

03

What happened?

The MTST organises occupations of unused land and buildings, especially plots designated for social housing that owe property taxes.1

  • Families build shacks of wood and plastic sheeting, organise kitchens and assemblies, and press for the land to be used for housing.1
  • The movement combines occupation with negotiation with city, state and federal governments.1
  • In 2014, as Brazil hosted the football World Cup, the MTST set up the "People's Cup" (Copa do Povo) occupation near the new stadium in São Paulo, and held large protests.2
  • The protests helped win commitments to build housing under the federal "My House, My Life" programme.12
  • Occupations have often faced eviction orders and police operations.1
04

What happened next?

  • Politics: Guilherme Boulos ran for president in 2018 and for mayor of São Paulo in 2020 and 2024, and was elected to Congress in 2022.1
  • Continuing occupations: the movement remains active in many Brazilian cities.
05

Why does it matter?

The MTST is one of the largest urban social movements in Latin America, raising questions about the right to housing and the use of empty property.

  • It links housing to the wider struggle against inequality.
  • Its leader Guilherme Boulos was elected to Congress in 2022.1
  • Occupations such as Nova Palestina in São Paulo became symbols of the housing struggle.

What do others say?How it was seen at the time, and where historians disagree

At the time

Landowners and critics describe occupations as illegal invasions of property; the MTST argues that Brazil's constitution requires property to fulfil a "social function".1

Debates since

  • Politics: critics argue the movement is too close to left-wing parties; supporters say electoral politics is needed to win housing.1
  • Violence: occupations are generally non-violent, but evictions have sometimes led to clashes with police.
