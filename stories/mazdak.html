Mazdak and the Mazdakite Movement

During a famine, a Persian priest told the king that God made the earth's resources for everyone to share. His followers opened the granaries — and for a time the king supported them. Then the crown prince had them massacred.

When
c. 488 – 528 AD
Where
Ctesiphon and the Sasanian Empire (Iran and Iraq)
Facts checked
2026-11-16

See it on the map →

01

What is the background to this event?

The Sasanian Empire ruled Iran and much of the Middle East from 224 to 651 AD. Society was rigidly divided into classes, with great nobles and Zoroastrian priests at the top.

  • At the end of the fifth century, the empire faced crisis: defeat in war, and droughts and locust swarms that caused widespread famine.1
  • Nobles held vast estates and large harems, while many poor men could not afford to marry.1
  • King Kavad I (reigned 488–531) was in conflict with the nobility and the priests.1
02

Who were the key people?

  • Mazdak

    A Zoroastrian priest and social reformer who preached equality. He was executed around 524 or 528.

  • King Kavad I

    Who supported Mazdak for a time, probably to weaken the nobles.

  • Khosrow I (Anushirvan)

    Kavad's son, who suppressed the movement.

  • The Mazdakites

    Followers, many of them poor peasants and townspeople.

03

What happened?

According to the Persian epic the Shahnameh, Mazdak rose to prominence during a famine. He told King Kavad that the wealthy who hoarded grain while the poor starved were sinful, and with the king's permission opened the royal granaries to the people.1

Mazdak preached that:1

  • God had created the earth's resources to be shared equally by all people;
  • inequality arose because the strong had seized from the weak;
  • wealth should be redistributed to end poverty.

Hostile sources also claim he preached the "sharing of women" — which modern scholars suggest may have been an attack on the nobles' harems, allowing poor men to marry.1

Kavad supported Mazdak for some years, probably to weaken the aristocracy.1 But the nobility and priests resisted. Around 524 or 528, Kavad's son Khosrow arranged a religious debate in which Mazdak was condemned, and Mazdak and his followers were massacred.1 According to later legend, they were buried alive, head down, in a garden.

04

What happened next?

  • Reforms: after crushing the movement, Khosrow I carried out reforms of taxation and land, partly to address the crisis that had produced it.
  • Survival: Mazdakite ideas survived and influenced later movements, such as the Khurramites, who rebelled against the Abbasid caliphs in the ninth century.1
  • Modern interest: in the twentieth century, Mazdak was celebrated by Iranian socialists as an early communist.
05

Why does it matter?

Mazdak's movement is one of the earliest recorded movements for social equality and the sharing of wealth. It shows that radical ideas are not only modern or Western.

  • It links religion with demands for social justice.
  • It shows how the powerful could adopt a radical movement — and then destroy it.
  • His ideas survived his execution and influenced later movements, such as the Khurramites who rebelled against the Abbasids.1

What do others say?How it was seen at the time, and where historians disagree

In the sources

Almost all our sources were written by Mazdak's enemies — Zoroastrian priests, Byzantine historians and later Muslim writers — who portrayed him as a dangerous heretic.

Historians' debates

  • Communist or religious reformer? Some historians see Mazdak as a proto-communist; others emphasise his religious ideas, rooted in an earlier Zoroastrian sect.
  • The "sharing of women": scholars debate whether this was a real teaching, a hostile distortion, or a challenge to aristocratic marriage customs.1
  • Violence: sources describe both Mazdakites seizing property from the rich and the brutal massacre of Mazdakites by Khosrow.
How do we know?The evidence, and a source from the time
  • The Shahnameh of Ferdowsi (completed around 1010).
  • Byzantine and Arabic historians, such as Procopius and al-Tabari.

'The Execution of Mazdak', from a fourteenth-century copy of the Shahnameh, Ferdowsi's epic of Iran's kings. Made eight centuries after the events, it shows how the story was told by his enemies' heirs: Mazdak's followers are planted upside down in a garden.

Reading it

This painting was made eight centuries after the events, for a copy of Iran's great epic of kings. It tells the story from the side of Mazdak's enemies' heirs: his followers are planted upside down in a garden. Almost everything we know about the movement comes through sources like this, which is why its real aims remain so uncertain.
