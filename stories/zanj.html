The Zanj Rebellion

Enslaved East Africans forced to dig salt-crusted marshland in southern Iraq rose against the Abbasid Caliphate, built their own state and held out for fourteen years.

When
869 – 883
Where
Basra and the marshes of southern Iraq
Facts checked
2026-11-16

See it on the map →

01

What is the background to this event?

In the ninth century, the Abbasid Caliphate, ruled from Baghdad and Samarra, was one of the richest and most powerful states in the world. Its port of Basra, at the head of the Persian Gulf, traded across the Indian Ocean.1

  • Wealthy residents of Basra had acquired large areas of marshland in southern Iraq, which they could own on condition that they made it fit for farming. To do this, the salty, nitrous topsoil had to be dug away by hand.1
  • The work was done by large gangs of enslaved people, many of them East Africans brought through the Indian Ocean slave trade, known in Arabic as the Zanj. They lived in work camps, and appear to have been poorly fed and badly treated.1
  • Earlier revolts by the Zanj, in 689–90 and 694, had quickly failed.1
  • From 861 the caliphate was weakened by the "Anarchy at Samarra", a decade of violent struggle in which several caliphs were murdered and the army was divided.1

In their own words

03

What happened?

In 869, Ali ibn Muhammad, who had already failed to start rebellions in Bahrain and in Basra itself, returned to the region and began to seek out enslaved workers in the Basra marshes, asking about their working conditions and food. He promised them freedom, wealth and a better life, and quickly won followers.1

The revolt, which began in September 869, grew rapidly:1

  • The rebels waged a guerrilla war in the marshes, raiding towns, villages and army camps — often at night — seizing weapons, horses and food, and freeing other enslaved people.
  • As the rebellion grew, they built fortresses and a capital, al-Mukhtara, created a fleet to move through the canals and rivers, collected taxes and minted their own coins.
  • The rebels took the port of al-Ubulla in 870 and Ahwaz in 871. In September 871, after a long blockade, they captured Basra itself; the city was burned and many of its inhabitants were massacred.
  • By 879 the rebellion had reached its furthest extent: the rebels sacked Wasit and advanced along the Tigris to within fifty miles of Baghdad.

From late 879 the Abbasid government, led by al-Muwaffaq and his son Abu al-Abbas, regained the initiative. They drove the rebels back into the marshes and, from February 881, besieged al-Mukhtara. Al-Muwaffaq offered generous terms to rebels who surrendered, and many did.1 Al-Mukhtara fell in August 883, and Ali ibn Muhammad was killed. His head was mounted on a lance and carried by boat up and down the canals so that everyone could see that he was dead, and was later paraded through Baghdad.1

04

What happened next?

  • Devastation: the war left southern Iraq devastated. Cities and towns were burned, farmland abandoned and trade disrupted. The tenth-century historian al-Mas'udi reported an estimate of 500,000 dead, but added that this was "empty conjecture".
  • Abbasid recovery: the victory strengthened al-Muwaffaq and his son, who as caliph al-Mu'tadid (892–902) briefly restored the power of the caliphate.
  • Plantation slavery declined: some historians argue that the revolt discouraged the use of large gangs of enslaved agricultural workers in the region, although slavery in other forms continued.
05

What do others say?

The sources

Almost everything we know comes from al-Tabari and other writers who supported the Abbasid government. They present Ali ibn Muhammad as an impostor and the rebels as brutal destroyers. No account by the rebels themselves survives, although their coins do.

Historians' debates

  • A slave revolt? Historians such as Alexandre Popovic see it primarily as a revolt of enslaved Africans. Ghada Talhami argues that most participants were free East Africans and Arabs, and that runaway slaves could not have organised such a movement alone.
  • Against slavery? According to al-Tabari, the rebels themselves took captives and enslaved people, including Arab women. Historians caution that this comes from hostile sources, but most agree that the rebellion did not aim to abolish slavery as such.
  • What did Ali ibn Muhammad believe? He claimed descent from the family of the Prophet and at times adopted the egalitarian ideas of the Kharijites; historians debate how sincere or consistent these claims were.
06

The evidence

  • Al-Tabari's History of the Prophets and Kings, which gives a detailed, year-by-year account.
  • The writings of al-Mas'udi and other Arabic historians and geographers.
  • Coins minted by the rebels.
  • Archaeological and environmental evidence of land reclamation in southern Iraq.
07

How is it remembered?

  • The rebellion has been remembered in modern Arabic literature and art, and by historians of the African diaspora.
  • In the twentieth century, some Arab writers and activists presented it as an early social revolution of the oppressed.
  • Communities of African descent in southern Iraq, around Basra, trace part of their history to the slave trade of this period.
08

Why does it matter?

The Zanj Rebellion was one of the largest and longest revolts of enslaved people in history. For fourteen years, people brought from Africa to labour in the marshes of Iraq fought one of the world's great empires and built a state of their own.

  • It is an important part of the history of the African diaspora in the Islamic world and the Indian Ocean, which is much less well known than the Atlantic slave trade.
  • It shows that plantation-style slavery and resistance to it existed long before the colonisation of the Americas.
  • It raises difficult questions about how rebellions against oppression can also produce violence and new forms of domination.

Questions to think about

  1. What can the conditions of work in the Basra marshes tell us about why the revolt began?
  2. How should historians use sources written entirely by the rebels' enemies?
  3. Can a rebellion be both a struggle for freedom and a source of new oppression?
  4. Why is the history of slavery in the Indian Ocean world less well known than that of the Atlantic?

What other events are like this?

  • 73 BCESpartacus and the Slave Revolt — Seventy gladiators broke out of a training school armed with kitchen knives. Within two years, an army of some 120,000 enslaved people had defeated Rome's legions again and again.
