The Tenants' Strike and the March of the Brooms

When rents soared, tenants in Buenos Aires's crowded tenements refused to pay. Women and children marched with brooms to 'sweep away the landlords'.

When
August – November 1907
Where
La Boca, Buenos Aires, Argentina

See it on the map →

01

What is the background to this event?

Around 1900, Buenos Aires was one of the fastest-growing cities in the world. Hundreds of thousands of immigrants, mainly from Italy and Spain, arrived looking for work.1

  • Most working families lived in conventillos — tenement houses where whole families shared a single room around a courtyard. In 1904, more than 10 per cent of the city's population lived in them.1
  • Conditions were terrible: up to ten people lived in a room, and 20 to 70 people might share a single toilet.1
  • Rents kept rising. By 1907, a room cost three times as much as in 1870.1
  • Anarchists and socialists had been organising workers, and earlier attempts to form leagues against high rents had failed.1
02

Who were the key people?

  • The women of the conventillos

    Who organised the strike in their tenements, led the protests and blocked evictions.

  • Miguel Pepe

    A young anarchist activist killed by police during the strike.

  • Colonel Ramón Falcón

    The chief of police, who ordered the evictions.

  • The Argentine Regional Workers' Federation (FORA)

    An anarchist-led union federation that campaigned for lower rents.

03

What happened?

In August 1907, a municipal tax increase was passed on to tenants as higher rents. The tenants of a conventillo on Calle Ituzaingó went on strike, refusing to pay.1

  • The strikers formed a committee and sought support from other tenements and workers' organisations. Within weeks, hundreds of conventillos had joined, in Buenos Aires and other cities including La Plata, Bahía Blanca and Rosario.1
  • More than 2,000 dwellings went on strike across the country.1
  • Women played a central role in organising the protests and resisting the police, landlords and courts.1
  • In La Boca, children and their mothers held the "March of the Brooms", using household brooms to "sweep out the landlords".1
  • In San Juan Street, women used their brooms to block the door against the police sent to carry out an eviction.1

The state responded with force. On 22 October, the young anarchist Miguel Pepe was killed by police acting under the orders of the police chief, Ramón Falcón. Between October and November, police carried out mass evictions, and the government used the Residence Law to deport foreign-born activists.1

04

What happened next?

  • Defeat — and a legacy: the evictions ended the strike, and rents remained high.1 But the strike became a turning point in the struggle for decent housing in Argentina.
  • Escalation: two years later, on 1 May 1909, police under Falcón fired on a workers' demonstration, killing several people. Later that year, Falcón was assassinated by the young anarchist Simón Radowitzky.1
  • Similar strikes: rent strikes took place in Montevideo, Santiago, Rio de Janeiro, New York and Glasgow, and in Mexico in 1922.1
05

What do others say?

At the time

Landlords and the government saw the strike as the work of foreign anarchist agitators. The strikers and their supporters argued that they were defending their homes against greed.

Historians' debates

  • Who led it? Historians debate the role of anarchist and socialist organisations compared with the spontaneous organisation of tenants, especially women.
  • Violence: the strike itself was largely peaceful, but it took place amid a cycle of violence between the police and anarchists, including the killing of Miguel Pepe and, later, the assassination of Falcón.
  • Public health: the strike won sympathy partly because it drew attention to disease and overcrowding, which concerned the middle classes.
06

The evidence

  • Photographs published in the magazine Caras y Caretas, now in the Archivo General de la Nación.

  • Municipal censuses of tenement housing.
07

How is it remembered?

  • A memorial tile (baldosa) in Buenos Aires commemorates Miguel Pepe.
  • The conventillo has become an icon of Buenos Aires's immigrant history, remembered in tango and in the colourful houses of La Boca.
08

Why does it matter?

The tenants' strike of 1907 was one of the first great urban housing struggles in Latin America. Women and children, often left out of histories of labour, were at its centre.

  • It shows how housing and rent can be as important as wages to working people.
  • The broom became a symbol of how everyday life could become a form of protest.
  • Rent strikes and tenants' unions remain important tactics around the world.

What other events are like this?

  • 1915The Glasgow Rent Strikes — Working-class women in wartime Glasgow refused to pay rent rises and physically blocked evictions — forcing the government to introduce rent controls.
  • 1978Crossroads: The Squatters Who Defied Apartheid — Under apartheid, Black families were not allowed to live together in Cape Town. At Crossroads, thousands built homes anyway — and women led the fight to stop the bulldozers.
