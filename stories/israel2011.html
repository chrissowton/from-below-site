The Tent Protests: Israel's Summer of Social Justice

When a young video editor could not afford a new flat, she pitched a tent in central Tel Aviv. Within weeks, tent camps had spread across Israel, and hundreds of thousands marched against the cost of living.

When
July – October 2011
Where
Rothschild Boulevard, Tel Aviv, and cities across Israel

01

What is the background to this event?

By 2011, many Israelis were struggling with the rising cost of living, especially housing, and with stretched public services such as health and education.1

  • In June 2011, a consumer boycott over the price of cottage cheese succeeded in bringing the price down.1
  • Protests elsewhere in the region that year had shown how social media could bring people onto the streets.1
02

In their own words

03

What happened?

On 14 July 2011, Daphne Leef pitched a tent in Habima Square in Tel Aviv after having to leave her flat, and invited others on Facebook to join her.1

  • The next day, about 50 tents went up on Rothschild Boulevard. Tent camps soon spread to other cities.1
  • On 23 July, tens of thousands marched in Tel Aviv. A common chant was "The people demand social justice!"1
  • Protesters came from a wide range of social and religious backgrounds. As the protests grew, they began to question the wider social and economic order.1
  • On 8 August, Prime Minister Netanyahu appointed a committee under the economist Manuel Trajtenberg to propose solutions.3
  • On 3 September, the "March of the Million" brought an estimated 460,000 people onto the streets across Israel, 300,000 of them in Tel Aviv.1
  • On 3 October, police dismantled the Rothschild Boulevard camp.1
04

What happened next?

  • Government measures: the government announced measures to tackle the housing shortage, and the Trajtenberg Committee consulted protesters before making recommendations.13
  • Into politics: Stav Shaffir and Itzik Shmuli were elected to the Knesset for the Labor Party in 2013.4
  • Later attempts: in June 2012, Leef and other activists were arrested when they tried to re-pitch tents on Rothschild Boulevard.1
05

Why does it matter?

The tent protests brought hundreds of thousands of Israelis onto the streets. They showed how the cost of housing could unite people across divisions — and how hard it is to turn mass protest into lasting change.14

  • The government announced housing measures, and the Trajtenberg Committee consulted protesters.13
  • Two protest leaders were elected to the Knesset in 2013.4
  • Israelis remember it simply as 'the social protest'.1

What do others say?How it was seen at the time, and where historians disagree

At the time

Supporters saw a non-partisan movement for social justice; critics, including some politicians and journalists, accused it of a hidden left-wing political agenda and pointed to funding from left-wing donors.1

Debates since

  • Connected to the Arab Spring? Some see links: protests led by the young, organised on social media and driven by the cost of living.1
  • What changed? Critics within the movement said its leaders never set out clear goals.1
  • Violence: the protests were largely peaceful; more than 30 activists were arrested at protests against the clearing of tents in Tel Aviv.1
