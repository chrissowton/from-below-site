The Dublin Lockout

When 300 employers locked out some 20,000 workers to destroy a new union of the unskilled, Dublin's poorest families held out for five months, fed by food ships from British workers.

When
26 August 1913 – 18 January 1914
Where
Dublin, Ireland

See it on the map →

01

What is the background to this event?

In 1913, Dublin had some of the worst slums in Europe. In Henrietta Street, more than 830 people lived in just 15 houses, and infant mortality among the poor was 142 per 1,000 births.1

  • Unskilled workers competed every day for casual work, which usually went to whoever would accept the lowest wage.1
  • James Larkin, a Liverpool docker and union organiser, founded the Irish Transport and General Workers' Union (ITGWU) in 1909 to organise them. Membership grew from 4,000 to 10,000 between 1911 and 1913.1
  • Larkin used the sympathetic strike: workers striking in support of others in dispute.1
  • William Martin Murphy, Ireland's leading businessman and owner of the Dublin tramways, was determined to stop the ITGWU.1
02

Who were the key people?

  • James Larkin

    Founder of the ITGWU and the leading figure of the workers' side.

  • James Connolly

    A Marxist organiser for the ITGWU, later executed after the 1916 Rising.

  • Delia Larkin

    James Larkin's sister, founder of the Irish Women Workers' Union, who ran food kitchens at Liberty Hall.

  • William Martin Murphy

    Chairman of the Dublin United Tramway Company, who led the employers.

  • James Nolan and John Byrne

    Workers who died after police baton charges.

  • Constance Markievicz

    A nationalist who helped run the soup kitchens.

03

What happened?

In July 1913, Murphy led a meeting of 300 employers who agreed to resist the ITGWU. In August, he dismissed 40 workers suspected of union membership, then 300 more.1

  • On 26 August, tram workers walked off their trams during Dublin Horse Show week. Employers across the city then locked out workers who refused to sign a pledge not to join the ITGWU.1
  • About 20,000 workers were affected, with their families perhaps 100,000 people.12
  • The Dublin Metropolitan Police baton-charged workers' rallies. Two workers, James Nolan and John Byrne, died of their injuries.1
  • On 31 August, police charged crowds in Sackville Street after Larkin appeared on a hotel balcony; hundreds were injured. The day became known as Bloody Sunday.2
  • The British Trades Union Congress and others sent about £150,000, including ships of food.1
  • A scheme to send hungry children to the homes of British trade unionists was blocked by the Catholic Church, which feared they would lose their faith.1
  • The British TUC refused to call sympathy strikes. By January 1914, starving workers began to return, many having signed the pledge.1

04

What happened next?

  • The Irish Citizen Army: formed during the lockout to protect workers from the police, it fought in the Easter Rising of 1916.
  • The union survives: the ITGWU recovered and grew rapidly after 1917. Today it is part of SIPTU, Ireland's largest union.
  • Connolly was executed by the British after the Rising in 1916.
05

What do others say?

At the time

Murphy and his newspapers presented Larkin as a dangerous revolutionary. Supporters, including the writers W. B. Yeats and George Russell, attacked the employers.2

Debates since

  • Murphy: his defenders argue that he paid fair wages and accepted craft unions; critics see him as starving workers into submission.1
  • Violence: police baton charges caused deaths and hundreds of injuries; some strikers also attacked trams and strikebreakers.2
  • Larkin's leadership: some historians argue his tactics alienated potential allies.

07

How is it remembered?

  • A statue of Larkin stands on O'Connell Street.
  • The centenary in 2013 was marked by re-enactments, exhibitions and a tapestry.
  • The lockout is the setting of James Plunkett's novel Strumpet City (1969).
08

Why does it matter?

The lockout was a defeat in the short term, but it established the right of unskilled workers in Ireland to organise.

  • It linked labour to the struggle for Irish independence.
  • It showed both the power and the limits of international working-class solidarity.

Questions to think about

  1. Why did Murphy see the ITGWU as such a threat?
  2. Why did the Catholic Church oppose the Kiddies' Scheme?
  3. Why did the British TUC send food but not strike?
  4. Was the lockout a defeat or a victory for the workers?
09

What other events are like this?

  • 1888The Matchwomen's Strike — About 1,400 women and girls walked out of a match factory, won their demands in three weeks, and founded the largest union of women in Britain.
  • 1915The Glasgow Rent Strikes — Working-class women in wartime Glasgow refused to pay rent rises and physically blocked evictions — forcing the government to introduce rent controls.
  • 1912The Bread and Roses Strike — More than twenty thousand migrant textile workers, speaking dozens of languages, shut down the mills of Lawrence and won.
