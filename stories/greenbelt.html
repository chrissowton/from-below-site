Land & environment

Wangari Maathai, the Green Belt Movement and Freedom Corner

Rural women planted millions of trees, and a biologist stopped a dictator from building a skyscraper in a city park. Then mothers of political prisoners went on hunger strike in the same park — and faced down the police.

When
1977 – 1992
Where
Uhuru Park, Nairobi, Kenya

See it on the map →

01

What is the background to this event?

In the 1970s, rural Kenyan women told researchers that their streams were drying up, firewood was scarce and food was harder to grow. Deforestation was destroying the land.

  • Wangari Maathai, the first woman in East and Central Africa to earn a doctorate, was a professor at the University of Nairobi.1
  • From 1982, Kenya was a one-party state under President Daniel arap Moi. Critics were imprisoned and tortured.
02

Who were the key people?

  • Wangari Maathai

    A biologist and activist who founded the Green Belt Movement and won the Nobel Peace Prize in 2004. She died in 2011.

  • Rural women of Kenya

    Who grew tree nurseries and planted trees across the country.

  • The mothers of political prisoners

    Who held a hunger strike at Freedom Corner in 1992.

  • President Daniel arap Moi

    Who ridiculed Maathai and whose government persecuted her.

03

What happened?

On World Environment Day, 5 June 1977, Maathai and the National Council of Women of Kenya marched through Nairobi and planted seven trees — the first action of the Green Belt Movement.1 She encouraged women across Kenya to grow tree nurseries using seeds from local forests, and paid them a small amount for each tree that survived.1

In October 1989, Maathai learned of a plan to build a 60-storey office tower for the ruling party's newspaper in Uhuru Park, Nairobi's main public park.1

  • She protested and wrote to the British High Commissioner, since a British businessman, Robert Maxwell, was a major investor.1
  • The government called her a "crazy woman", and President Moi suggested she should respect men and be quiet.1
  • After public outcry and the withdrawal of foreign investors, the project was abandoned.

On 28 February 1992, Maathai joined a hunger strike at a corner of Uhuru Park, which they named Freedom Corner, to demand the release of political prisoners. Most of the hunger strikers were mothers of the prisoners.1

  • On 3 March, police forcibly removed them. Maathai and three others were knocked unconscious and taken to hospital.1
  • The mothers moved their protest to All Saints Cathedral, across from the park, and continued it for months.1

04

What happened next?

  • Prisoners freed: the political prisoners were released in early 1993.
  • Karura Forest: in 1998–99, Maathai led protests against the giving away of Karura Forest to political allies; she and others were attacked by guards.
  • Democracy: multi-party elections were introduced in 1992. In 2002, Moi's party lost power, and Maathai was elected to parliament and became an assistant minister.1
  • Nobel Prize: in 2004, she became the first African woman to win the Nobel Peace Prize.1 The Green Belt Movement has planted tens of millions of trees.
05

What do others say?

At the time

The government accused Maathai of being subversive and of encouraging women to strip at Freedom Corner, where some mothers bared their bodies as a traditional curse against the police.1 Supporters saw them as heroes.

Debates since

  • Environment and politics: some criticised the Green Belt Movement for becoming political; Maathai argued that environmental destruction and bad governance were inseparable.
  • Non-violence: the movement was non-violent; the violence came from the police and hired guards.

07

How is it remembered?

  • Freedom Corner in Uhuru Park is remembered as a site of struggle for democracy.
  • The Wangari Maathai Institute for Peace and Environmental Studies at the University of Nairobi bears her name.
08

Why does it matter?

Wangari Maathai linked the environment, women's rights and democracy. Planting a tree became an act of resistance.

  • It shows how environmental movements can grow from the everyday needs of rural women.
  • The Freedom Corner protest shows the power of mothers as political actors.

Questions to think about

  1. Why did planting trees become a political act?
  2. Why did Maathai oppose the tower in Uhuru Park?
  3. Why were mothers so powerful as protesters?
  4. How are environmental and democratic struggles connected?
