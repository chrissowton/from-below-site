Against the Bomb: French Polynesia and Moruroa

For thirty years, France tested nuclear weapons on Polynesian atolls. When testing resumed in 1995, Tahitians rose in protest — and Pacific peoples demanded a nuclear-free ocean.

When
1966 – 1996
Where
Papeete, Tahiti, and Moruroa atoll, French Polynesia

See it on the map →

01

What is the background to this event?

After Algeria became independent, France moved its nuclear weapons testing to French Polynesia. Between 1966 and 1996, it carried out nearly 200 nuclear tests at Moruroa and Fangataufa atolls.1

  • Until 1974, many of the tests were in the atmosphere, using devices suspended from balloons, dropped from aircraft or exploded on barges.1
  • Fallout spread across the region. Polynesians who worked at the test sites, and people on nearby islands, were exposed to radiation, often without being told of the risks.
  • The testing brought money and jobs to Tahiti but also deepened dependence on France.
  • From 1973, after protests led by New Zealand, which sent warships to the test zone, France moved to underground testing.1
02

Who were the key people?

  • Oscar Temaru

    Mayor of Faʻaʻā and leader of the pro-independence party Tavini Huiraʻatira, a leading opponent of the tests. He later became President of French Polynesia.

  • Pouvanaa a Oopa

    An earlier Polynesian nationalist leader, who was exiled to France in 1958.

  • Former test-site workers

    Polynesian workers who later organised to demand recognition and compensation for radiation-related illnesses.

  • Pacific campaigners

    Churches, unions and activists across the Pacific who built the Nuclear-Free and Independent Pacific movement.

03

What happened?

In 1995, newly elected President Jacques Chirac announced a final series of tests at Moruroa, just a year before the Comprehensive Test Ban Treaty was due to be signed. The decision caused protests around the world, including boycotts of French wine.1

  • Protest ships, including Greenpeace's Rainbow Warrior II, sailed to the test zone and were boarded by French commandos.
  • The first test took place on 5 September 1995. In Tahiti, protests by independence supporters, unions and young people turned into a riot in Papeete that lasted two days. Rioters damaged the international airport, and about 40 people were injured.2
  • Similar rioting had followed a test in 1987.2

The last test at Moruroa took place on 27 December 1995, and the final test, at Fangataufa, on 27 January 1996. France then dismantled the test site and signed the Comprehensive Test Ban Treaty.1

04

What happened next?

  • Health: former workers' associations campaigned for years for recognition of radiation-related cancers. A French compensation law was passed in 2010, but few claims were accepted at first.
  • Revelations: a 2021 investigation, based on declassified documents, estimated that far more Polynesians had been exposed to fallout than France had admitted.3
  • Acknowledgement: in 2021, President Emmanuel Macron acknowledged in Tahiti that France owed a "debt" to French Polynesia for the tests, though he did not apologise.
  • Independence: the question of independence remains alive; Temaru served several terms as President, and French Polynesia was re-listed by the UN as a non-self-governing territory in 2013.
05

What do others say?

At the time

The French government argued that the tests were necessary for national security and safe for the population. Opponents argued that they endangered Pacific peoples and the environment, and that France was treating Polynesia as a colony.

Debates since

  • Safety: France long insisted the tests were "clean"; declassified documents and later research suggest exposure was underestimated.3
  • The 1995 riot: some independence supporters saw the riot as an expression of justified anger; others, including many Tahitians, regretted the destruction, which hurt local people and businesses.
  • Dependence: the testing programme transformed Tahiti's economy; historians debate how far it created lasting dependence on France.
06

The evidence

  • Declassified French military documents.
  • Testimonies of former workers and residents of nearby islands.
  • Satellite images and scientific studies of the atolls.
07

How is it remembered?

  • Each year on 2 July, the anniversary of the first test in 1966, ceremonies are held in Papeete to remember the victims.
  • A memorial to the victims of nuclear testing stands in Papeete.
08

Why does it matter?

The struggle against nuclear testing in French Polynesia was part of a wider movement for a nuclear-free and independent Pacific. It raised questions about who bears the costs of great powers' weapons.

  • It links peace, health, environment and decolonisation.
  • The people most affected by testing had the least say in it.
  • Survivors and their families are still seeking justice.

Questions to think about

  1. Why do you think France chose French Polynesia for its nuclear tests?
  2. Why was the 1995 decision to resume testing so controversial?
  3. How should states compensate people harmed by weapons testing?
  4. How is the struggle against testing linked to the struggle for independence?
