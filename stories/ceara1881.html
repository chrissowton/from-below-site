The Sea Dragon: Ceará's Raftsmen Refuse to Carry Slaves

When landowners in drought-stricken Ceará tried to sell enslaved people south, the raft fishermen who ferried passengers to the ships went on strike. Three years later, Ceará became the first Brazilian province to abolish slavery.

When
August 1881 – 1884
Where
Fortaleza, Ceará, Brazil

01

What is the background to this event?

In Brazil, the campaign against slavery took on a truly popular character from the 1870s.1

  • A severe drought struck the province of Ceará in 1877–78, and many landowners rushed to sell the people they enslaved to the south, through the port of Rio de Janeiro.2
  • In Fortaleza, jangadeiros — fishermen who sailed small rafts called jangadas — earned extra money carrying passengers and goods out to ocean-going ships.2
02

Who were the key people?

  • Francisco José do Nascimento ('Dragão do Mar')

    An Afro-Brazilian raft fisherman and harbour pilot who led the strike.

  • The jangadeiros of Fortaleza

    The raft fishermen who refused to carry enslaved people.

  • Ceará's abolitionists

    A strong movement, led mainly by white intellectuals.

03

What happened?

On 30 August 1881, the jangadeiros of Fortaleza went on strike, refusing to carry enslaved people out to the ships bound for the south.2

  • Their leader was Francisco José do Nascimento, known as Chico da Matilde, a fisherman and harbour pilot who had joined the abolitionists.2
  • The port authority dismissed him and other jangadeiros.2
  • Despite the dismissals, the abolitionist campaign in Ceará went on.2
  • In 1884, Ceará abolished slavery, four years before the rest of Brazil.2
  • Nascimento was invited to Rio de Janeiro and welcomed as a hero. The writer Aluísio de Azevedo gave him the name 'Dragão do Mar', the Sea Dragon.2
04

What happened next?

  • Pressure grows: Ceará's decision increased pressure on the imperial government. Amazonas also abolished slavery.1
  • Mass escapes: from 1885, enslaved people fled the plantations in large numbers, many to the Jabaquara quilombo near Santos, and the army asked not to be used to recapture them.1
  • Abolition: on 13 May 1888, Princess Isabel signed the Golden Law, ending slavery in Brazil.1
05

Why does it matter?

The jangadeiros showed that workers could refuse to take part in the slave trade. Their strike helped make Ceará the first province to end slavery, and the Sea Dragon became a national hero.21

  • Ceará's decision increased the pressure on the imperial government, and Amazonas also abolished slavery.1
  • From 1885, enslaved people fled the plantations in large numbers, and Brazil abolished slavery in 1888.1
  • In 2017, Francisco José do Nascimento's name was added to Brazil's Book of National Heroes.2

What do others say?How it was seen at the time, and where historians disagree

At the time

The port authority sided with the slave traders by dismissing the strikers; abolitionists hailed Nascimento as a hero.2

Debates since

  • From above or below? The Golden Law was signed by a princess, but it was the result of the country's first national mobilisation of public opinion, in which enslaved and freed people, workers, students, journalists and politicians all took part.1
  • Violence: the strike was peaceful; the mass escapes elsewhere in the later 1880s involved violence and deaths.1
How do we know?The evidence, and a source from the time
  • Abolitionist newspapers and illustrated magazines, such as Angelo Agostini's drawings for the Revista Ilustrada, and provincial records.21

Rio de Janeiro, 1884

A drawing by the abolitionist artist Angelo Agostini, published in the Revista Ilustrada in 1884, honouring the jangadeiro Dragão do Mar. Illustrated magazines like this helped make abolition a popular cause.

Reading it

Angelo Agostini, an abolitionist artist, published this drawing in a Rio magazine in 1884 to honour the raftsman known as the Sea Dragon. Illustrated magazines were powerful campaigning tools, reaching readers who never saw a pamphlet. Celebrating a fisherman from far-off Ceará helped make abolition a national cause.
