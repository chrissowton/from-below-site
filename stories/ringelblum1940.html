Oyneg Shabes: The Secret Archive of the Warsaw Ghetto

Inside the Warsaw Ghetto, a secret group of historians, writers, rabbis and social workers collected everything they could about Jewish life and death under Nazi occupation — then buried it in milk cans and metal boxes, so that the world would one day know.

When

Where

01

What is the background to this event?

After Germany invaded Poland in 1939, Warsaw's Jews were forced into a ghetto, sealed on 16 November 1940.1

  • The historian Emanuel Ringelblum, who had worked in Jewish social welfare, organised a secret group to document what was happening.12
  • The group met on the Sabbath, and so took the code name Oyneg Shabes — 'Sabbath delight'.1

03

What happened?

From September 1939 until January 1943, the group collected documents and asked volunteers of all ages for testimonies and reports. They gathered essays, diaries, drawings and wall posters about every side of life in the ghetto.1

  • In January 1942, Szlama Ber Winer, who had escaped from the Chełmno extermination camp, gave the group a detailed account of the camp. His report was smuggled out through the Polish underground, reached London and was published by June.1
  • Between December 1942 and March 1943, Rachel Auerbach recorded the testimony of Jakub Krzepicki, who had escaped from Treblinka. It is among the earliest detailed accounts of the camp.13
  • As the deportations to Treblinka grew, Ringelblum had the archive packed into three milk cans and ten metal boxes and buried in three places in the ghetto.1

04

What happened next?

  • Murdered: all but three members of the group were killed. Ringelblum escaped the ghetto but kept returning to work on the archive; in March 1944 his hiding place was betrayed, and he and his family were executed with the people who had hidden them.12
  • Recovered: after the war, Rachel Auerbach started the search. Two milk cans were dug up on 18 September 1946, and ten boxes on 1 December 1950. The third cache has never been found.1
  • Preserved: the archive — about 35,000 pages — is held by the Jewish Historical Institute in Warsaw, and in 1999 UNESCO listed it on its Memory of the World register.1
  • Testimony: Auerbach went on to direct the collection of witness testimony at Yad Vashem from 1954 to 1968.3
05

What do others say?

At the time

The group first collected material to write a book after the war; as it became clear that few would survive, the archive itself became their message to the future.1

Debates since

  • Whose history? The historian Samuel Kassow's study of the archive is titled Who Will Write Our History? — a question at the heart of the project.1
06

The evidence

  • The archive itself — about 6,000 documents — available to researchers in Warsaw and in digital form.1

How is it remembered?

  • A 2019 documentary film, Who Will Write Our History?, told the story of the archive.1
08

Why does it matter?

Facing destruction, the people of the ghetto made sure that their own history would be written from their own records — not only from the files of the people who murdered them.1

Questions to think about

  1. Why did the group collect ordinary things like diaries, drawings and posters, as well as reports of killings?
  2. Why did they bury the archive rather than try to smuggle it all out?
  3. What would we know about the ghetto without the archive?
  4. Who decides what is kept for the historians of the future?
09

What other events are like this?

  • 1945Nuremberg: Witnesses Against the Nazi Leaders — The victorious Allies put the surviving leaders of Nazi Germany on trial for crimes against humanity. The prosecutors relied mostly on the Nazis' own documents — but a few survivors, like the French resistance fighter Marie-Claude Vaillant-Couturier, spoke for those who could not.
  • 1961The Eichmann Trial: When Survivors Took the Stand — Adolf Eichmann organised the deportation of Europe's Jews to the death camps. At his trial in Jerusalem, survivors told their stories on the witness stand, night after night, to a worldwide audience — changing how the Holocaust was remembered.
  • 1995The Genocide Widows of Rwanda — After the genocide against the Tutsi in 1994, fifty widows founded an association to help each other survive. Survivors' groups led by women went on to demand justice, health care and recognition of sexual violence as a crime of genocide.
