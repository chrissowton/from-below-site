Prague 1968: Resisting the Tanks

When half a million Warsaw Pact troops invaded to crush 'socialism with a human face', Czechs and Slovaks resisted without weapons — removing road signs, broadcasting from secret radios and arguing with soldiers in the streets.

When
21 August 1968 – January 1969
Where
Prague and across Czechoslovakia

01

What is the background to this event?

In January 1968, Alexander Dubček became leader of the Communist Party of Czechoslovakia.1

  • In April, he launched an "Action Programme" promising "socialism with a human face": freedom of the press, freedom of speech and movement, and the possibility of a multi-party system.1
  • Censorship ended, and political debate flourished in newspapers, on television and in public meetings — the "Prague Spring".
  • The Soviet Union and its allies, especially East Germany, feared the reforms would spread.1

In their own words

03

What happened?

On the night of 20–21 August 1968, about 250,000 Warsaw Pact troops, later rising to about 500,000, with thousands of tanks, invaded Czechoslovakia.1

  • The government ordered the army not to resist. Instead, ordinary people resisted without weapons.
  • People removed or turned around street signs and house numbers to confuse the invaders, painted slogans on walls, and gathered around tanks to argue with the young soldiers.
  • Journalists ran clandestine radio and television stations, and an extraordinary Communist Party congress met in secret in a Prague factory.
  • A one-hour general strike was held on 22 August.

During the occupation, 137 Czechoslovaks were killed and 500 seriously wounded.1 Dubček and other leaders were taken to Moscow and forced to sign the Moscow Protocol, accepting the presence of Soviet troops.

On 16 January 1969, Jan Palach, a student, set himself on fire in Wenceslas Square in protest against the occupation and the public's growing resignation. He died three days later.

04

What happened next?

  • "Normalisation": Dubček was replaced in April 1969 by Gustáv Husák. Reformers were purged, censorship restored, and hundreds of thousands lost their jobs.
  • Emigration: tens of thousands of people left the country.
  • Dissent: in 1977, dissidents including Václav Havel launched Charter 77, demanding that the government respect human rights.
  • 1989: in November 1989, the Velvet Revolution peacefully ended communist rule. Dubček returned to speak to the crowds.
05

What do others say?

At the time

The Soviet Union claimed it had been invited to save socialism from counter-revolution. Czechoslovaks and most of the world saw an invasion.

Historians' debates

  • Could reform have survived? Historians debate whether Dubček's reforms could have succeeded without provoking invasion.
  • Non-violence: some argue that non-violent resistance saved many lives; others that it could not defeat a determined occupier.
  • Self-immolation: Jan Palach's act is honoured as a heroic sacrifice, but has also raised difficult questions about protest suicide; several others followed him.

07

How is it remembered?

  • A memorial to Jan Palach and Jan Zajíc stands in Wenceslas Square.
  • 21 August is commemorated each year in Czechia and Slovakia.
08

Why does it matter?

The resistance to the invasion of 1968 is one of the great examples of mass non-violent resistance. Although it could not stop the occupation, it denied the invaders legitimacy.

  • It shows the creativity of non-violent resistance.
  • It ended many people's hopes that Soviet communism could be reformed from within.

Questions to think about

  1. Why did the Soviet Union see the Prague Spring as a threat?
  2. Why do you think the people chose non-violent resistance?
  3. Was the resistance a success or a failure?
  4. How should we remember Jan Palach?

What other events are like this?

  • 1956The Hungarian Revolution of 1956 — Students, workers and soldiers rose against Hungary's Soviet-backed dictatorship and pulled down Stalin's statue. For twelve days they won — until Soviet tanks returned.
  • 1989The Baltic Way — Around two million people joined hands in a human chain 675 kilometres long, across Estonia, Latvia and Lithuania, to demand freedom from the Soviet Union.
  • 1980The Gdańsk Shipyard Strike and Solidarity — A strike over the sacking of a crane operator grew into the first independent trade union in the Soviet bloc, with ten million members.
