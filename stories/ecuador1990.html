The Indigenous Uprising of 1990

Indigenous communities blocked roads, occupied a church and shut down Ecuador for a week, demanding land, bilingual education and recognition of a 'plurinational' state. It changed the country.

When
May – June 1990
Where
Quito and the Andean highlands, Ecuador
Facts checked
2026-11-16

See it on the map →

01

What is the background to this event?

Indigenous peoples — Kichwa, Shuar and many others — make up a large part of Ecuador's population. For centuries they were exploited on large haciendas and excluded from political life.

  • Land reforms in the 1960s and 1970s ended some forms of servitude but left most land in the hands of large owners.
  • In the Amazon, oil exploration from the 1960s polluted Indigenous lands.
  • In 1986, Indigenous organisations from the highlands (ECUARUNARI) and the Amazon (CONFENIAE) founded the Confederation of Indigenous Nationalities of Ecuador (CONAIE).1

03

What happened?

In May 1990, Indigenous activists occupied the Santo Domingo church in Quito. In early June, around the time of the Inti Raymi (sun festival), CONAIE called a national uprising (levantamiento).1

  • Thousands of people blocked roads, paralysed transport and shut down the country for a week.1
  • Indigenous communities stopped bringing food to the markets, and occupied some haciendas and public buildings.
  • CONAIE issued 16 demands. The first was that Ecuador be declared a plurinational state; others included land, bilingual education, local autonomy and control of archaeological sites.1

It was the largest uprising in Ecuador's history, and it established a new form of protest that would be used again and again.1 The government was forced to negotiate with Indigenous leaders.

04

What happened next?

  • Land and education: the government agreed to negotiate land disputes, and bilingual Indigenous education was expanded.
  • More uprisings: CONAIE organised at least five national uprisings in the 1990s and early 2000s, contributing to the fall of presidents Abdalá Bucaram (1997) and Jamil Mahuad (2000).1
  • Politics: in 1996, CONAIE launched the Pachakutik political movement.1
  • A plurinational state: Ecuador's 2008 constitution recognised the country as plurinational, and also gave rights to nature.1
05

What do others say?

At the time

Landowners and many politicians saw the uprising as a threat to national unity and the economy. Indigenous leaders argued that recognising plurinationality would strengthen, not divide, the nation.

Debates since

  • Plurinationalism: debate continues about what a plurinational state means in practice.
  • Coups: CONAIE's participation in the 2000 coup against President Mahuad was controversial, even among supporters.1
  • Violence: the 1990 uprising was largely peaceful; later protests, such as those of 2019 and 2022, involved clashes in which protesters, police and soldiers were killed or injured.
  • Oil and mining: tensions continue between Indigenous rights and governments' plans for oil and mining.

07

How is it remembered?

  • The 1990 uprising is remembered as the moment Indigenous peoples entered Ecuador's political life as a national force.
08

Why does it matter?

The 1990 uprising transformed Ecuadorian politics and made Indigenous peoples a powerful political force. It became a model for Indigenous movements across Latin America.

  • It linked land, culture and political recognition.
  • It showed the power of road blockades and economic boycotts by rural communities.

What other events are like this?

  • 2000The Cochabamba Water War — When Bolivia sold its city water system to a multinational consortium and bills soared, farmers, factory workers and residents rose up — and won.
  • 1994The Zapatista Uprising — On the day the North American Free Trade Agreement came into force, masked Indigenous rebels seized towns in southern Mexico and declared 'Enough!'
  • 1781Túpac Katari, Bartolina Sisa and the Siege of La Paz — An Aymara coca trader and his wife led tens of thousands in a siege of the Spanish city of La Paz. Thousands died of starvation; the leaders were executed. But the Aymara say: 'I will return, and I will be millions.'
