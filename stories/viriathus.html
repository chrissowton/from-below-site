Viriathus and the Lusitanian War

After a Roman governor massacred Lusitanians who had laid down their arms, one survivor — a shepherd turned warrior — led eight years of resistance to Rome. Rome could not defeat him in battle, so it paid his own men to kill him.

When
147 – 139 BCE
Where
Lusitania (Portugal and western Spain)
Facts checked

01

What is the background to this event?

In the second century BCE, Rome was expanding into the Iberian peninsula. The Lusitanians lived in what is now Portugal and western Spain.1

  • In 151 BCE, the Roman commander Lucullus made peace with a tribe of the Vaccaei, then had its men killed.1
  • In 150 BCE, the governor Servius Sulpicius Galba offered the Lusitanians land in exchange for their surrender. When they gathered unarmed, his troops massacred them.1
  • Viriathus was one of the few who escaped.1

In their own words

03

What happened?

In 147 BCE, the Lusitanians were again surrounded by a Roman army. Viriathus persuaded them not to surrender, and led them to safety.1

  • For eight years he led the Lusitanians and their allies, including Vettones and Celtiberians, in a war of ambushes and raids.1
  • He defeated several Roman armies and encouraged other peoples to rebel.1
  • In about 140 BCE, he forced a Roman army to accept a peace treaty, under which he was recognised as a "friend of the Roman people".
  • Rome broke the treaty. In 139 BCE, Caepio bribed three of Viriathus's companions, who killed him in his sleep.1

04

What happened next?

  • The assassins: according to tradition, when they asked for their reward, Rome replied that it did not pay traitors.
  • Conquest: resistance continued, notably at Numantia, which fell in 133 BCE after a long siege.
05

What do others say?

At the time

Roman writers called the Lusitanians bandits; some, like Appian, admired Viriathus's leadership and condemned Rome's treachery.2

Debates since

  • Violence on both sides: Lusitanian raids and Roman massacres were both part of the war.1
  • National hero: later nationalists, especially in Portugal, turned Viriathus into a founding figure, which historians treat with caution.
06

The evidence

  • Appian, Diodorus, Livy's summaries and other Greek and Roman writers.2
07

How is it remembered?

  • Statues of Viriathus stand in Viseu, Portugal, and Zamora, Spain.
08

Why does it matter?

Viriathus became a symbol of resistance to empire, and of the treachery that empires use.

  • His story shows how massacres can create the resistance they are meant to prevent.

Questions to think about

  3. Why did Rome break the treaty?
