1908: The Young Turk Revolution and the Strike Wave

In July 1908, rebel officers in the Balkan mountains forced the Sultan to restore the constitution. Muslims and Christians celebrated together. Then workers across the empire went on strike — some 119 strikes in five months — and the new government moved to ban them.

When
3 July – December 1908
Where

01

What is the background to this event?

Sultan Abdul Hamid II had suspended the Ottoman constitution and parliament in 1878, and ruled as an absolute monarch for 30 years.1

  • Crop failures caused famine in 1905, and wages did not keep up with prices.1
  • Revolutions in Russia in 1905 and in Iran in 1906 inspired Ottoman reformers.1
  • In 1908, workers in Istanbul began to strike.1
  • In Macedonia, there was constant violence between rival national groups, and Ottoman officers were frustrated.1

In their own words

03

What happened?

On 3 July 1908, Major Ahmed Niyazi raided the garrison at Resen and took 160 volunteers into the mountains.1

  • He toured villages, promising Muslims reform and Christians equality under a constitution; Bulgarians joined his force.1
  • Other officers followed his example, using their links within the Third Army, based in Salonica, to spread the revolt.1
  • On the night of 23–24 July, the Sultan restored the constitution and called elections.1
  • Muslims and Christians celebrated together in churches and mosques, and former enemy bands handed in their weapons.1
  • Between late July and December 1908, some 119 strikes involving thousands of workers swept the empire, in the tobacco industry and many others, demanding higher wages and better conditions.3

04

What happened next?

  • Strikes restricted: in September 1908 the government issued a temporary law against strikes, and in 1909 parliament passed a strike law (the Tatil-i Eşgal law) that banned most strikes and unions.24
  • Elections: the new parliament, elected in late 1908, included Turks, Arabs, Albanians, Greeks, Armenians, Jews, Bulgarians and Serbs.2
  • Counter-revolution: after a failed uprising in favour of the Sultan in April 1909, Abdul Hamid was deposed.1
  • Dictatorship: by 1913 the CUP had set up a one-party dictatorship, which went on to repress minorities.1
05

What do others say?

At the time

Crowds hailed the rebel officers as 'heroes of liberty'; the government soon came to see strikes as a threat to order.12

Debates since

  • From above or below? The revolution was led by army officers, but it relied on villagers, local bands and wider unrest. Historians debate how much ordinary people shaped it.1
  • A betrayed revolution? Many scholars describe the CUP's later repression of labour and minorities as a betrayal of the revolution's promises.1
  • Violence: the revolution was largely bloodless, but CUP agents carried out a string of assassinations which helped force the Sultan to give way.1

07

How is it remembered?

  • The 1908 revolution is remembered in Turkey as the start of the Second Constitutional Era.1
08

Why does it matter?

The 1908 revolution brought a brief moment of freedom and hope across a multi-ethnic empire. The strike wave that followed showed that workers expected the new freedoms to apply to them — and how quickly the new rulers limited them.

What other events are like this?

  • 1905Iran's Constitutional Revolution — Merchants, clerics, craftsmen and radicals forced the Shah to grant Iran a constitution and an elected parliament — the first in the Middle East.
  • 1905Bloody Sunday, St Petersburg — Tens of thousands of workers and their families, led by a priest and carrying icons, marched to ask the tsar for help. Soldiers opened fire, and the revolution of 1905 began.
  • 1919Egypt's 1919 Revolution — When Britain exiled the nationalist leader Saad Zaghlul, Egyptians of every class and religion rose up in strikes and demonstrations — and women marched in public for the first time.
