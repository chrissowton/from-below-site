The Women's March on Versailles

Angry at the price of bread, thousands of Paris market women marched twelve miles in the rain to Versailles — and brought the King of France back to Paris.

When
5 – 6 October 1789
Where
Paris and Versailles, France
Facts checked
2026-11-15

See it on the map →

01

What is the background to this event?

By the autumn of 1789, the French Revolution was under way. The Estates-General had become a National Assembly, the Bastille had fallen on 14 July, and in August the Assembly had abolished feudal privileges and adopted the Declaration of the Rights of Man and of the Citizen.1

  • But for ordinary Parisians, the most urgent question was bread. Most workers spent nearly half their income on it, and shortages and high prices were constant.1
  • King Louis XVI was hesitating to approve the August decrees and the Declaration, and many feared that he would use royal troops to dissolve the Assembly.1
  • At a banquet for newly arrived royal troops at Versailles on 1 October, officers were said to have trampled on the revolutionary tricolour cockade. The story caused outrage in Paris.1

In their own words

03

What happened?

On the morning of 5 October 1789, a young woman beat a drum at the edge of a market in eastern Paris. Women from the markets, many carrying kitchen knives and other makeshift weapons, gathered at the Hôtel de Ville, the city hall, demanding bread and arms.1 The crowd grew to between six and seven thousand, perhaps ten thousand, and broke into the building, seizing weapons. Stanislas-Marie Maillard took up a drum and led them out of the city with the cry "To Versailles!"1

The women marched about 20 kilometres to Versailles in the pouring rain, dragging cannon with them. Thousands of National Guardsmen, threatening to desert, forced their commander, Lafayette, to lead them after the women.1 At Versailles:1

  • The women crowded into the National Assembly, and a delegation was received by the King, who promised to provide grain to Paris.
  • That night, the King agreed to accept the August decrees and the Declaration of the Rights of Man.
  • Early on 6 October, part of the crowd broke into the palace. Two of the royal bodyguards were killed, and their heads were carried on pikes. The Queen fled through a passage to the King's rooms.

Lafayette's guardsmen restored order. The crowd demanded that the King come to Paris; he agreed. That afternoon, the royal family was escorted to Paris by a huge procession, with the crowd shouting that they were bringing back "the baker, the baker's wife and the baker's boy". Within weeks, the National Assembly also moved to Paris.1

04

What happened next?

  • The King in Paris: the royal family lived in the Tuileries palace under the eyes of the Paris crowd. Louis XVI tried to flee in 1791, was deposed in 1792 and executed in January 1793.
  • Women in the Revolution: women continued to take part in revolutionary politics. Olympe de Gouges wrote her Declaration of the Rights of Woman (1791). But in 1793 the revolutionary government banned women's political clubs, and women did not win the vote in France until 1944.
  • Bread: the price of bread remained a central political issue throughout the Revolution.
05

What do others say?

At the time

Royalists described the march as the work of a bloodthirsty mob — some even claimed that many of the "women" were men in disguise — and the violence at the palace horrified many in France and abroad. Edmund Burke's Reflections on the Revolution in France (1790) famously described the scene as an outrage. Revolutionaries celebrated the march as a victory of the people.

Historians' debates

  • Spontaneous or organised? Some historians argue that the march was encouraged or manipulated by revolutionary politicians; others, such as Dominique Godineau, stress the independent action of working women.
  • The violence: the killing of the bodyguards and the threats against the Queen are part of the story; historians debate how far they reflected the crowd as a whole.
  • Women and the Revolution: feminist historians have recovered the role of women in 1789, and asked why the Revolution that they helped to make later excluded them from politics.

The evidence

  • Contemporary prints and pamphlets, both celebrating and condemning the march.
  • The records of the National Assembly and the memoirs of participants.

07

How is it remembered?

  • The march became one of the most famous images of the French Revolution, and of women's political action.
  • It has been depicted in countless prints, paintings and films.
08

Why does it matter?

The October Days were one of the turning points of the French Revolution. Ordinary working women, driven by hunger and anger, forced the King to accept the Revolution's founding laws and moved the centre of power to Paris.

  • It was one of the first great political actions led by women in modern history.
  • It shows how bread and survival — not only abstract ideas — drove revolutionary politics.
  • It ended the King's independence and set the stage for the end of the monarchy.

Questions to think about

  1. Why do you think the price of bread was such a powerful political issue?
  2. Why were women at the forefront of this march?
  3. How should we judge the violence of 6 October alongside the achievements of the march?
  4. Why do you think the Revolution later excluded women from politics?

What other events are like this?

  • 1888The Matchwomen's Strike — About 1,400 women and girls walked out of a match factory, won their demands in three weeks, and founded the largest union of women in Britain.
  • 1381The Peasants' Revolt — Tens of thousands of villagers and townspeople marched on London to demand an end to serfdom. For a few days, the government of England was at their mercy.
  • 1915The Glasgow Rent Strikes — Working-class women in wartime Glasgow refused to pay rent rises and physically blocked evictions — forcing the government to introduce rent controls.
