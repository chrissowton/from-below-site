The Kazym Rebellion

When Soviet officials took their children to boarding schools and their shamans to prison, and then trespassed on a sacred lake, the Khanty and Nenets of Western Siberia resisted. It was the last armed conflict between Siberia's Indigenous peoples and the Russian state.

When
1931 – 1934
Where
Kazym and Lake Numto, Western Siberia, Russia

See it on the map →

01

What is the background to this event?

The Khanty and Nenets are Indigenous peoples of Western Siberia who lived by reindeer herding, hunting and fishing.

  • In 1930, the Soviet government established a "cultural base" at Kazym, with a school, hospital and store, to draw the Khanty into settled village life and collectivisation.1
  • Khanty children were removed from their parents and sent to boarding schools; families who refused had their fishing equipment confiscated, leaving them unable to feed themselves.1
  • Traditional leaders were labelled "kulaks" (rich peasants) and arrested or executed by the secret police.1
  • Shamans, the spiritual leaders, were imprisoned for "wrong understanding of the world".1
02

Who were the key people?

  • The Khanty and Nenets

    Reindeer herders, fishers and hunters of the Kazym and Numto region.

  • Khanty shamans

    Spiritual leaders who were imprisoned by the Soviet authorities.

  • Polina Schneider

    A Communist Party official who led an expedition to the sacred lake and was killed.

  • Yeremey Aypin

    A Khanty writer whose novel brought the story to light in 2002.

03

What happened?

In 1931, Khanty and Nenets families broke into the Kazym boarding school and took back their children.1 Many moved deeper into the forest, away from Soviet settlements.1

In 1933, local authorities decided to fish in Lake Numto — "Lake of the God" — which was sacred to the Khanty and Nenets. Indigenous people prevented the first work party from fishing.1

  • In December 1933, another party of five, led by the official Polina Schneider, went to the lake and to its sacred island, where women were forbidden. Schneider is said to have fired her pistol at sacred images.1
  • The Khanty and Nenets seized the five and killed them, strangling them with reindeer lassos.1

In 1934, the Soviet authorities sent Red Army and secret police units. Accounts differ greatly:1

  • Khanty and Nenets accounts tell of settlements destroyed, people shot, bombed from aircraft, drowned or left to freeze, with hundreds killed.
  • Soviet archives record that 88 people were detained and claim that no one was executed and the operation was carried out humanely.

Either way, the resistance was crushed.1

04

What happened next?

  • Collectivisation: the Khanty and Nenets were forced into collective farms, and their religious practices were suppressed.
  • Oil: from the 1960s, the region became the centre of Soviet and then Russian oil production, threatening reindeer pastures and sacred sites.
  • Numto today: Lake Numto is now in a nature park, but oil companies have sought to drill there, leading to protests by Khanty and Nenets in the 2010s.
05

What do others say?

At the time

Soviet authorities saw the Khanty resistance as kulak and shamanic counter-revolution. The Khanty and Nenets saw it as defence of their children, their land and their sacred places.

Historians' debates

  • Whose account? Historians must weigh oral testimonies of mass killing against official archives that record few deaths.1
  • Violence on both sides: the Khanty and Nenets killed five members of the Soviet party at Numto; the Soviet response, by Indigenous accounts, killed far more.
  • Boarding schools: some note that the Kazym school taught in the Khanty language, but it still separated children from their families.1
06

The evidence

  • Soviet archives, opened after 1991.
  • Oral testimonies of Khanty and Nenets families.
  • Yeremey Aypin's novel Our Lady in the Blood-Stained Snow (2002) and the film The Khanty Saga (2008).1
07

How is it remembered?

  • The rebellion is remembered by the Khanty and Nenets, and has become better known since the 2000s through literature and film.
08

Why does it matter?

The Kazym rebellion is a rare example of open Indigenous resistance to Soviet rule. It shows how Soviet modernisation affected small Indigenous peoples — through boarding schools, attacks on religion and forced collectivisation.

  • It raises questions about the rights of Indigenous peoples to their land, children and beliefs.
  • It shows how archives and oral memory can tell very different stories.

Questions to think about

  1. Why do you think families took their children back from the boarding school?
  2. Why was Lake Numto so important to the Khanty and Nenets?
  3. How should historians deal with very different accounts of the same events?
  4. What rights should Indigenous peoples have over sacred places?

What other events are like this?

  • 1916The 1916 Central Asian Revolt (Ürkün) — When the Tsar ordered Central Asian men to be conscripted for labour behind the front lines, the region rose in revolt. The repression, and the flight across the mountains to China, cost well over a hundred thousand lives.
  • 1979The Alta Controversy — Sámi activists and environmentalists chained themselves together in the Arctic winter to stop a dam on the Alta river. They lost the dam — but won recognition for the Sámi people.
  • 2016Standing Rock and the Dakota Access Pipeline — Indigenous 'water protectors' and their allies camped beside the Missouri River to oppose an oil pipeline, in the largest gathering of Native Americans in more than a century.
