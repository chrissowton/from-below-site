Peace for Vieques

For sixty years, the US Navy bombed part of a small inhabited island in Puerto Rico. When a stray bomb killed a local guard, islanders and supporters occupied the bombing range until the Navy left.

When

Where

01

What is the background to this event?

From 1941, the US Navy took over much of the island of Vieques for military exercises and bombing practice.1

  • Islanders were moved off their land, and the remaining residents lived next to a live firing range.1
  • Residents raised concerns about environmental damage and health.1

In their own words

03

What happened?

On 19 April 1999, David Sanes, a civilian guard working for the Navy, was killed by a stray bomb during an exercise.1

  • Islanders and activists from across Puerto Rico began campaigning against the bombing, including by entering and camping on the range.1
  • On 4 May 2000, US Marshals, Marines and the Navy removed the protest camps.1
  • Days later, hundreds of protesters from many backgrounds and countries entered the range again.1
  • Hundreds were arrested over the following years for civil disobedience.
04

What happened next?

  • Withdrawal: military operations ended in 2001, and the Navy left Vieques completely in 2003.1
  • Contamination: the former range remains contaminated with chemicals and unexploded ordnance, and clean-up continues.1
  • Health: a Puerto Rico Health Department survey found cancer rates 27% higher than on the main island.1
05

Why does it matter?

Vieques showed how a small community, using civil disobedience, could force the world's most powerful navy to leave.

  • It raised questions about Puerto Rico's colonial relationship with the United States.
  • The former range remains contaminated with chemicals and unexploded ordnance, and clean-up continues.1
  • A Puerto Rico Health Department survey found cancer rates on Vieques 27% higher than on the main island.1

What do others say?How it was seen at the time, and where historians disagree

At the time

The Navy argued that Vieques was essential for training; protesters argued that the island's people had a right to peace and health.13

Debates since

  • Health: scientists debate how far the high cancer rates are caused by the bombing.1
  • Violence: the protest was overwhelmingly non-violent; a celebration on the old range in 2003 turned destructive. The years of bombing harmed land, sea and people.1
