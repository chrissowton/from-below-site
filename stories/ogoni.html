The Ogoni Struggle and Ken Saro-Wiwa

The Ogoni people's peaceful campaign against oil pollution forced Shell out of their land. Nigeria's military government responded with violence, and in 1995 hanged the writer Ken Saro-Wiwa and eight others.

When
1990 – 1995
Where
Ogoniland, Rivers State, Nigeria
Facts checked
2026-11-15

See it on the map →

01

What is the background to this event?

Ogoniland is a small area of the Niger Delta, home to roughly 500,000 Ogoni people at the time of the campaign (current estimates are much higher, at over 2 million). Oil was discovered there in the 1950s.1

  • Oil spills and gas flaring polluted farmland, creeks and the air.1
  • The Ogoni received little of the wealth from the oil, and had few schools, hospitals or roads.1
  • Nigeria was ruled by the military for most of this period.

In their own words

03

What happened?

In 1990, Ken Saro-Wiwa and others founded MOSOP and issued the Ogoni Bill of Rights, demanding political autonomy, a share of oil wealth and protection of the environment.1

  • MOSOP committed itself to non-violence.1
  • On 4 January 1993, a large part of the Ogoni population marched to demand environmental justice.3
  • In 1993, Shell withdrew its staff from Ogoniland.1
  • The military government launched a campaign of repression. Soldiers raided villages; Amnesty International and others reported many killings, rapes and the destruction of homes.3
  • In May 1994, four conservative Ogoni chiefs were murdered by a mob. Saro-Wiwa and others were arrested and charged with incitement to murder, although Saro-Wiwa was not present.1
  • A special military tribunal convicted nine men. They were hanged on 10 November 1995, despite international appeals.1

Two key prosecution witnesses later said they had been bribed to testify.1

04

What happened next?

  • Sanctions: Nigeria was suspended from the Commonwealth. Abacha died in 1998, and civilian rule returned in 1999.1
  • Settlement: in 2009, Shell paid $15.5 million to settle a lawsuit in the United States accusing it of complicity in the executions, while denying wrongdoing.
  • The UN report: in 2011, the UN Environment Programme found severe contamination and said clean-up could take 25–30 years.4
  • Courts: in 2021, a Dutch court ordered Shell Nigeria to compensate farmers in Oruma, Goi and Ikot Ada Udo.
05

What do others say?

At the time

The Nigerian government said MOSOP was responsible for the murder of the four chiefs. MOSOP and human rights groups said the trial was a pretext to silence Saro-Wiwa.1

Debates since

  • The four chiefs: their murder was real, and their families have also sought recognition. Who was responsible remains disputed.1
  • Shell: Shell denies any role in the executions or in military abuses; campaigners argue it benefited from them.
  • Pardon: in 2025, Nigeria's government pardoned the Ogoni Nine; campaigners demanded full exoneration.
06

The evidence

  • Saro-Wiwa's writings, including his prison diary, A Month and a Day.
  • Court documents from cases in the US, UK and the Netherlands.
  • The UN Environment Programme report (2011).4

07

How is it remembered?

  • 4 January is marked as Ogoni Day.
  • A mobile memorial, the "Battle Bus", was created by the artist Sokari Douglas Camp.
  • MOSOP received the Right Livelihood Award in 1994.
08

Why does it matter?

The Ogoni struggle became one of the best-known environmental justice campaigns in the world.

  • It linked the environment to the rights of minorities and indigenous peoples.
  • It raised questions about the responsibility of oil companies for the actions of governments.

Open questions

  1. Why did the Ogoni receive so little from the oil on their land?
  2. Why did MOSOP choose non-violence?
  3. Was Ken Saro-Wiwa given a fair trial?
  4. What responsibility does an oil company have for government violence?
