The Victims Who Brought Hissène Habré to Trial

A former prisoner gathered the testimonies of hundreds of survivors of Hissène Habré's secret police. After 25 years of campaigning, Habré became the first former head of state convicted by another country's courts for crimes against humanity.

When
1982 – 1990 (dictatorship); 2016 (verdict)
Where
N'Djamena, Chad, and Dakar, Senegal
Facts checked
2026-11-16

01

What is the background to this event?

Hissène Habré seized power in Chad in 1982 and ruled until 1990. His regime was supported by the United States and France as a barrier to Libya.1

  • His secret police, the Documentation and Security Directorate (DDS), tortured and killed opponents and members of ethnic groups seen as hostile.1
  • The DDS received training and support from the United States.1
  • In 1990, Habré was overthrown by Idriss Déby and fled to Senegal, reportedly with $11 million from the treasury.1

In their own words

03

What happened?

After Habré fell, a Chadian truth commission estimated that 40,000 people had died under his rule and 200,000 had been tortured. Human Rights Watch later documented 1,200 killings and 12,000 victims of abuse in DDS files.1

  • Souleymane Guengueng and other survivors set up an association and collected testimonies.1
  • In 2001, Human Rights Watch found thousands of DDS documents in an abandoned building in N'Djamena.2
  • In 2000, a Senegalese court charged Habré, but higher courts ruled that Senegal had no jurisdiction. Belgium then sought his extradition.1
  • In 2012, the International Court of Justice ordered Senegal to try him or extradite him.1
  • Senegal and the African Union created the Extraordinary African Chambers in Dakar. The trial began in 2015, with nearly 100 witnesses.1
  • On 30 May 2016, Habré was convicted of crimes against humanity, torture, war crimes and rape, and sentenced to life imprisonment.1

04

What happened next?

  • Appeal: the life sentence was upheld in 2017.1
  • Compensation: victims were awarded compensation, but most had not been paid.3
  • Death: Habré died of COVID-19 in Dakar in August 2021.1
05

What do others say?

At the time

Western governments presented Habré as an ally against Libya's Muammar Gaddafi. Human rights groups called him "Africa's Pinochet".1

Debates since

  • The death toll: the truth commission's figure of 40,000 is widely cited, but harder to verify than the 1,200 deaths documented in DDS files.1
  • Selective justice: critics noted that officials of Déby's government, some of whom served under Habré, were not tried in Dakar.
  • Outside support: survivors and historians have pointed to the support given to Habré by the United States and France.2

07

How is it remembered?

  • The documentary The Dictator Hunter (2007) follows Reed Brody and Souleymane Guengueng.
  • Survivors' groups in Chad continue to campaign for compensation and memorials.
08

Why does it matter?

The Habré case showed that survivors themselves could drive international justice, and that African courts could try a former African leader.

  • It was the first case of universal jurisdiction to reach a verdict in Africa.
  • It raised questions about the foreign governments that supported Habré.

Questions to think about

  1. Why did it take 25 years to bring Habré to trial?
  2. Why was it important that the trial took place in Africa?
  3. What role did the survivors play?
  4. Should foreign governments that supported Habré also be held responsible?
