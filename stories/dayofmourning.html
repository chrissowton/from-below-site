Civil rights & anti-racism

The Aboriginal Day of Mourning

While Sydney celebrated 150 years since the arrival of the First Fleet, Aboriginal activists met in protest and demanded full citizenship and equality.

When

Where
Australian Hall, Sydney, Australia

01

What is the background to this event?

On 26 January 1788 the First Fleet arrived at Sydney Cove, beginning the British colonisation of Australia. For Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander peoples, colonisation brought dispossession, violence, disease and the destruction of communities.

By the 1930s, Aboriginal people across Australia lived under "protection" laws:

  • State Aborigines Protection Boards controlled where many Aboriginal people could live, work and travel, managed their wages and could take their children away.
  • Many were confined to reserves and missions, with little education or health care.
  • Aboriginal people were not counted in the national census, and in most states could not vote.

Aboriginal activists had begun to organise. In Melbourne, the Yorta Yorta leader William Cooper founded the Australian Aborigines' League (AAL) and collected signatures for a petition to King George V. In Sydney, Jack Patten and William Ferguson founded the Aborigines Progressive Association (APA) in 1937.1

For the 150th anniversary celebrations of 1938 — the "sesquicentenary" — the New South Wales government planned a re-enactment of the landing of Captain Arthur Phillip. Aboriginal activists resolved that the anniversary should be marked instead as a day of mourning.1

03

What happened?

In early January 1938, Patten and Ferguson published a statement, "Citizen Rights for Aborigines", and a manifesto, Aborigines Claim Citizen Rights!, which attacked the "protection" laws. Ferguson argued that all Aboriginal legislation was designed to drive Aboriginal people onto reserves, where there was "no future for them".1

On 26 January 1938, while Sydney celebrated the sesquicentenary with a re-enactment of the First Fleet's landing, Aboriginal people from New South Wales and Victoria marched through the streets of Sydney. Refused access to the Town Hall, they met in the Australian Hall in Elizabeth Street. Only Aboriginal people were admitted to the conference.1

The conference unanimously passed a resolution, read by Jack Patten. It described the anniversary as marking "the Whiteman's seizure of our country", condemned "the callous treatment of our people by the whitemen during the past 150 years", and called on the nation to make new laws to raise Aboriginal people "to full citizen status and equality within the community".1

A few days later, on 31 January 1938, a delegation of Day of Mourning leaders met the Prime Minister, Joseph Lyons. Patten presented a ten-point policy, which included a federal takeover of Aboriginal affairs, a national department for Aboriginal affairs with Aboriginal representation, and full citizenship rights.1

04

What happened next?

  • A 'New Deal': in December 1938 the federal government announced a "New Deal for Aborigines", which promised a path to full citizenship. Its implementation stalled with changes of government and the outbreak of the Second World War, and it was widely criticised as assimilationist.
  • A newspaper: in April 1938 Patten launched The Australian Abo Call, the first national newspaper for Aboriginal people; it ran until September 1938.
  • Long struggle: the campaign for equal rights continued through the 1967 referendum, which allowed the Commonwealth to make laws for Aboriginal people and to count them in the census, and the Aboriginal Tent Embassy of 1972.
  • NAIDOC Week: the Day of Mourning was later marked each year as Aborigines Day, a tradition that developed into today's NAIDOC Week.
05

What do others say?

At the time

The Sydney newspapers paid relatively little attention to the protest, focusing instead on the sesquicentenary celebrations. Supporters of the "protection" system argued that Aboriginal people were not ready for full citizenship.

Historians' debates

  • Citizenship or sovereignty? The 1938 activists demanded equal citizenship within Australia. Later Aboriginal activists placed greater emphasis on land rights, sovereignty and treaty. Historians debate how these demands relate to each other.
  • Assimilation? Some historians argue that the 1938 demands for "full citizen status" fitted with the assimilation policies of the time; others stress that the activists demanded equality on their own terms.
  • Divisions: the APA soon split between supporters of Patten and Ferguson, and historians have explored the tensions between the activists.

07

How is it remembered?

  • The Australian Hall in Elizabeth Street, Sydney, was saved from demolition after a campaign and is now on the National Heritage List.
  • Invasion Day and Survival Day events are held each year on 26 January in cities across Australia.
  • The Day of Mourning was re-enacted for its fiftieth anniversary in 1988, the year of Australia's bicentenary.
08

Why does it matter?

The Day of Mourning is one of the earliest major Aboriginal civil rights protests in Australia. On the day that settler Australia celebrated its foundation, Aboriginal people insisted on their own view of history — as a story of invasion and dispossession — and on their right to full and equal citizenship.

  • It was organised and led entirely by Aboriginal people.
  • It began a tradition of protest on 26 January, now known as Invasion Day or Survival Day.
  • The debate about the meaning and date of Australia Day continues today.

What other events are like this?

  • 1966The Wave Hill Walk-Off — Gurindji stockmen and their families walked off a British-owned cattle station. What began as a strike over pay became the foundation of the Aboriginal land rights movement.
  • 1972The Māori Language Petition — Young Māori activists delivered a petition of more than 30,000 signatures calling for te reo Māori to be taught in schools — the start of the language's revival.
  • 1969The Occupation of Alcatraz — Calling themselves 'Indians of All Tribes', Native American students and activists occupied the abandoned prison island for nineteen months — and changed federal policy.
