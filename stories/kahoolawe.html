Protect Kahoʻolawe: Reclaiming a Sacred Island

Native Hawaiian activists landed on an island the US Navy had been bombing for decades. Their campaign stopped the bombing and won the island back for the Hawaiian people.

When
1976 – 1994
Where
Kahoʻolawe, Hawaiʻi
Facts checked
2026-11-16

See it on the map →

01

What is the background to this event?

Kahoʻolawe, the smallest of the eight main Hawaiian islands, is sacred to Native Hawaiians as a place associated with Kanaloa, god of the ocean, and has many archaeological and religious sites. After the overthrow of the Hawaiian Kingdom in 1893 and annexation by the United States in 1898, the island was leased for ranching, which badly eroded its land.1

  • After the Japanese attack on Pearl Harbor in December 1941, the US military took over Kahoʻolawe and used it as a training ground and bombing range.1
  • Bombing continued throughout the Cold War and the Vietnam War. In 1965, in "Operation Sailor Hat", 500 tons of TNT were exploded on the island's shore to test the blast resistance of ships, leaving a large crater.1
  • From the late 1960s, a "Hawaiian Renaissance" saw a revival of Hawaiian language, music, hula and voyaging, and a new political awareness among Native Hawaiians.1
02

Who were the key people?

  • George Helm

    A musician and activist, a leader of the Protect Kahoʻolawe ʻOhana. He was lost at sea with Kimo Mitchell in March 1977 while trying to return from the island.

  • Kimo Mitchell

    A park ranger and activist who disappeared with George Helm.

  • Walter Ritte and Emmett Aluli

    Activists among the first group to land on the island in January 1976. Aluli, a doctor, led the ʻOhana for decades.

  • Protect Kahoʻolawe ʻOhana (PKO)

    The organisation formed to stop the bombing and restore the island, which practises the principle of aloha ʻāina (love of the land).

  • The US Navy

    Which used the island as a bombing range from 1941 to 1990.

In their own words

03

What happened?

In January 1976, more than fifty people from across the Hawaiian islands gathered on Maui with the aim of "invading" Kahoʻolawe. A small group — including Walter Ritte, Emmett Aluli and others — succeeded in landing on the island, in defiance of the Navy. They were removed and arrested.1

The landings continued:1

  • Activists repeatedly landed on the island, risking arrest and the danger of unexploded bombs. Several, including Ritte and Richard Sawyer, served jail sentences for trespass.
  • In March 1977, George Helm and Kimo Mitchell were lost at sea while trying to return from the island; their bodies were never found. They became martyrs of the movement.
  • The Protect Kahoʻolawe ʻOhana went to federal court to stop the bombing and to require the Navy to protect the island's cultural sites and comply with environmental laws.

In 1977, a federal court allowed the bombing to continue, but ordered the Navy to prepare an environmental impact statement and survey the island's historic sites.1 In 1980, a consent decree allowed the ʻOhana regular access to the island for religious, cultural and educational purposes, and in 1981 the entire island was placed on the National Register of Historic Places.

04

What happened next?

  • The bombing stops: after years of protest and legal action, the Navy ended live-fire training on Kahoʻolawe in 1990, following an order by President George H. W. Bush.
  • The island returned: in 1994 the whole island was transferred to the state of Hawaiʻi. The Kahoʻolawe Island Reserve was created to manage it, and the island may be used only for Native Hawaiian cultural, spiritual and subsistence purposes, restoration and education.
  • Clean-up: the Navy carried out a ten-year clean-up of unexploded ordnance, but large areas remain dangerous.
  • Restoration: volunteers plant native vegetation and hold ceremonies, including the annual Makahiki, on the island.
05

Why does it matter?

The struggle for Kahoʻolawe was a turning point in the Native Hawaiian movement. Through non-violent direct action, legal challenges and the revival of cultural practice, a small group of activists forced the US military to stop bombing a sacred island and return it to the Hawaiian people.

  • It brought together cultural revival and political activism under the principle of aloha ʻāina.
  • It inspired the wider Hawaiian sovereignty movement.
  • It is an example of land being returned to Indigenous stewardship.

What do others say?How it was seen at the time, and where historians disagree

At the time

The Navy argued that Kahoʻolawe was essential for military training and national security. Some politicians and members of the public saw the activists as trespassers. Supporters saw the bombing as the desecration of a sacred place and a symbol of colonial domination.

Historians' and commentators' debates

  • Sovereignty or stewardship? Some activists saw Kahoʻolawe as the first step towards Hawaiian sovereignty; others focused on cultural restoration and land protection.
  • Military presence: the struggle raised wider questions about the large US military presence in Hawaiʻi, which continue in campaigns over sites such as Pōhakuloa and the Red Hill fuel tanks.
  • The cost: the deaths of Helm and Mitchell remain a painful part of the story, and the clean-up left much of the island still contaminated.

A 500-ton TNT explosion on Kahoʻolawe during the Navy's Operation Sailor Hat, which tested the effects of blasts on warships. Photographs like this help explain why Native Hawaiians saw the island's use as a target range as the desecration of sacred land.

Reading it

A 500-ton TNT explosion on Kahoʻolawe during a Navy test of blast effects on warships. The Navy took the photograph as a record of an experiment. To Native Hawaiians the same image showed sacred land being blown apart — the gap between those two readings is the whole dispute.

How is it remembered?Memorials, anniversaries and arguments over memory
  • George Helm and Kimo Mitchell are honoured as heroes of the Hawaiian movement.
  • The Protect Kahoʻolawe ʻOhana continues to organise trips to the island for restoration and ceremony.
  • The struggle is celebrated in Hawaiian music and literature.
