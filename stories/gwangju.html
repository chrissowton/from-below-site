The Gwangju Uprising

Students and citizens in Gwangju rose against a military coup. Paratroopers killed hundreds, but the uprising became the foundation of South Korean democracy.

When
18 – 27 May 1980
Where
Gwangju, South Jeolla Province, South Korea

See it on the map →

01

What is the background to this event?

From 1961 to 1979 South Korea was ruled by the authoritarian President Park Chung Hee. Rapid industrial growth was achieved alongside harsh repression of trade unions, students and the political opposition. The south-western Jeolla region, including its capital Gwangju, was widely seen as having been neglected by a government that favoured Park's home region.

In October 1979 Park was assassinated by the head of his own intelligence service. A brief period of hope, known as the "Seoul Spring", followed, but power was quickly seized by the military:

  • On 12 December 1979, Major General Chun Doo-hwan, head of the Defense Security Command, took control of the army in a coup.
  • In spring 1980 students and workers protested for democracy; on 15 May more than 100,000 people demonstrated at Seoul Station.
  • On 17 May Chun extended martial law to the whole country, closed universities, banned political activity, censored the press and arrested opposition leaders, including Kim Dae-jung, who came from the Jeolla region.

Who were the key people?

  • The students of Chonnam National University

    Who gathered at their closed university gates on the morning of 18 May to protest.

  • Kim Gyeong-cheol

    A 29-year-old deaf man, clubbed to death by paratroopers on 18 May — the first known fatality.

  • The taxi and bus drivers

    Who led a convoy to the Provincial Office on 20 May after seeing paratroopers beat passengers and drivers.

  • The citizens' militia

    Ordinary residents who took up arms after troops fired on crowds, and who held the Provincial Office until 27 May.

  • Yun Sang-won

    The spokesman of the citizens' militia, killed in the final assault on the Provincial Office.

  • Jürgen Hinzpeter

    A German television journalist who smuggled footage of the massacre out of the country.

  • Chun Doo-hwan

    The general who ordered the crackdown and became President later in 1980.

03

What happened?

On the morning of 18 May 1980, around 200 students gathered at the gates of Chonnam National University to protest its closure. Paratroopers of the Special Warfare Command charged them, and the protest moved to the city centre, in front of the South Jeolla Provincial Office.1 The paratroopers attacked protesters and bystanders with extreme brutality; the first known person killed was Kim Gyeong-cheol, a deaf man who had not been taking part.1

The violence turned the city against the troops:1

  • On 20 May hundreds of taxis led a convoy of buses and trucks to the Provincial Office in protest. Soldiers beat and arrested drivers, and troops fired on crowds near Gwangju Station.
  • On 21 May, with the main street filled with ordinary citizens, the army opened fire on a crowd in front of the Provincial Office, causing many casualties.
  • Some citizens then armed themselves from police stations and armouries, formed militias and fought the troops, who withdrew to the edge of the city.

For several days Gwangju was run by its citizens. Settlement committees of preachers, lawyers and professors negotiated with the army, demanding the release of those arrested and compensation for victims.1 Meanwhile, the military government cut off communications and claimed that the uprising was a riot instigated by "communist sympathizers" acting for North Korea — a claim for which there was no evidence.1

In the early hours of 27 May, troops re-entered the city. Members of the citizens' settlement committee tried to block their advance by lying down in the streets, and militia members made a last stand at the Provincial Office, where many were killed.1 The government at the time claimed that around 165 civilians had died; scholars today estimate that between 600 and 2,300 people were killed.1

04

What happened next?

  • Dictatorship: Chun Doo-hwan became President in September 1980 and ruled until 1988. Discussion of Gwangju was suppressed, but students and activists kept its memory alive.
  • Democracy: mass protests in June 1987 forced the government to accept direct presidential elections, marking South Korea's transition to democracy.
  • Justice: in 1996 Chun and his successor Roh Tae-woo were convicted of mutiny, treason and corruption; Chun was sentenced to death, commuted to life imprisonment, and both were pardoned in 1997.
  • Recognition: 18 May became a national day of commemoration in 1997, and a national cemetery was built for the victims. In 2020 an independent Truth Commission began a new investigation.
05

What do others say?

At the time

The military government described the uprising as a riot instigated by communists and North Korean agents, and controlled the press to present that version. Within South Korea, this account went largely unchallenged in public until the late 1980s.

Historians' debates

  • The death toll: official figures and independent estimates differ greatly, and the fate of many missing people is still unknown.
  • The United States' role: historians and journalists such as Tim Shorrock have documented that the US government approved the use of Korean troops under joint command to retake the city; the extent of American responsibility remains debated and has shaped anti-American sentiment in Korea.
  • Who gave the orders to fire? This question was a central focus of later investigations, including the 2020 Truth Commission.
  • Denial: some far-right groups continue to claim, without evidence, that North Korean agents were involved — claims rejected by official investigations.
06

The evidence

  • Footage smuggled out by foreign journalists, especially Jürgen Hinzpeter.
  • Photographs, diaries, leaflets and citizens' statements, collected in archives inscribed on UNESCO's Memory of the World Register in 2011.
  • Military records, many released only after democratisation, and the findings of parliamentary hearings in 1988–89 and trials in 1995–97.
  • Testimonies of survivors, families and former soldiers.
07

How is it remembered?

  • The May 18th National Cemetery holds the graves of victims, and 18 May is a national memorial day.
  • The former Provincial Office is now part of the Asian Culture Complex, after campaigns to preserve the building.
  • The uprising is the subject of the film A Taxi Driver (2017) and of Han Kang's novel Human Acts (2014); Han Kang received the Nobel Prize in Literature in 2024.
  • The song "March for the Beloved", written in memory of the uprising, became an anthem of the democracy movement.
08

Why does it matter?

The Gwangju Uprising is the foundational event of South Korean democracy. Ordinary people — students, taxi drivers, shopkeepers, housewives — resisted a military seizure of power at enormous cost, and their sacrifice became a moral reference point for the democracy movement that eventually succeeded in 1987.

  • It showed how state violence against a civilian population can transform a city's politics overnight.
  • The days of citizen self-government have become an important example of grassroots democracy under pressure.
  • The struggle over its memory, including attempts to deny or distort it, continues in Korean politics today.

Questions to think about

  1. Why did the violence of the troops turn the whole city against them?
  2. What can we learn from the days when Gwangju was run by its citizens?
  3. Why does it matter who gave the orders to fire, more than forty years later?
  4. How do governments try to control the memory of events like Gwangju, and how do people resist?
