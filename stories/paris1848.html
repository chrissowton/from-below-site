Paris 1848: The February Revolution and the June Days

In three days of February 1848, the people of Paris overthrew their king and founded a republic that gave the vote to all men and abolished slavery. Four months later, the same republic crushed the workers who had made it.

When

01

What is the background to this event?

Since 1830, France had been ruled by King Louis Philippe, under a constitution that gave the vote to only a small, wealthy minority.

  • The government of François Guizot refused to widen the vote.1
  • Unemployment was high, and writers such as Louis Blanc called for "the right to work".1
  • Reformers held "banquets" to campaign for change; in February 1848 the government banned them.1

03

What happened?

On 22 February 1848, a large protest against the Guizot government began in Paris.1

  • Crowds built the first barricades and fought the Municipal Guard.1
  • After intense street fighting, the crowds took control of the capital.1
  • On 24 February, King Louis Philippe abdicated, and a Provisional Government proclaimed the Second Republic.1
  • The Republic brought in the vote for all adult men.3
  • On 27 April, it abolished slavery in all French colonies.4
  • The news from Paris sparked revolutions across Europe.1
04

What happened next?

  • The June Days: the government set up National Workshops to give work to the unemployed, then closed them. In June, Paris workers rose again.5
  • Repression: General Cavaignac crushed the rising. About 1,500 troops and about 3,000 insurgents were killed or injured, and 4,000 insurgents were deported to Algeria.5
  • Reaction: in December 1848, Louis-Napoléon Bonaparte was elected president; in 1851 he seized power in a coup.
05

What do others say?

At the time

Liberals wanted a republic with political rights; many workers wanted a "democratic and social republic" with the right to work.5

Debates since

  • Two revolutions: historians often see February and June as a turning point at which middle-class republicans and workers split.5
  • Violence on both sides: both the February and June risings involved armed fighting. In June, the Archbishop of Paris was killed trying to make peace; the repression by the army was far bloodier.5
06

The evidence

What is it?
Public proclamation
Made by, and when
Provisional Government of the French Republic, 1848

A printed proclamation of the new republic, pasted on walls for people to read. Proclamations like this announced the republic's decisions — including the end of slavery in the French colonies.

07

How is it remembered?

  • Universal male suffrage, introduced in 1848, survived in France.3
08

Why does it matter?

Paris 1848 shows how a revolution made in the streets could win great reforms — and how quickly its leaders could turn on the workers who made it.

Open questions

  1. Why did a ban on political banquets lead to revolution?
  2. Which reforms of 1848 lasted?
  3. Why did the Republic turn against the Paris workers in June?
  4. Why did news from Paris spread so quickly across Europe?

What other events are like this?

  • 1848Palermo Rises: The First Revolution of 1848 — On 12 January 1848, the people of Palermo rose against their Bourbon king. It was the first of the revolutions that swept Europe that year, and Sicily governed itself for sixteen months under one of the most democratic constitutions of its time.
  • 1871The Paris Commune — After defeat in war, the working people of Paris took over their city and governed it for 72 days. The Commune ended in 'Bloody Week', when the army killed thousands — and Communards shot their hostages.
  • 184822 May 1848: The Enslaved of Martinique Free Themselves — France had decreed the end of slavery, but the news had not arrived. When an enslaved drummer was jailed, thousands marched on Saint-Pierre — and forced the governor to declare abolition at once.
