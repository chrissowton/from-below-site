The Chilembwe Uprising

A Baptist minister educated in the United States led a short-lived rising against forced labour, racism and the recruitment of Africans to die in Britain's war.

When
23 January – February 1915
Where
Chiradzulu and Magomero, Nyasaland (Malawi)

See it on the map →

01

What is the background to this event?

The region around Lake Nyasa became a British protectorate in 1891, and was named Nyasaland in 1907. In the fertile Shire Highlands, European settlers established large plantations growing cotton and tobacco.1

  • Africans living on the estates were subject to thangata, a system in which tenants had to work for the estate owner as rent, and to pay "hut tax". In practice it often became forced labour.1
  • The largest estate, at Magomero, belonged to the A. L. Bruce Estates and was managed by William Jervis Livingstone, a relative of the famous explorer, who was notorious for his harsh treatment of workers. The estate burned down churches that Chilembwe's followers had built on its land.1
  • When the First World War broke out in 1914, Nyasaland became a base for the campaign against German East Africa. Thousands of Africans were recruited as soldiers and porters, and many died.1

John Chilembwe, born around 1871, had been inspired by the radical English missionary Joseph Booth. In 1897 he travelled to the United States, where he studied at the Virginia Theological Seminary and College and mixed in African-American circles, learning about the abolitionist John Brown and the educator Booker T. Washington. In 1900 he returned to found his own church, the Providence Industrial Mission, at Mbombwe, with schools and model farms.1

02

Who were the key people?

  • John Chilembwe

    Baptist minister and founder of the Providence Industrial Mission, who led the uprising. He was shot dead by a police patrol on 3 February 1915.

  • John Gray Kufa

    A medical assistant and church elder, one of Chilembwe's closest lieutenants, who was executed after the rising.

  • David Kaduya

    A former soldier who led the attack on Magomero and was later publicly executed.

  • William Jervis Livingstone

    The manager of the Magomero estate, killed by the rebels.

  • The rebels

    Members of Chilembwe's church and their supporters — many of them educated, landowning Africans — and estate workers.

03

What happened?

In November 1914, after reports of heavy African losses in the fighting at Karonga, Chilembwe wrote a letter to the Nyasaland Times protesting against the recruitment of Africans to fight in a war that was not theirs. He suggested that the war should be fought by white settlers, traders and missionaries, "who are, indeed, of much value and who also know the cause of this war". The letter was censored before it could be fully published.1

From 1914, Chilembwe's sermons became more militant, drawing on Old Testament stories of the Israelites' escape from slavery in Egypt. On the evening of 23 January 1915, his followers launched the uprising:1

  • Rebels attacked the headquarters of the A. L. Bruce Estates at Magomero and killed three white men, including William Jervis Livingstone, whose head was cut off and taken to Chilembwe's church. Women and children on the estate were taken captive but released unharmed.
  • A smaller group attacked a store in Blantyre to seize weapons and ammunition.
  • The rising received little support beyond Chilembwe's own followers.

By the morning of 24 January, the colonial authorities had mobilised the settler Volunteer Reserve and called in regular troops of the King's African Rifles. The rebels' attacks on government forces failed, and Chilembwe's church at Mbombwe was captured and blown up.1 Many rebels, including Chilembwe, fled towards Portuguese Mozambique. On 3 February 1915, Chilembwe was shot dead by a police patrol near the border.1 Around 40 rebels were executed and 300 imprisoned.1

04

What happened next?

  • Repression: independent African churches were suppressed, and the colonial authorities became deeply suspicious of educated Africans.
  • A commission of inquiry: an official commission in 1916 blamed Chilembwe's "fanaticism" but also criticised the abuses of the thangata system and the conduct of the Magomero estate; some limited reforms followed.
  • Nationalism: after the Second World War, Malawian nationalists rediscovered Chilembwe as a forerunner of their struggle. Malawi became independent in 1964.
05

What do others say?

At the time

The colonial authorities and settlers blamed the rising on religious fanaticism and on "Ethiopianism" — independent African churches — and on the influence of African-American ideas. Some missionaries argued that the abuses on the estates were the real cause.

Historians' debates

  • What did Chilembwe intend? Some historians believe he planned a general rising to overthrow colonial rule; others that he expected to fail and intended a symbolic sacrifice. He is reported to have urged his followers to "strike a blow and die".
  • Millenarianism: George Shepperson and Thomas Price's classic study explored the influence of millenarian ideas, including Watch Tower predictions of Armageddon in 1914.
  • The killing of Livingstone: historians discuss how the violence at Magomero, aimed at a notoriously cruel manager, fits Chilembwe's Christian beliefs and his aims.
06

The evidence

  • The report of the official commission of inquiry (1916) and colonial records.
  • Chilembwe's letter to the Nyasaland Times, and his correspondence with supporters in the United States.
  • Photographs of Chilembwe, his family and his church, and of the aftermath of the rising.
07

How is it remembered?

  • 15 January is observed as John Chilembwe Day, a public holiday in Malawi.
  • Chilembwe's portrait appears on Malawian banknotes.
  • A church has been rebuilt at Mbombwe on the site of the Providence Industrial Mission.
08

Why does it matter?

The Chilembwe uprising was small and quickly crushed, but it has become one of the most important events in Malawian history. It was a protest by educated African Christians against the injustice of colonial rule — forced labour, racial discrimination and the use of African lives in a European war.

  • It shows how Christianity, education and connections with African Americans could inspire resistance to colonialism.
  • It was one of a number of risings across Africa during the First World War against wartime recruitment.
  • Chilembwe is now honoured as a national hero in Malawi.

Questions to think about

  1. Why do you think Chilembwe objected so strongly to the recruitment of Africans for the war?
  2. How might his time in the United States have shaped his ideas?
  3. Was the uprising a failure, or a symbolic success?
  4. Why did Malawian nationalists later turn Chilembwe into a national hero?

What other events are like this?

  • 1905The Maji Maji War — Forced to grow cotton for German colonial rulers, peoples across southern Tanzania united behind a sacred water medicine and rose in one of Africa's largest anti-colonial wars.
  • 1916The 1916 Central Asian Revolt (Ürkün) — When the Tsar ordered Central Asian men to be conscripted for labour behind the front lines, the region rose in revolt. The repression, and the flight across the mountains to China, cost well over a hundred thousand lives.
  • 1952The Mau Mau Uprising — An armed rebellion over land and freedom was met with mass detention and torture. Decades later, the British government paid compensation to survivors.
