The Donghak Peasant Revolution

Korean peasants inspired by a new religion rose against corrupt officials, briefly governed their province through peasant councils, and then fought the Japanese army.

When
1894 – 1895
Where
Jeolla Province, Korea

See it on the map →

01

What is the background to this event?

In the late nineteenth century, Korea was ruled by the Joseon dynasty. Its government faced growing problems at home and pressure from abroad:1

  • Local officials, many of whom had bought their posts, extorted heavy taxes and labour from the peasants, and the rural poor suffered from debt, famine and the exploitation of landlords.
  • After Korea was forced to open its ports in 1876, Japanese and other foreign merchants bought up rice for export, pushing up prices.
  • China (under the Qing dynasty) and Japan competed for influence over the Korean court.

In 1860 Choe Je-u founded a new religion, Donghak ("Eastern Learning"), which combined elements of Confucianism, Buddhism, Daoism and Korean folk belief, and taught that every person carried the divine within them. The government banned it and executed Choe in 1864, but Donghak spread rapidly among the peasants of the south.1 In 1892–93, Donghak believers held mass petitions demanding that the ban be lifted — at Boeun in 1893, some 80,000 gathered under banners calling for the expulsion of Westerners and Japanese.1

02

Who were the key people?

  • Jeon Bong-jun

    A village schoolteacher and local Donghak leader in Gobu, known as the 'Mung Bean General' because of his small stature. He led the revolution and was executed in 1895.

  • Kim Gae-nam and Son Hwa-jung

    Donghak leaders who commanded peasant armies alongside Jeon.

  • Choe Si-hyeong

    The second leader of the Donghak religion, who at first opposed armed revolt but later joined the second rising.

  • Jo Byeong-gap

    The magistrate of Gobu, whose extortion sparked the first rising.

  • The peasant armies

    Tens of thousands of peasants, many of them Donghak believers, from the provinces of the south-west.

03

What happened?

The immediate cause of the rising was the conduct of Jo Byeong-gap, the magistrate of Gobu county, who imposed harsh taxes and forced peasants to build a reservoir and then charged them for the water.1 In January 1894, a thousand rebels gathered and seized the county office.1

The rising grew into a revolution in the spring:1

  • Under Jeon Bong-jun, with Kim Gae-nam and Son Hwa-jung as generals, the rebels occupied Taein and Buan and defeated government troops at Hwangtohyeon.
  • In late May they captured Jeonju, the provincial capital. The alarmed government asked China for troops; Japan sent its own forces in response.
  • The government agreed a truce, the Treaty of Jeonju, and accepted many of the rebels' demands.

The rebels then established peasant councils (jipgangso) in most of the towns of Jeolla Province, which took over much of local government. Their reform programme included demands to:1

  • punish corrupt officials and cruel landlords;
  • free all slaves and burn the slave registers;
  • legalise the remarriage of widows;
  • cancel unjust taxes and debts, and distribute land equally for cultivation.

Meanwhile Japan used the crisis to go to war with China. In the summer of 1894 Japanese troops occupied the royal palace in Seoul and defeated the Chinese army. In the autumn, fearing that Korea would fall under Japanese control, the Donghak leaders rose again, with an army that may have numbered tens of thousands.1 They marched on Gongju, but at the Ugeumchi pass they were decisively defeated by Japanese and Korean government troops armed with modern rifles.1 Jeon Bong-jun was betrayed and captured in December 1894, tried in Seoul and executed in 1895, with other leaders.1

04

What happened next?

  • Massacre: Japanese and government troops hunted down Donghak followers across the south. Estimates of the number of people killed in the revolution and its suppression range from tens of thousands to several hundred thousand.
  • Reforms from above: under Japanese pressure, the Korean government passed the Gabo Reforms (1894–96), which abolished slavery and the legal class system and allowed widows to remarry — echoing some of the rebels' demands.
  • Japanese domination: Japan's victory over China in 1895 removed Chinese influence from Korea. Japan made Korea a protectorate in 1905 and annexed it in 1910.
  • Religion and independence: Donghak became Cheondogyo ("Religion of the Heavenly Way"), whose leaders played a central role in the March First independence movement of 1919.
05

What do others say?

At the time

The Joseon government and Confucian elites condemned the rebels as heretics and bandits. Japanese officials presented their intervention as restoring order. The rebels described themselves as a "righteous army".

Historians' debates

  • Peasant war or religious rebellion? Historians debate how far the movement was driven by the Donghak religion and how far by peasant economic grievances; many argue the religion provided the organisation for a social movement.
  • Anti-foreign or reformist? Some emphasise the rebels' hostility to Japan and the West; others stress their programme of internal social reform.
  • How radical? The famous list of twelve reform demands survives in a later account, and historians debate exactly what the rebels demanded at the time — for example, how radical their proposals on land were.
06

The evidence

  • The rebels' proclamations and the records of Jeon Bong-jun's interrogation and trial.
  • Korean government records and the diaries of officials.
  • Japanese military and diplomatic records, and photographs, including that of Jeon Bong-jun under arrest.
  • Folk songs and oral traditions in the south-west of Korea.
07

How is it remembered?

  • In 2004, a special law was passed in South Korea to restore the honour of those who took part in the revolution.
  • Since 2019, 11 May — the anniversary of the victory at Hwangtohyeon — has been a national day of commemoration.
  • Jeon Bong-jun is honoured with statues and a memorial at his birthplace, and the folk song about the 'blue bird' is widely understood to refer to him.
08

Why does it matter?

The Donghak Peasant Revolution was the largest peasant uprising in Korean history. Peasants demanded the end of slavery, corruption and inequality, governed parts of their own province through peasant councils, and then took up arms against foreign domination.

  • It combined the struggle for social justice at home with resistance to imperialism.
  • It helped to trigger the First Sino-Japanese War, which transformed the balance of power in East Asia.
  • It has inspired later Korean democratic and popular movements, from 1919 to the struggles against military rule.

What other events are like this?

  • 1980The Gwangju Uprising — Students and citizens in Gwangju rose against a military coup. Paratroopers killed hundreds, but the uprising became the foundation of South Korean democracy.
  • AD 184The Yellow Turban Rebellion — Inspired by a Daoist healer who promised a new age of 'Great Peace', hundreds of thousands of peasants rose against the Han dynasty — a revolt that helped to bring the empire down.
  • 1911Zapata and the Plan of Ayala — The peasant revolutionary Emiliano Zapata declared that the land, woods and waters taken from Mexico's villages must be returned — a demand that shaped the Mexican Revolution.
