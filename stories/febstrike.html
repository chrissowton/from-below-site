Civil rights & anti-racism

The February Strike

After German police seized 425 Jewish men in Amsterdam, the city's tram drivers, dockers and factory workers walked out. It was the first mass public protest against the persecution of Jews in Nazi-occupied Europe.

When
25 – 26 February 1941
Where
Amsterdam and nearby towns, the Netherlands
Facts checked
2026-11-16

See it on the map →

01

What is the background to this event?

Germany occupied the Netherlands in May 1940. Anti-Jewish measures began within weeks; in November 1940, Jews were dismissed from all public posts.1

  • Dutch Nazis of the NSB and its militia, the WA, staged provocations in Amsterdam's Jewish quarter.1
  • Jewish self-defence groups and their neighbours fought back. In a street battle on 11 February 1941, a WA man, Hendrik Koot, was fatally injured.1
  • The Germans sealed off the Jewish quarter. On 22–23 February, they seized 425 Jewish men aged 20 to 35 in a violent round-up.1
  • Shipyard workers were also angry at the threat of forced labour in Germany.1

In their own words

03

What happened?

The outlawed Communist Party of the Netherlands called a strike in protest at the round-ups, spreading the call by pamphlet: "Strike! Strike! Strike!"1

  • On 25 February 1941, Amsterdam's tram drivers stopped work. Other city services, dockers, factories, shops such as De Bijenkorf, and schools followed.1
  • The strike spread to Zaanstad, Haarlem and the Kennemerland, Hilversum and Utrecht.1
  • By 26 February, about 300,000 people had joined.1
  • German forces put down the strike with gunfire and arrests. Nine people were killed and many wounded. By 27 February it was largely over.12
  • Four strikers were later executed, 22 were imprisoned, and Amsterdam was fined 15 million guilders. Communist organisers were also shot.1

04

What happened next?

  • No rescue: the strike did not stop the persecution. Deportations began in 1942, and about three-quarters of the Jews of the Netherlands — more than 100,000 people — were murdered.
  • Warnings: the Germans threatened severe reprisals for any repeat, and no comparable mass protest in defence of Jews followed.1
  • Later strikes: large strikes against the occupation took place in 1943 and 1944.
05

What do others say?

At the time

The Germans blamed Jews and Communists; the Dutch authorities and police largely carried out German orders.2

Debates since

  • Who owns the memory? During the Cold War, Communists and others commemorated the strike separately, because of the party's leading role.1
  • Pride and shame: historians warn against using the strike to hide how many Dutch officials and citizens later assisted, or did not resist, the deportations.
  • Jewish resistance: the street fighting by Jewish self-defence groups before the strike is often overlooked.1

How is it remembered?

  • Every 25 February, people file past the statue De Dokwerker (1952) in Amsterdam's old Jewish quarter.1
08

Why does it matter?

The February Strike is regarded as the first public protest against the Nazis' treatment of Jews in occupied Europe — and one of very few.1

  • It was a strike by non-Jews in solidarity with their Jewish neighbours.
  • It also shows the limits of protest against a regime prepared to use unlimited force.

Questions to think about

  1. Why did workers risk their lives to strike?
  2. Why did the strike not prevent the deportations?
  3. Why was there no second strike in defence of the Jews?
  4. How should the Netherlands remember both the strike and the fate of its Jews?

What other events are like this?

  • 1936The Battle of Cable Street — When the British Union of Fascists tried to march through the Jewish East End, tens of thousands of local people blocked the way.
  • 1984Lesbians and Gays Support the Miners — During the great miners' strike, a group of lesbians and gay men in London raised money for a mining community in South Wales. The solidarity was returned: miners marched at Pride, and their union backed gay rights.
  • 1919The Winnipeg General Strike — For six weeks, 30,000 workers brought Canada's third-largest city to a halt to win the right to collective bargaining. It ended with 'Bloody Saturday' — and transformed Canadian politics.
