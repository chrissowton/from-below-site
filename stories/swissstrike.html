The Swiss Women's Strike

'If women want, everything stands still.' Ten years after equality was written into the constitution with little effect, half a million Swiss women stopped work, at home and in the workplace.

When
14 June 1991
Where
Bern, Zürich, Geneva and across Switzerland
Facts checked
2026-11-16

See it on the map →

01

What is the background to this event?

Swiss women won the right to vote in national elections only in 1971. One canton, Appenzell Innerrhoden, held out until a court ruling in 1990.

  • On 14 June 1981, voters approved a constitutional article on equal rights for women and men, including equal pay.1
  • Ten years later, little had changed. Women were still paid far less than men.1
  • Women watchmakers in the Vallée de Joux, who suffered an unusually large pay gap, proposed a strike.1

03

What happened?

Christiane Brunner, of the metal and watch workers' union, took the watchmakers' idea to the Swiss Trade Union Federation. Its board backed it by a narrow majority, despite resistance from some male trade unionists.1

  • The main federation of women's associations publicly opposed the plan.1
  • On 14 June 1991, about half a million women took part in strikes and protests, under the motto "If women want, everything stands still".1
  • Because strikes were rare and legally risky in Switzerland, many women took long breaks, hung out brooms and banners, wore purple, or gathered in town squares.
  • It was the largest public mobilisation in Switzerland since the general strike of 1918.1

04

What happened next?

  • 1993: when parliament passed over Christiane Brunner for a seat in the government, protests helped ensure that another woman, Ruth Dreifuss, was elected.
  • Equality Act: a law to enforce equality at work came into force in 1996.
  • Later reforms: paid maternity leave followed in 2005.
  • 2019: a second women's strike, again on 14 June, drew hundreds of thousands.
05

What do others say?

At the time

Employers and some politicians said a strike was un-Swiss and broke the tradition of "labour peace"; some women's groups preferred quieter lobbying.1

Debates since

  • Impact: the pay gap narrowed but did not close, which is why women struck again in 2019.
  • A strike? Historians debate whether a day of symbolic action counts as a strike.

How is it remembered?

  • 14 June is now a regular day of action for women's rights in Switzerland.
08

Why does it matter?

The strike showed that a right on paper means little until people insist on it — and that unpaid work at home is work too.

  • It united trade unionists and feminists.
  • It was entirely peaceful.

What other events are like this?

  • 1975Iceland's Women's Day Off — Around 90 per cent of Iceland's women stopped work — paid and unpaid — for a day, to show how much the country depended on them.
  • 1893Women Win the Vote in New Zealand — A nationwide petition campaign made New Zealand the first self-governing country in which all women could vote in parliamentary elections.
  • 1888The Matchwomen's Strike — About 1,400 women and girls walked out of a match factory, won their demands in three weeks, and founded the largest union of women in Britain.
