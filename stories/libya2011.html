Benghazi Rises: The Libyan Uprising

It began with families of murdered prisoners protesting the arrest of their lawyer. Within ten days, eastern Libya had broken free of Muammar Gaddafi's 42-year rule — and the country was sliding into civil war.

When
15 – 27 February 2011
Where
Benghazi, Bayda and towns across eastern Libya
Facts checked

01

What is the background to this event?

Muammar Gaddafi had ruled Libya for more than 40 years. In February 2011, opponents inside Libya and in exile called for a "Day of Revolt" on 17 February.12

  • The date recalled protests in Benghazi on 17 February 2006, which had begun over cartoons of the Prophet Muhammad and turned against Gaddafi.2
  • In 1996, more than 1,000 prisoners had been killed by security forces in Abu Salim prison. Their families had long campaigned for the truth.3

In their own words

03

What happened?

On 15 February 2011, about 200 people, many of them relatives of the Abu Salim prisoners, protested outside police headquarters in Benghazi after the arrest of their lawyer, Fathi Terbil. Police broke up the protest violently.32

  • Protests spread to Bayda, Zintan and other towns, where crowds called for the end of the regime and set fire to police buildings.2
  • On 17 February, the "Day of Revolt", protests grew. By then, according to figures compiled by the news agency AFP, at least 41 people had been killed.2
  • In Benghazi, protesters took control of the streets, seized weapons from the main security headquarters and took over the radio station, broadcasting as the "Voice of Free Libya".2
  • By 25 February, most of eastern Libya was reported to be under the control of protesters and rebel forces. Crowds gathered daily outside the courthouse in Benghazi.2
  • On 27 February, a National Transitional Council was formed in Benghazi.2
04

What happened next?

  • War: Gaddafi's forces retook many towns in March. On 17 March, the UN Security Council authorised "all necessary measures" to protect civilians, and NATO-led air strikes followed.21
  • The end of Gaddafi: on 20 October 2011, Gaddafi was captured and killed in Sirte.1
  • Lasting conflict: Libya did not become stable, and a second civil war broke out in 2014.5
05

Why does it matter?

The Libyan uprising began from below, with families demanding justice for murdered prisoners. It shows how quickly a protest movement can become an armed revolt — and how foreign intervention can change its course.31

  • The UN Security Council authorised 'all necessary measures' to protect civilians, and NATO-led air strikes followed.21
  • Libya did not become stable, and a second civil war broke out in 2014.5
  • The lawyer whose arrest sparked the protests later won a human rights prize and sat on the transitional council.3

What do others say?How it was seen at the time, and where historians disagree

At the time

Protesters called for the end of the regime; the government released prisoners and organised rallies of its supporters while its forces fired on demonstrators.21

Debates since

  • Intervention: supporters of the UN-authorised air campaign argue that it protected civilians in Benghazi; critics argue that it went beyond protecting civilians and helped leave Libya unstable.
  • Violence: the International Criminal Court's prosecutor estimated that 500–700 people were killed by security forces in February 2011, before the rebels took up arms. Estimates for the whole war range from 1,914–3,466 (Uppsala Conflict Data Program) to about 30,000 (the rebel health minister). Both sides committed abuses.1
