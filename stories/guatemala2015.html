"Resign!": Guatemala's Protests Bring Down a President

When UN-backed investigators exposed a customs fraud ring at the top of government, tens of thousands of Guatemalans gathered in the capital's main square to demand resignations. Within five months, the vice-president and the president had resigned and been arrested.

01

What is the background to this event?

Since an agreement signed in 2006, a UN-backed body, the International Commission against Impunity in Guatemala (CICIG), had been helping Guatemala's prosecutors investigate organised crime and corruption.3

  • The president, Otto Pérez Molina, was a retired general who had campaigned in 2007 under the slogan "Mano dura, cabeza y corazón" ("A firm hand, head and heart").2
  • In April 2015, prosecutors and the CICIG revealed a customs fraud ring known as "La Línea", in which importers paid bribes in exchange for lower duties. The private secretary of the vice-president, Roxana Baldetti, was among those implicated.24

In their own words

03

What happened?

On 25 April 2015, between 20,000 and 30,000 people gathered in the Plaza de la Constitución in Guatemala City. They called for the resignation of the president and vice-president.1

  • The protest was peaceful, and children as well as adults took part. Protesters also called on voters to reject the presidential candidate Manuel Baldizón, whom they suspected of corruption.1
  • On 8 May, Vice-President Baldetti resigned. Several ministers also resigned.12
  • On 20 May, investigators revealed another case of large-scale corruption, at the Guatemalan Social Security Institute.4
  • Protests continued through the summer. On 21 August, Baldetti was arrested, and the chief prosecutor, Thelma Aldana, called for the president to be impeached.25
  • On 27 August, a national strike was held. Students, businesses, farmers and public institutions took part, and more than 170,000 people protested in the capital, according to the Spanish-language Wikipedia.4
  • On 1 September, Congress voted 132–0 to remove the president's immunity from prosecution. Pérez Molina resigned on 2 September and was arrested on 14 September.2

04

What happened next?

  • A new president: Jimmy Morales, a television comedian with no experience of government, won the 2015 election.6
  • Convictions: in December 2022, Pérez Molina and Baldetti were each sentenced to 16 years in prison. Pérez Molina was released on bail in January 2024.2
  • The CICIG expelled: in 2018, Morales, himself under investigation over his campaign finances, refused to renew the CICIG's mandate. It ended in 2019.3
05

What do others say?

At the time

Pérez Molina refused to resign for months, insisting he would serve out his term; protesters argued that he could not remain in office while under suspicion.1

Debates since

  • The street or the courts? How much credit for the president's fall belongs to the protesters, and how much to investigators, prosecutors and Congress, is a matter of debate.
  • Violence: the protests were peaceful, and our sources report no deaths.1

How is it remembered?

  • Later protests were smaller, as quick results proved hard to achieve, while the investigations continued.1
08

Why does it matter?

The protests of 2015 showed that peaceful mass protest, together with independent investigators, could bring down a sitting president. They also showed how much depended on independent investigators — and how quickly that could be reversed.13

Questions to think about

  1. Why did people of all ages and backgrounds join the protests?
  2. How important was the CICIG's investigation to the protests' success?
  3. Why might voters have chosen an outsider like Jimmy Morales afterwards?
  4. Why did the CICIG's work end?

What other events are like this?

  • 1944Guatemala's 'Ten Years of Spring' — Students, teachers and workers brought down a dictator. A decade of democracy and land reform followed — until a US-backed coup ended it.
  • 2019'Resist!' — Montenegro's Anti-Corruption Protests — A video of a businessman handing an envelope of cash to the mayor of Podgorica brought thousands into the streets every week. The protests did not bring down the government — but its 30-year rule ended at the ballot box the next year.
  • 2019Lebanon's 17 October Revolution — A tax on WhatsApp calls sparked a nationwide uprising against a political system built on sectarian division. 'All of them means all of them,' protesters chanted — demanding the removal of the entire political class.
