The Grunwick Strike

South Asian women workers walked out of a photo-processing factory in north London and became the centre of one of the biggest industrial disputes of the 1970s.

When
August 1976 – July 1978
Where
Grunwick Film Processing Laboratories, Dollis Hill, London
Facts checked

01

What is the background to this event?

In the late 1960s and early 1970s, many people of Indian origin who had settled in East Africa came to Britain, often after being expelled or pressured to leave newly independent countries such as Uganda and Kenya. Many found work in low-paid jobs in London's factories.

The Grunwick film-processing laboratories in Chapter Road, Willesden, developed holiday photographs sent in by post. Its workforce was mainly of Asian origin, many of them women:

  • Average pay at Grunwick was £28 a week, at a time when the average national wage was £72.
  • Workers described compulsory overtime, petty restrictions, and humiliating treatment by managers.
  • The company's application forms asked for passport numbers and "date of arrival in the UK".
  • There was no union.

The summer of 1976 was one of the hottest on record, and the factory's air-conditioning was not working.

In their own words

03

What happened?

On 20 August 1976, Jayaben Desai was told off for preparing to leave at the end of her shift. She walked out, telling the manager:

"What you are running is not a factory, it is a zoo … We are the lions, Mr Manager."Jayaben Desai to a Grunwick manager, 20 August 1976, as widely reported1

Others had walked out over the dismissal of a colleague that day. On 23 August six workers began picketing the factory, and on advice from the Trades Union Congress they joined the white-collar union APEX.1 More workers came out on strike, and on 2 September the company dismissed all 137 strikers.1

The dispute grew into a national cause:

  • Postal workers at Cricklewood sorting office refused to handle Grunwick's mail, threatening its business.
  • In June and July 1977, trade unionists from across Britain joined mass pickets at the factory. On some days thousands of people, including miners led by Arthur Scargill, faced large numbers of police.
  • There were 550 arrests during the strike — at the time the highest in any industrial dispute since the General Strike of 1926.1

The government set up a Court of Inquiry under Lord Scarman, which reported in August 1977 and recommended that the strikers be reinstated and the union recognised. George Ward refused to accept its recommendations.1 In November 1977 Jayaben Desai and other strikers went on hunger strike outside the TUC's headquarters, and APEX suspended them. The strike committee called off the dispute in June 1978.1

04

What happened next?

  • Defeat: the strikers did not win reinstatement or union recognition. The House of Lords ruled in favour of Grunwick in a related legal case, and the TUC withdrew its support.
  • Political impact: the dispute became a symbol for both left and right. The Conservative Party and its allies saw Ward's victory as a sign that trade union power could be resisted, foreshadowing the industrial policies of the 1980s.
  • Changing attitudes: the strike transformed the image of Asian women in British public life and showed that the trade union movement could unite behind workers of colour.
05

What do others say?

At the time

George Ward and his supporters argued that the strike was a political campaign by militant trade unionists against a small business, and that most Grunwick workers did not want a union. The mass pickets were widely criticised in the press for violence and intimidation. Supporters of the strikers pointed to police violence and to the company's refusal to accept the findings of the government's own inquiry.

Historians' debates

  • Did the unions let the strikers down? Many historians argue that the TUC and APEX abandoned the strikers when the dispute became politically inconvenient.
  • Race and gender: historians such as Amrit Wilson have emphasised that the strikers challenged both employers and the expectations of their own communities.
  • A turning point? Some historians see Grunwick as the beginning of the end of union strength in Britain; others stress its positive role in drawing Black and Asian workers into the labour movement.

How is it remembered?

  • For the 40th anniversary in 2016, the Grunwick 40 project held exhibitions and created a mural near Dollis Hill station.
  • A plaque commemorates Jayaben Desai, who died in 2010.
  • A mural on Soho Road in Handsworth, Birmingham, depicts Desai on the picket line.
08

Why does it matter?

Grunwick was the first major British dispute in which most of the strikers were from an ethnic minority and still received mass support from the wider labour movement. Earlier disputes involving immigrant workers in Leicester and Southall had largely been ignored or opposed by white trade unionists. Although the strike was lost, it changed how many people saw both trade unionism and South Asian women in Britain.

  • It challenged the stereotype of Asian women as passive and silent.
  • It showed both the power and the limits of solidarity within the labour movement.
  • It foreshadowed the bitter industrial conflicts of the 1980s.

What other events are like this?

  • 1888The Matchwomen's Strike — About 1,400 women and girls walked out of a match factory, won their demands in three weeks, and founded the largest union of women in Britain.
  • 1963The Bristol Bus Boycott — West Indian activists in Bristol organised a boycott of the city's buses to end a colour bar that kept Black and Asian people from working as bus crews.
  • 1980The Gdańsk Shipyard Strike and Solidarity — A strike over the sacking of a crane operator grew into the first independent trade union in the Soviet bloc, with ten million members.
