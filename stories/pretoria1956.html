Civil rights & anti-racism

The Women's March on the Union Buildings

Twenty thousand women of every race marched on the seat of South Africa's government to reject the pass laws — and stood for thirty minutes in silence.

When
9 August 1956
Where
Union Buildings, Pretoria, South Africa
Facts checked

01

What is the background to this event?

Under apartheid, introduced after the National Party won the election of 1948, every aspect of life in South Africa was segregated by race. One of the most hated instruments of control was the pass system:

  • Black African men had to carry a "pass" — a reference book — at all times, recording where they were allowed to live and work. Those who could not produce it could be arrested on the spot.
  • Hundreds of thousands of people were arrested every year under the pass laws.
  • In 1952 the government announced that passes would be extended to Black women. Women feared that they would be separated from their children and families, harassed by police and arrested.

Women had resisted passes before: in 1913, women in Bloemfontein had successfully protested against passes for women in the Orange Free State. In 1954, women from different organisations founded the Federation of South African Women (FEDSAW), which brought together the ANC Women's League and women of all races, and adopted a Women's Charter.1

In their own words

03

What happened?

FEDSAW organised a march to the Union Buildings in Pretoria, the seat of the government, to present a petition against the pass laws to the Prime Minister, J. G. Strijdom.1

On 9 August 1956, an estimated 20,000 women of all races arrived in Pretoria by train, bus and car. Because large processions had been banned, they walked to the Union Buildings in small groups of twos and threes.1 The march was led by Lilian Ngoyi, Helen Joseph, Rahima Moosa and Sophia Williams-De Bruyn:1

  • Representatives of each racial group carried bundles of petitions — some 14,000, with tens of thousands of signatures — to the Prime Minister's office.
  • Strijdom had made sure he was elsewhere; the petitions were received by his secretary.
  • The women then stood in complete silence for thirty minutes, before singing "Nkosi Sikelel' iAfrika" and a new freedom song composed for the occasion.

The petition declared that passes would mean homes broken up when women were arrested, and that an insult to African women was an insult to all women. It ended with a pledge that the women would not rest until they had won for their children "their fundamental rights of freedom, justice and security".1

The song the women sang addressed the Prime Minister directly, and gave the movement its most famous slogan: Wathint' abafazi, wathint' imbokodo — "You strike a woman, you strike a rock."1

04

What happened next?

  • The Treason Trial: in December 1956, 156 anti-apartheid leaders — including Lilian Ngoyi and Helen Joseph — were arrested and charged with treason. All were eventually acquitted in 1961.
  • Passes imposed: despite the protests, passes were extended to women. Women continued to resist, and burned passes during the campaigns of 1960, the year of the Sharpeville massacre.
  • Banning: Ngoyi and Joseph were banned for many years, unable to attend gatherings or leave their homes for long periods.
  • The end of the pass laws: the pass laws were finally abolished in 1986, and apartheid ended with the first democratic elections in 1994.
05

What do others say?

At the time

The government dismissed the march and the petition, and treated FEDSAW and its leaders as subversives. The march was nonetheless widely reported, and impressed many observers by its discipline and dignity.

Historians' debates

  • Women's rights or national liberation? Historians such as Cherryl Walker have discussed how far FEDSAW pursued women's own demands, and how far these were subordinated to the wider national struggle led by men.
  • Success or failure? The march did not stop passes for women, but historians stress its importance in building women's organisation and leadership.
  • Remembering the many: the four leaders are well remembered; historians have worked to recover the stories of the thousands of ordinary women who took part.

07

How is it remembered?

  • 9 August has been celebrated as National Women's Day, a public holiday in South Africa, since 1995.
  • In 2000, the monument Strike the Rock was unveiled at the Union Buildings. Climbing its steps triggers the words "You strike the woman, you strike the rock" in South Africa's eleven official languages.
  • Streets, buildings and ships in South Africa are named after the leaders of the march.
08

Why does it matter?

The Women's March of 1956 was one of the largest demonstrations in South African history, and a defining moment in the role of women in the struggle against apartheid. Women of every race acted together, at a time when the state was determined to divide them.

  • It showed women as leaders of the anti-apartheid movement, not just supporters.
  • Its slogan, "You strike a woman, you strike a rock", has become a symbol of women's strength across Africa and beyond.
  • The anniversary is now National Women's Day in South Africa.
