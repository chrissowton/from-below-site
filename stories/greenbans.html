The Green Bans

Construction workers refused to build. At the request of residents, the Builders Labourers Federation banned work on 54 projects that would have destroyed bushland, historic buildings and working-class homes — and saved much of old Sydney.

When
1971 – 1974
Where
Sydney, New South Wales, Australia
Facts checked

01

What is the background to this event?

In the early 1970s, a property boom in Sydney threatened parks, old working-class neighbourhoods and historic buildings with demolition and redevelopment.1

  • The New South Wales branch of the Builders Labourers Federation (BLF), led by Jack Mundey, Bob Pringle and Joe Owens, believed workers should have a say in what they built.12
  • The union also campaigned for women's right to work on building sites and for Aboriginal rights.2

03

What happened?

The first green ban protected Kelly's Bush, the last undeveloped bushland in Hunters Hill. Local women had appealed to the council and the state government without success.1

  • The BLF asked them to call a public meeting. Six hundred residents asked the union to stop construction.1
  • When the developer, A. V. Jennings, threatened to use non-union labour, BLF members stopped work on its other sites. The company gave up.1
  • Green bans were never imposed by the union alone, only at the request of residents' groups.1
  • Between 1971 and 1974, 54 bans were imposed in New South Wales.1
  • They saved nineteenth-century buildings in The Rocks, Sydney's oldest neighbourhood, and stopped the Royal Botanic Gardens being turned into a car park for the Opera House.1
  • Workers, residents and students occupied buildings at The Rocks in 1973; police arrested many.2

04

What happened next?

  • The end: in 1974, the federal BLF under Norm Gallagher took over the NSW branch and expelled its leaders. The green bans ended.1
  • Juanita Nielsen disappeared in 1975 while campaigning against a development; she is believed to have been murdered, and no one was convicted.
  • Heritage law: the bans helped bring about the NSW Heritage Act of 1977.
  • Influence: the idea spread to Germany, where Petra Kelly cited the green bans as an influence on the Green Party.
05

What do others say?

At the time

Developers and the state government accused the union of holding the city to ransom. Supporters said it was protecting the city for ordinary people.2

Debates since

  • Unions and democracy: critics ask whether unelected unions should decide what gets built; supporters reply that residents made the requests.1
  • Why it ended: historians debate how far the NSW branch was destroyed by developers, employers and its own federal union.2

07

How is it remembered?

  • Plaques at The Rocks and elsewhere mark the green bans.
  • A Sydney ferry was named after Jack Mundey, who died in 2020.
08

Why does it matter?

The green bans showed that workers could use their power not only for wages but for the environment and the community.

  • They united unions with middle-class residents and environmentalists.
  • They anticipated later ideas of a "just transition" between labour and the environment.

Questions to think about

  1. Why would construction workers refuse to build?
  2. Why did the union only act at the request of residents?
  3. What was saved by the green bans?
  4. Should unions take action over issues beyond pay and conditions?
