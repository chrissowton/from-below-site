The vote & democracy

Tonga's Democracy Movement

In the Pacific's last kingdom, commoners campaigned for decades for a parliament they could elect. Their struggle ended in riot and reform.

When
1980s – 2010
Where
Nukuʻalofa, Tongatapu, Tonga

See it on the map →

01

What is the background to this event?

Tonga is the only Pacific kingdom never formally colonised. Its 1875 constitution created a parliament, but power lay with the king and the nobles.

  • The king appointed the government. In parliament, 33 nobles elected nine members, and around 100,000 commoners elected only nine.
  • From the 1980s, a democracy movement grew, led by teachers, church leaders and civil servants.
  • ʻAkilisi Pōhiva, a teacher, co-founded a radio programme in 1981 and a newsletter, Keleʻa, in 1986, criticising corruption and the privileges of the elite.2
02

Who were the key people?

  • ʻAkilisi Pōhiva

    A teacher and leader of the democracy movement, elected to parliament in 1987, repeatedly prosecuted, and in 2014 the first commoner elected Prime Minister by parliament.

  • Public servants

    Civil servants whose six-week strike in 2005 strengthened the movement.

  • King Tāufaʻāhau Tupou IV and King George Tupou V

    Monarchs during the struggle; George Tupou V agreed to give up most of his powers.

03

What happened?

Pōhiva was dismissed from the civil service in 1984 for criticising the government; he sued and won. He was imprisoned in 1996 for contempt of parliament — later ruled unlawful — and charged with sedition in 2002, but acquitted by a jury.2

In 2005, a long strike by public servants over pay drew thousands into the streets and added to pressure for reform.

On 16 November 2006, as parliament was about to adjourn for the year without passing reforms, a crowd of democracy supporters gathered in the capital, Nukuʻalofa.1

  • Protest turned to riot. Rioters attacked government buildings, then businesses linked to the prime minister and the royal family, including the Royal Pacific hotel.1
  • Many Chinese-owned shops were looted and burned.1
  • Between 60 and 80 per cent of the central business district was destroyed. Eight bodies were found in burned buildings, probably of looters.1

The government declared a state of emergency, and Australian and New Zealand troops and police were sent at Tonga's request.1

04

What happened next?

  • Prosecutions: hundreds were charged over the riot. Pōhiva and other democracy MPs were charged with sedition, but the charges were later dropped.2
  • Reform: in 2008 King George Tupou V announced that he would give up most of his powers. In 2010, under a new system, 17 of 26 seats were elected by the people.
  • A commoner Prime Minister: in 2014, Pōhiva became the first commoner elected Prime Minister by parliament. He died in office in 2019.2
05

What do others say?

At the time

Defenders of the old system argued that the monarchy and nobility were central to Tongan culture and stability. Reformers argued that ordinary Tongans deserved an equal voice.

Debates since

  • The riot: some argued that the riot accelerated reform; others, including many democracy supporters, condemned the destruction and the attacks on Chinese-owned businesses, which looked like racist scapegoating.
  • Responsibility: the government blamed democracy leaders for inciting the crowd; they denied it, and the sedition charges were dropped.2
  • How democratic? Nobles still elect nine seats, and the king retains some powers; debate continues about further reform.
06

The evidence

  • Back issues of Keleʻa and other Tongan newspapers.
  • Reports of the Constitutional and Electoral Commission (2009).

07

How is it remembered?

  • Pōhiva is widely honoured as the father of Tongan democracy.
  • Central Nukuʻalofa was rebuilt after the riot, partly with Chinese loans.
08

Why does it matter?

Tonga's democracy movement shows how peaceful campaigning over decades — through journalism, courts, elections and strikes — can transform a political system. It also shows how frustration can explode into violence that harms innocent people.

  • It is one of the few cases of a monarchy giving up most of its power in the twenty-first century.
  • It raises questions about how to balance tradition and democracy.

Questions to think about

  1. Why do you think Tongans campaigned for democracy for so long?
  2. Did the riot help or harm the democracy movement?
  3. Why do you think Chinese-owned shops were targeted?
  4. Can a monarchy and democracy coexist?
