The Baptist War

A Baptist deacon called on enslaved workers to strike after Christmas until they were paid. Up to 60,000 people joined what became the largest slave rising in the British Caribbean — and it helped end slavery across the empire.

When
25 December 1831 – January 1832
Where
St James, near Montego Bay, Jamaica

See it on the map →

01

What is the background to this event?

In 1831, about 300,000 people were enslaved in Jamaica.1

  • Enslaved Baptists had followed the abolition movement in London.1
  • Black deacons led their own congregations, and read the Bible in terms of their own experience.1
  • Rumours spread that the king had already granted emancipation, and that planters were hiding it.1
02

Who were the key people?

  • Samuel Sharpe

    Enslaved Baptist deacon, known as 'Daddy' Sharpe, who inspired the rising; hanged in 1832.

  • Robert Gardner, Thomas Dove and George Taylor

    Among Sharpe's literate officers.

  • William Knibb

    Baptist missionary arrested and later a leading abolitionist.

  • Colonel William Grignon

    Planter and militia commander.

03

What happened?

Sharpe and his followers planned a peaceful general strike after Christmas, demanding freedom and a wage.1

  • Workers took an oath to stay away from work until their demands were met.1
  • On 27 December, the Kensington estate was burned, and the strike became a rising.1
  • Up to 60,000 enslaved people took part, and the rebels defeated the militia at first.1
  • The rising was crushed within about two weeks.1

04

What happened next?

  • Executions: courts executed hundreds of rebels, sometimes for stealing a pig or a cow; Sharpe was hanged in Montego Bay in May 1832.1
  • Missionaries: missionaries such as William Knibb were arrested, and white mobs destroyed Black chapels.1
  • Abolition: Parliament passed the Slavery Abolition Act in 1833; full emancipation came in 1838.1
05

What do others say?

At the time

Planters blamed missionaries; abolitionists in Britain were horrified by the reprisals.1

Debates since

  • Strike or revolt? Historians such as Mary Turner stress the rebels' demand for wages and the role of their own religious leaders.2
  • Violence on both sides: fourteen white people were killed and much property burned; over 300 rebels were killed in the fighting or executed.1
06

The evidence

  • Trial records and the Jamaican Assembly's report of 1832.1
  • The account of the missionary Henry Bleby.1
07

How is it remembered?

  • Samuel Sharpe is a National Hero of Jamaica, and Sam Sharpe Square in Montego Bay is named after him.
  • Andrea Levy's novel The Long Song (2010) is set in this period.1
08

Why does it matter?

The Baptist War played a major part in ending slavery in the British Empire.1

  • It began as a strike — a demand for wages by people who were not paid at all.

Questions to think about

  1. Why did Sharpe plan a strike rather than an armed rising?
  2. How did religion shape the rebels' ideas?
  3. How did the rising influence Parliament?
  4. Why is Sharpe a National Hero of Jamaica?
09

What other events are like this?

  • 1760Tacky's War — On Easter Monday 1760, enslaved Africans led by a former Fante chief rose to make Jamaica free. It was the greatest slave rebellion in the Caribbean before the Haitian Revolution.
  • 1816Bussa's Rebellion — On Easter Sunday 1816, enslaved people across south-east Barbados rose up — the largest slave revolt in the island's history, and the first of three great risings that shook British slavery.
  • 1811The German Coast Uprising — Enslaved people on the sugar plantations above New Orleans rose and marched on the city with drums and flags. It was the largest slave revolt in United States history, and it was crushed with mass executions.
