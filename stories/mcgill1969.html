McGill Français: Montreal Marches for French

In a mostly French-speaking province, the most prestigious university taught in English. Ten to fifteen thousand students, workers and nationalists marched on McGill demanding it become French — 'McGill for Quebecers! McGill for the workers!'

When
28 March 1969
Where
McGill University, Montreal, Quebec (Canada)
Facts checked

01

What is the background to this event?

In the 1960s, Quebec was going through the 'Quiet Revolution', a period of rapid modernisation and rising nationalism.1

  • Although most Quebecers spoke French, English-speakers held much economic power, and many saw McGill University as a symbol of Anglo-Saxon dominance.1
  • Only three of Quebec's six publicly funded universities taught in French, and French-speaking students had less access to higher education.1
  • A McGill lecturer, Stanley Gray, had founded a group of radical students and staff in 1967. He was dismissed in 1969.1

In their own words

03

What happened?

On 28 March 1969, between 10,000 and 15,000 people marched along Sherbrooke Street to the Roddick Gates of McGill University.1

  • They carried signs reading "McGill aux Québécois!" (McGill to Quebecers!) and "McGill aux travailleurs" (McGill to the workers).1
  • The marchers included trade unionists, nationalists, students and leftists, with many different demands, but their key aim was for McGill to become a French-speaking university.1
  • Fearing violence, the police deployed 100 officers with 1,300 more on call. About 3,000 spectators were waiting at the gates.1
  • The march was mostly peaceful, with some scuffles with English-speaking students who sang "God Save the Queen". Police arrested 96 students.1

04

What happened next?

  • A French university: less than a month later, the Université du Québec à Montréal (UQAM) was founded as a French-language public university in the city.1
  • McGill: McGill itself remained an English-language university.1
05

What do others say?

At the time

Marchers saw McGill as a colonial institution in a French-speaking society; some English-speaking students answered by singing "God Save the Queen".1

Debates since

  • Symbol or reality? Turning McGill French was popular mainly on the far left; there was much broader agreement on the need for more French-language public universities.1

How is it remembered?

08

Why does it matter?

The march made language a question of class and power: who gets access to education, and in which language. It was part of the wave of activism that changed the place of French in Quebec.1

Questions to think about

  1. Why was McGill seen as a symbol of English-speaking power?
  2. Why did trade unions join a march about a university?
  3. Was the march a success, even though McGill stayed English?
  4. How does Quebec compare with other places where a majority language was disadvantaged?
