Trinta di Mei: The Curaçao Uprising

A strike by oil workers turned into a day of rioting that burned the centre of Willemstad — and transformed politics, language and Black identity on the island.

When
30 May 1969
Where
Willemstad, Curaçao
Facts checked
2026-11-15

01

What is the background to this event?

Curaçao, in the southern Caribbean, was a Dutch colony with a long history of slavery, which was abolished only in 1863. After abolition, Black Curaçaoans continued to face racism and discrimination.1

  • Shell opened an oil refinery on the island in 1918. The oil boom made Curaçao one of the wealthiest islands in the region and attracted immigrants from the Caribbean, Suriname, Madeira and the Netherlands.1
  • From 1954, Curaçao was part of the Netherlands Antilles, a semi-autonomous country within the Kingdom of the Netherlands.1
  • In the 1960s, automation reduced the refinery's workforce dramatically: by 1969, Shell employed only around 4,000 people in Curaçao. Unemployment rose, and work was increasingly done by sub-contractors on lower wages.1
  • Economic power remained largely in the hands of a white, Dutch and local elite, while most of the Afro-Curaçaoan population was poor. Inspired by the Black Power movement, young radicals began to challenge this order.

In their own words

03

What happened?

In 1969, a labour dispute broke out between a Shell sub-contractor, Wescar, and its employees, who demanded the same pay as Shell's own workers. Other unions joined in solidarity, and on 30 May workers held a protest rally and marched towards the government buildings in Willemstad.1

The demonstration turned violent:1

  • Police fired on the marchers, wounding Godett and others.
  • Crowds looted and set fire to shops, offices and vehicles in the central business district of Punda, much of it owned by the island's elite.
  • The government of the Netherlands Antilles called in Dutch marines stationed on the island to restore order.

The riots left two people dead and much of central Willemstad destroyed; hundreds of people were arrested.1 Yet the protesters achieved most of their immediate demands: higher wages for the workers and the resignation of the government of the Netherlands Antilles.1

04

What happened next?

  • New politics: in elections in September 1969, the leaders of the uprising won seats in the parliament of the Netherlands Antilles through their new party, the Frente Obrero Liberashon 30 di Mei (Workers' Liberation Front of 30 May).
  • Afro-Curaçaoan advancement: Afro-Curaçaoans gained greater access to political office and public employment.
  • Language and culture: Papiamentu, the island's Creole language, gained social prestige and much wider use after the uprising.
  • Constitutional change: the Netherlands, shocked by the events, became more eager to grant independence; Suriname became independent in 1975. Curaçao chose to remain in the Kingdom, and became a separate constituent country in 2010.
05

What do others say?

At the time

Many in the business community and the Netherlands saw the events as senseless rioting and looting. The movement's supporters saw them as a justified explosion against racism, poverty and colonial rule.

Historians' debates

  • Riot or revolution? Historians debate whether 30 May was a spontaneous riot, a labour protest that got out of hand, or a political revolt against the colonial order.
  • The violence: the destruction of the city centre is seen by some as an attack on the symbols of elite power; others stress the damage to small businesses and to people's livelihoods, and the deaths that resulted.
  • The role of the Netherlands: the use of Dutch marines raised the question of whether the Netherlands was still acting as a colonial power.

07

How is it remembered?

  • The anniversary of 30 May is commemorated on the island, and the date is part of the name of the party it produced.
  • The artist Ras Elijah painted a mural of the events in Otrobanda in 1996.
  • The uprising is widely discussed in Curaçao as the birth of modern Afro-Curaçaoan political consciousness.
08

Why does it matter?

Trinta di Mei was a turning point in the history of Curaçao and the Dutch Caribbean. A labour dispute became a revolt against racial and economic inequality, and changed who held power, which language mattered and how people saw themselves.

  • It brought Afro-Curaçaoans into political power for the first time.
  • It strengthened the status of Papiamentu and Afro-Caribbean culture.
  • It shaped the relationship between the Netherlands and its Caribbean territories.

What other events are like this?

  • 1937The Butler Riots: Trinidad's Oilfield Strike — Oil workers led by the preacher 'Buzz' Butler struck against low wages and racism. The violence that followed killed police and strikers alike — and the island's labour movement was born.
  • 1963The Bristol Bus Boycott — West Indian activists in Bristol organised a boycott of the city's buses to end a colour bar that kept Black and Asian people from working as bus crews.
  • 1865The Morant Bay Rebellion — Thirty years after slavery ended, poor Black Jamaicans marched on a courthouse demanding justice and land. The governor's response — hundreds killed, flogged and hanged — scandalised Britain.
