The Comuneros of New Granada

A market woman tore down a notice of new taxes in a small Andean town. Within weeks, an army of up to 20,000 was marching on Bogotá, and the Spanish authorities signed away the taxes — before breaking their word.

When
March – October 1781
Where
Socorro, Santander, Colombia
Facts checked
2026-11-15

See it on the map →

01

What is the background to this event?

In the 1770s, Spain introduced the Bourbon Reforms to make its American colonies more profitable.1

  • When Spain went to war with Britain in 1779, it raised taxes and demanded "voluntary" donations across its empire.1
  • In New Granada (now Colombia and parts of Venezuela), the prices of tobacco and brandy rose, and new taxes were imposed.1
  • There had already been protests, some violent, against Crown policies between 1740 and 1779.1

In their own words

03

What happened?

On 16 March 1781, in Socorro, Manuela Beltrán tore down the posted edicts announcing the new taxes.1

  • Protests spread to many towns. People elected committees, known as el común, to lead the movement.1
  • Juan Francisco Berbeo gathered 10,000 to 20,000 rebels to march on Bogotá.1
  • After defeating troops sent against them, the rebels stopped near the capital, and Spanish officials signed an agreement accepting their demands.1
  • Once the rebels had gone home, the officials declared the agreement void because it had been forced on them.1
  • José Antonio Galán continued the fight with a small band, but was defeated and executed.1
04

What happened next?

  • Punishment: Galán and others were executed, and other leaders were sentenced to life in prison.1
  • Spread: similar risings broke out in Mérida and the Venezuelan Andes.1
  • Independence: later historians saw the revolt as a forerunner of independence from Spain, won in 1819.1
05

What do others say?

At the time

The rebels' slogan was "Long live the king, death to bad government" — they claimed loyalty to the Crown.3

Debates since

  • Precursor of independence? In the nineteenth century, historian Manuel Briceño saw it as a forerunner of independence; John Leddy Phelan argued it was a defence of traditional rights.13
  • Violence: the revolt was mostly a show of mass force; the authorities' response included executions, and Galán's body was cut into pieces and displayed.3
06

The evidence

  • The capitulations of Zipaquirá, the agreement signed with the rebels.3
07

How is it remembered?

  • Manuela Beltrán and José Antonio Galán are national heroes in Colombia.
08

Why does it matter?

The comuneros brought together people of different classes and races against colonial taxation.

  • The revolt began with the act of a working woman.
