The Lip Watch Factory Occupation

When the owners of a famous watch factory planned mass sackings, the workers hid the stock, restarted production and sold watches to pay themselves. 'It's possible,' they said: 'we make, we sell, we pay ourselves.'

When
June 1973 – 1976
Where
Palente, Besançon, France
Facts checked
2026-11-16

See it on the map →

01

What is the background to this event?

Lip, founded in Besançon in 1867, was one of France's best-known watchmakers. In the 1960s it faced cheap competition from Asia, and the Swiss firm Ébauches SA took a stake.1

  • On 17 April 1973, the managing director resigned and the company filed for bankruptcy.1
  • An action committee, inspired by May 1968, formed among the workers.1
  • On 12 June, workers grabbed an administrator's briefcase and discovered plans for 480 redundancies.1
02

Who were the key people?

  • Charles Piaget

    A skilled worker and union leader (CFDT and PSU) who became the face of the struggle.

  • Jean Raguenès

    A Dominican priest who worked in the factory and helped lead the action committee.

  • The women workers of Lip

    Women made up much of the workforce and played a key role in the struggle.

  • Claude Neuschwander

    A businessman who restarted the company in 1974.

  • Pierre Messmer

    Prime minister of France, 1972–74.

In their own words

03

What happened?

The workers occupied the factory at Palente immediately. That night, they hid the stock of 25,000 watches in secret locations.1

  • They restarted production under their own control and sold the watches directly, at a 42 per cent discount, to pay their own wages.1
  • Their slogan was: "It's possible: we make, we sell, we pay ourselves."1
  • On 14 August 1973, riot police (CRS) evicted the workers from the factory and occupied it until February 1974. Workers in Besançon struck in protest, and there were clashes with police.1
  • On 29 September 1973, more than 100,000 people joined a march through Besançon in support.1
  • The workers rejected a government plan that involved redundancies.1
04

What happened next?

  • A new company: in January 1974, an agreement brought in Claude Neuschwander to restart Lip and rehire the workers.
  • Collapse again: the new company failed in 1976, and the workers occupied the factory again.
  • Co-operatives: some workers later formed co-operatives to continue production.
05

Why does it matter?

Lip became the symbol of workers' self-management (autogestion) in 1970s Europe.

  • It showed workers could run production themselves.
  • It raised questions about who should control a firm when its owners fail.
  • An agreement in 1974 restarted the company and rehired the workers, and some later formed co-operatives to continue production.

What do others say?How it was seen at the time, and where historians disagree

At the time

Many on the left saw Lip as a model; employers and the government saw illegal sales and the seizure of stock as a threat to property.1

Debates since

  • Success or failure? Lip's later collapse leads some to see it as a heroic failure; others stress the lasting influence of its ideas.3
  • Why it ended: some argue that the government and employers deliberately let Lip fail to prevent it becoming a model.1
  • Violence: the occupation itself was peaceful; clashes followed the police eviction.1

Lip workers, 1973

Reading it

'It's possible: we make, we sell, we pay ourselves.' The slogan was aimed at everyone who had been told workers could not run a business. Its plain confidence is what made Lip inspiring across Europe: not a theory of self-management, but a factory doing it.

How is it remembered?Memorials, anniversaries and arguments over memory
  • The Lip struggle remains a reference point for French unions and for co-operatives.
  • The Palente factory district remains a site of memory for the struggle.
