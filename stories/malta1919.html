Sette Giugno: Bread, Flags and Bullets in Valletta

After the First World War, prices soared in British-ruled Malta while some merchants grew rich. On the day a national assembly met to demand self-government, crowds attacked symbols of British rule, and soldiers opened fire.

When
7 – 8 June 1919
Where
Valletta, Malta
Facts checked

01

What is the background to this event?

Malta was a British Crown Colony and a naval fortress. After the First World War, the colonial government failed to keep the islands supplied with basic food.1

  • The cost of living rose sharply. Wages for dockyard and government workers did not keep up, and many people believed that grain importers and flour millers were making excessive profits from the price of bread.1
  • Dockyard workers formed a union in 1916 and went on strike in 1917, after being offered a 10% pay rise.1
  • In February 1919, a National Assembly of Maltese representatives met for the first time. It passed a resolution claiming for Malta the rights given to other nations at the Paris peace conference.1

In their own words

03

What happened?

On Saturday 7 June 1919, the National Assembly met for the second time in Valletta. Crowds gathered outside.1

  • The crowd forced its way into a shop flying the Union Jack above the Maltese flag and tore it down. People then broke into offices, pulled down British flags and burned one in the street.1
  • They wrecked the presses of the Daily Malta Chronicle and attacked the homes of merchants believed to be profiteering, including a leading importer.1
  • Small groups of soldiers were sent against crowds of thousands. A commission later claimed a shot had first come from a window, but eyewitnesses reported that a soldier fired into the crowd and the rest of his troop followed.1
  • Three men were killed and about 50 injured. Ġużè Bajada fell on top of the Maltese flag he was carrying.1
  • The Assembly asked for the troops to be withdrawn, and a member persuaded the crowd to disperse. The next day, crowds attacked the house of a flour mill owner. Royal Marines cleared it, and one of them bayoneted Carmelo Abela, who died on 16 June.1
04

What happened next?

  • Censorship and trials: political censorship was reinstated, and a court-martial investigated 32 people who had taken part.1
  • Self-government: Lord Plumer recommended concessions. In 1921, a new constitution gave Malta control of local affairs, and the first elections under it were held that October.1
  • Claimed by others: in 1924, Fascist Italy celebrated the four dead as martyrs of Italian nationalism in Malta.1
05

Why does it matter?

Sette Giugno joined anger over the price of bread to the demand for self-government. Within two years, Malta had its first constitution with an elected parliament.1

  • A new constitution in 1921 gave Malta control of local affairs, and the first elections under it were held that October.1
  • In 1924 Fascist Italy tried to claim the four dead as martyrs of Italian nationalism.1
  • Since 1989, 7 June has been one of Malta's five national days.1

What do others say?How it was seen at the time, and where historians disagree

At the time

Nationalists in the Assembly argued that the British government could not be trusted to grant reform; the commission of inquiry later found that the police had not appreciated the gravity of the situation.1

Debates since

  • Who fired first? The commission suggested a shot came from a window; eyewitnesses said the soldiers began the shooting. Later historians criticised sending so few soldiers against crowds of thousands.1
  • Violence: four Maltese were killed and about 50 injured; the crowds also attacked property and assaulted police officers.1

This newspaper page was published a week after the riots, when censorship of political articles had already been reinstated. It shows both what Maltese people were told and, by its silences, what could not be said. Whose point of view is missing is part of what it tells us.
