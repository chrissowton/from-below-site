The Christian Agrarian Leagues of Paraguay

Under the dictatorship of Alfredo Stroessner, Paraguayan peasants gathered to read the Bible together — and built communities where land was farmed in common and children learned in Guaraní. The regime crushed them.

When
1960 – 1976
Where
Misiones, Cordillera and Caaguazú, Paraguay
Facts checked
2026-11-16

01

What is the background to this event?

From 1954 to 1989, Paraguay was ruled by General Alfredo Stroessner, one of Latin America's longest dictatorships. The ruling Colorado Party, the police and local government delegates controlled rural life.1

  • Most Paraguayan peasants (campesinos) had too little land, while large estates and, later, foreign companies held most of it.
  • Most peasants spoke Guaraní, but it was banned in official schools.1
  • From the 1950s, Jesuit priests in the department of Misiones began meeting peasant communities to read and reflect on the Bible. Peasants spoke of their dependence on the regime, the lack of land and the high cost of food.1

03

What happened?

The name "Christian Agrarian Leagues" was first used in 1960, at a meeting of peasants, priests and the Christian Workers' Youth in Santa Rosa, Misiones, where the first League was formed.1

  • Leagues spread to Cordillera, Caaguazú and other departments.1
  • Members farmed land together, set up cooperative stores to lower the price of basic goods, and created peasant schools where children and adults learned in Guaraní.1
  • Some communities ran workshops making clothes from local cotton, and held their own festivals, independent of those organised by the authorities.1
  • Groups of peasants moved to found new communities, such as San Isidro de Jejui, where they lived out their ideals more fully.1
  • Over time, the Leagues became more independent of the Church hierarchy, which took a more cautious position.1

The Leagues' growth alarmed the dictatorship, which saw them as a political threat. In the second half of the 1970s — especially in 1976 — the regime launched a fierce repression, with confiscations, expulsions, arrests, torture and killings, which ended the movement.1

04

What happened next?

  • Repression: the Leagues were destroyed. Many members were imprisoned and tortured, some were killed or disappeared, and others fled.
  • Truth: after the fall of Stroessner in 1989, Paraguay's Truth and Justice Commission (2004–08) documented the repression of peasant movements. In 2014, the site of the San Isidro de Jejui community was declared a "Historic Site of Conscience".1
  • Land recovered: survivors founded an association in 1989 to recover their land, and finally obtained title in 2012.1
  • Land conflicts today: Paraguay has one of the most unequal distributions of land in the world, and peasant movements continue to struggle against large soya plantations.
05

Why does it matter?

The Christian Agrarian Leagues show how faith, community and education in people's own language could become a form of resistance to dictatorship.

  • They were part of the wider movement of liberation theology in Latin America.
  • They raise questions about land, language and self-organisation that remain alive in Paraguay.
  • In 2014 the site of the San Isidro de Jejui community was declared a Historic Site of Conscience.1

What do others say?How it was seen at the time, and where historians disagree

At the time

The regime portrayed the Leagues as communist subversion. Members saw themselves as living out the Gospel in community.

Historians' debates

  • Religion or politics? Historians debate how far the Leagues remained a religious movement and how far they became political.
  • The guerrilla link: the regime used the discovery of a small armed group, the Organización Político-Militar (OPM), in 1976 to justify a crackdown on the Leagues; historians note that most League members had no connection with it.
  • Violence: the Leagues were a non-violent movement; the violence came overwhelmingly from the state.
