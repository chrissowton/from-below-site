Tacky's War

On Easter Monday 1760, enslaved Africans led by a former Fante chief rose to make Jamaica free. It was the greatest slave rebellion in the Caribbean before the Haitian Revolution.

When
April 1760 – 1761
Where
Port Maria, St Mary, Jamaica
Facts checked
2026-11-16

See it on the map →

01

What is the background to this event?

Jamaica had been a British colony since 1655. Its sugar plantations depended on enslaved Africans.1

  • Jamaica was notorious for the cruelty of its slavery.1
  • Tacky had been a chief in Fante country (in present-day Ghana) before being captured in war and sold into slavery.1
  • He and his lieutenants planned to take over Jamaica and create a free state, inspired by the Maroons who had won their freedom in the 1730s.1
  • A secret network of Coromantee (Akan) people organised the rising across the island.1

In their own words

03

What happened?

Before dawn on Easter Monday, 7 April 1760, Tacky and his followers took the Frontier and Trinity plantations, killing some overseers.1

  • They seized gunpowder and firearms from Fort Haldane, near Port Maria.1
  • Hundreds more enslaved people joined them.1
  • On 25 May, another rising began in Westmoreland under Apongo, and defeated the local militia.1
  • British troops, the colonial militia and the Maroons fought a long campaign against the rebels.1
  • Tacky was killed, and order took months, and in some places years, to restore.1

04

What happened next?

  • Deaths: over 60 white colonists, a similar number of free people of colour, and about 400 rebels died.1
  • Punishment: two leaders were burned alive, two were hung in cages until they starved, and more than 500 rebels were sold away to British Honduras.1
  • Laws: the colonial assembly passed harsh new laws to control enslaved people.1
05

What do others say?

At the time

Planters blamed "Coromantee" Africans and tightened control; the rebels sought freedom and power.1

Debates since

  • Its importance: historian Trevor Burnard argues that only the American Revolution shocked the British imperial system more in the eighteenth century.1
  • Violence on all sides: the rebels killed overseers and colonists; the colonial response included summary executions, burnings and gibbeting; Tacky had himself sold war captives to slave traders before he was enslaved.1
06

The evidence

  • Planters' accounts, including Edward Long's History of Jamaica.
  • Colonial records studied by Vincent Brown.2

Primary source: Engraving

What is it?
Engraving
Made by, and when
Nicolas Lejeune and François-Anne David, Paris, late 1700s

A French engraving of an uprising of enslaved people in Jamaica, published in a history of England. Its caption dates it to 1759, but it most likely shows Tacky's War. It was made for European readers, and it shows the rebels as Europeans feared them.

07

How is it remembered?

  • Tacky is honoured in Jamaica, and a monument stands in Port Maria.
08

Why does it matter?

Tacky's War was the most significant slave rebellion in the Caribbean between 1733 and the Haitian Revolution.1

  • The historian Vincent Brown describes it as part of a wider Atlantic war over slavery.2

Questions to think about

  1. How did experiences in Africa shape the rising?
  2. Why did the Maroons fight against the rebels?
  3. How should we judge violence by enslaved people seeking freedom?
  4. Why was the rebellion such a shock to the British Empire?
09

What other events are like this?

  • 1763The Berbice Rebellion — Enslaved Africans led by Cuffy took control of almost an entire Dutch colony and held it for nearly a year. Cuffy proposed dividing the land with the Dutch. It was South America's first great slave revolt — three decades before Haiti.
  • 1816Bussa's Rebellion — On Easter Sunday 1816, enslaved people across south-east Barbados rose up — the largest slave revolt in the island's history, and the first of three great risings that shook British slavery.
  • 1791The Haitian Revolution — The only revolution in history in which enslaved people overthrew their enslavers and founded an independent state.
