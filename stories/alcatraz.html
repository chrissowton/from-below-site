Civil rights & anti-racism

The Occupation of Alcatraz

Calling themselves 'Indians of All Tribes', Native American students and activists occupied the abandoned prison island for nineteen months — and changed federal policy.

When
20 November 1969 – 11 June 1971
Where
Alcatraz Island, San Francisco Bay, USA
Facts checked

01

What is the background to this event?

By the 1960s, Native Americans faced the consequences of centuries of dispossession, broken treaties and attempts to destroy their cultures:

  • From the 1950s, the US government pursued a policy of "termination": ending the federal recognition of tribes, dissolving reservations and ending treaty obligations. More than a hundred tribes were terminated.1
  • Under the "relocation" programme, tens of thousands of Native Americans were encouraged to move from reservations to cities such as San Francisco, Los Angeles and Chicago, where many faced poverty and discrimination.
  • A new generation of young, urban Native Americans — many of them university students — was inspired by the civil rights movement to demand "Red Power".

Alcatraz Island, in San Francisco Bay, had been a notorious federal prison until it closed in 1963, and was declared surplus federal property. Activists cited the 1868 Treaty of Fort Laramie, which they argued promised that unused federal land would be returned to Native peoples. In March 1964 a small group of Lakota people had occupied the island for four hours. In October 1969, the San Francisco Indian Center, a vital meeting place for the city's Native community, was destroyed by fire.1

In their own words

03

What happened?

After two earlier symbolic landings on 9 November 1969, around 89 Native Americans landed on Alcatraz in the early hours of 20 November 1969 and began an occupation that would last nineteen months. They called themselves "Indians of All Tribes".1

The occupiers issued the Alcatraz Proclamation, which offered — with deliberate irony — to buy the island for "$24 in glass beads and red cloth", the price for which Manhattan was said to have been bought in the seventeenth century. They proposed to build a Native American cultural centre, university and museum on the island.1

  • On Thanksgiving Day 1969, hundreds of supporters came to the island for a celebration.
  • John Trudell made daily broadcasts from the island on "Radio Free Alcatraz", and the occupiers published a newsletter.
  • The occupation won wide public support. The rock band Creedence Clearwater Revival donated money to buy a boat to supply the island.
  • The occupiers painted slogans on buildings, including "Indians Welcome — Indian Land" on the water tower.

The occupation faced growing difficulties. In January 1970, Richard Oakes's thirteen-year-old stepdaughter, Yvonne, died after a fall, and the Oakes family left the island. The government cut off electricity and water, and in June 1970 a fire destroyed several buildings. As the original student leaders left, divisions grew and numbers declined.1 On 11 June 1971, federal marshals and police removed the last fifteen occupiers from the island.1

04

What happened next?

  • The end of termination: in July 1970, President Richard Nixon formally rejected the termination policy and called for a new era of Native American "self-determination".
  • Land returned: in December 1970, Nixon signed a law returning Blue Lake and 48,000 acres of land to the Taos Pueblo in New Mexico.
  • Red Power: the occupation inspired a wave of protests and occupations across the country, including the Trail of Broken Treaties (1972) and the occupation of Wounded Knee (1973).
  • New laws: the Indian Self-Determination and Education Assistance Act of 1975 gave tribes greater control over their own affairs.
05

What do others say?

At the time

Government officials initially hoped the occupation would end of its own accord, and offered to create a park on the island instead of a Native cultural centre. Some newspapers mocked the occupiers; many members of the public, however, sympathised and sent food, money and supplies.

Historians' debates

  • Who led? Media coverage focused on male spokesmen such as Richard Oakes and John Trudell. Historians and participants have emphasised the leadership of women such as LaNada Means, whom fellow activist Dean Chavers called "the real leader of the occupation".
  • The American Indian Movement: AIM is often associated with Alcatraz, but participants stress that it had no part in planning the occupation.
  • Success or failure? The occupiers did not win the island, but most historians argue that the occupation had a lasting effect on federal policy and Native activism.
06

The evidence

  • The Alcatraz Proclamation and other documents issued by Indians of All Tribes.
  • Recordings of Radio Free Alcatraz, and photographs and film of the occupation.
  • Graffiti on the island's buildings, preserved by the National Park Service.
  • The oral histories of participants, collected by historians such as Troy Johnson.

07

How is it remembered?

  • Every year on Thanksgiving Day and on Indigenous Peoples' Day, Native Americans hold "Unthanksgiving" sunrise ceremonies on Alcatraz to honour the occupation.
  • The occupiers' graffiti, including the slogans on the water tower, has been restored by the National Park Service.
  • The island's guided tours now tell the story of the occupation alongside that of the prison.
08

Why does it matter?

The occupation of Alcatraz was a turning point in the Native American rights movement. It brought together people from many different nations under a shared identity, drew national and international attention to Native American grievances, and helped bring the termination policy to an end.

  • It showed the power of symbolic direct action to change public opinion and government policy.
  • It inspired a generation of Native American activists.
  • It is part of a long struggle for Indigenous land, sovereignty and self-determination that continues today.
