The Ludlow Massacre and the Colorado Coalfield War

Striking coal miners and their families were living in a tent colony when National Guard soldiers and company guards attacked. Two women and eleven children died in a pit beneath a burning tent. The armed miners' revenge turned a strike into a war.

When
September 1913 – April 1914
Where
Ludlow, Colorado, United States
Facts checked
2026-11-16

01

What is the background to this event?

The southern Colorado coalfields were dominated by the Colorado Fuel and Iron Company (CF&I), controlled by John D. Rockefeller Jr. from New York.1

  • Between 1884 and 1912, mining accidents killed more than 1,700 people in Colorado.1
  • Miners lived in company towns, patrolled by armed guards; those who complained could be evicted.1
  • In September 1913, the United Mine Workers of America called a strike for union recognition, better pay and enforcement of safety laws.1
  • Evicted from company housing, strikers and their families moved into tent colonies, the largest at Ludlow, with about 1,200 people.1
02

Who were the key people?

  • Louis Tikas

    A Greek immigrant and leader of the Ludlow tent colony, killed in the attack.

  • Karl Linderfelt

    A National Guard lieutenant who commanded troops at Ludlow and was accused of killing Tikas.

  • Mary 'Mother' Jones

    A veteran union organiser who campaigned among the miners and was repeatedly arrested.

  • John D. Rockefeller Jr.

    The controlling owner of CF&I, widely blamed for the violence.

  • The Costa and Petrucci families

    Mothers and children who died in the pit beneath their tent.

In their own words

03

What happened?

The company hired the Baldwin–Felts Detective Agency to harass strikers. Guards shone searchlights on the tents at night, fired into them and patrolled in an armoured car with a machine gun, known to the strikers as the "Death Special". Families dug pits beneath their tents for shelter.1

  • Some strikers were also armed, and there were gun battles and killings on both sides before Ludlow.1
  • The governor sent the Colorado National Guard. Mine guards were enrolled in a new unit, "Troop A", and given Guard uniforms.1
  • On 20 April 1914, the Guard set up a machine gun overlooking the colony and fighting began. The battle lasted all day.1
  • Louis Tikas was captured and killed. The tents were set alight. Two women and eleven children who were sheltering in a pit died of suffocation.13
  • About 21 people were killed at Ludlow in total.1

The Ten Days' War

In revenge, armed miners attacked mines and company buildings along a 225-mile front, fighting the Guard and killing mine guards and strikebreakers.1 Federal troops arrived on 29 April. In all, an estimated 69 to 199 people were killed during the strike.1

04

What happened next?

  • Defeat: the strike ended in December 1914 without union recognition.
  • Investigation: congressional hearings and the 1915 report of the Commission on Industrial Relations helped promote reforms such as child labour laws and the eight-hour day.1
  • Company unions: Rockefeller introduced a company-run "employee representation plan" instead of an independent union.
  • Courts martial: Linderfelt and other Guardsmen were acquitted.
05

Why does it matter?

Thomas G. Andrews called it the deadliest strike in US history.1 Ludlow became a symbol of the violence that workers faced when they tried to organise.

  • It shows how private and state forces could act together against strikers.
  • It also shows how violence escalated on both sides once it began.
  • Congressional hearings and a 1915 federal report helped promote reforms such as child labour laws and the eight-hour day.1

What do others say?How it was seen at the time, and where historians disagree

At the time

The company and the Guard claimed the strikers fired first; the union called it a massacre. Rockefeller's reputation was badly damaged.1

Debates since

  • Who fired first? Accounts conflict. Later investigations and modern archaeology largely support some of the strikers' accounts.1
  • Violence on both sides: historians stress that armed strikers killed guards, strikebreakers and soldiers, especially in the Ten Days' War.3
  • Death toll: estimates for the whole conflict range from 69 to 199.1
How do we know?The evidence, and a source from the time
  • Congressional hearings and the Commission on Industrial Relations report.4
  • Photographs of the colony before and after the attack.
  • Archaeological excavations of the tent colony by the University of Denver.

What is it?
Magazine cover
Made by, and when
John Sloan, The Masses, June 1914

A socialist magazine's cover showing a miner defending his family at Ludlow. It was made to stir anger, not to record events.

Reading it

This magazine cover, showing a miner defending his family, was made to stir anger, not to record events. Unlike a news photograph it makes no claim to neutrality. The Masses was trying to reach readers who might be moved to act.

How is it remembered?Memorials, anniversaries and arguments over memory
  • The United Mine Workers own the site and hold a memorial service there every year.1
  • The site became a National Historic Landmark in 2009.1
  • Woody Guthrie wrote a song about the massacre.
