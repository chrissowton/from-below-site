The vote & democracy

The Burkinabè Uprising

When Blaise Compaoré tried to change the constitution to stay in power after 27 years, hundreds of thousands took to the streets. Within three days, the parliament was on fire and the president had fled.

When
28 – 31 October 2014
Where
Ouagadougou, Burkina Faso
Facts checked
2026-11-16

See it on the map →

01

What is the background to this event?

Blaise Compaoré took power in 1987 in a coup in which the revolutionary president Thomas Sankara was killed. He ruled Burkina Faso for 27 years.1

  • Article 37 of the constitution limited presidents to two terms.1
  • In October 2014, Compaoré's government asked the National Assembly to change Article 37 so that he could stand again in 2015.1
  • The citizens' movement Le Balai Citoyen ("the citizens' broom"), founded in 2013 by the musicians Smockey and Sams'K Le Jah, mobilised young people against the change.2

In their own words

03

What happened?

On 28 October 2014, hundreds of thousands of people marched in Ouagadougou against the constitutional change, in one of the largest demonstrations in the country's history.1

  • On 30 October, the day of the vote, protesters broke through police lines and stormed the National Assembly. Parts of the building were set on fire.1
  • Protesters also attacked the homes of politicians and the state television station.1
  • Security forces opened fire. At least five people were killed on 30 October; estimates of the total killed during the uprising range from about a dozen to more than 30.13
  • The government withdrew the bill. On 31 October, Compaoré resigned and fled to Côte d'Ivoire.1
  • The army seized power under Lieutenant Colonel Isaac Zida. Under pressure from protesters and the African Union, a civilian, Michel Kafando, became transitional president.1

04

What happened next?

  • The 2015 coup: in September 2015, Compaoré's former presidential guard (RSP) seized the transitional government. Mass protests and the regular army forced the coup leaders to give up within a week.
  • Elections: Roch Marc Christian Kaboré was elected president in November 2015.
  • Sankara: in 2022, Compaoré was convicted in his absence for his role in the killing of Thomas Sankara.
  • Military rule: after a jihadist insurgency spread, the army seized power again in 2022.
05

What do others say?

At the time

Supporters called it a revolution; Compaoré's supporters called it a coup by the street. The African Union demanded a quick return to civilian rule.1

Debates since

  • Violence: most protesters were peaceful, but some burned the parliament and looted homes. Security forces killed protesters.1
  • Revolution or transition? Some argue that the old elite and the army remained powerful, and that the uprising did not change the system.
  • Since 2022: the return of military rule has led to debate about the legacy of 2014.

07

How is it remembered?

  • The burned National Assembly became a symbol of the uprising.
  • Those killed are remembered as martyrs of the insurrection.
08

Why does it matter?

The uprising showed that popular protest could stop a long-serving leader from extending his rule, and inspired movements elsewhere in Africa.

  • Musicians and young people played a central role.
  • It drew on the memory of Thomas Sankara.

Questions to think about

  3. Was the burning of the National Assembly justified?

What other events are like this?

  • 2011Y'en a Marre — "Fed Up" — Angry about power cuts, a group of rappers and journalists launched a movement called 'Fed Up'. They registered hundreds of thousands of young voters and helped defeat President Abdoulaye Wade's bid for a third term.
  • 1991Mali's March Revolution — Students and democracy campaigners marched against 23 years of military rule. Soldiers fired on them — and then other soldiers refused, overthrew the dictator, and handed power to an elected government.
  • 2006Nepal's People's Movement of 2006 — Defying curfews and shoot-on-sight orders, millions of Nepalis took to the streets for nineteen days and forced their king to give up absolute power — the beginning of the end of a 240-year-old monarchy.
