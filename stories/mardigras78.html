The First Sydney Mardi Gras

A night-time street party for gay rights ended with police violence and 53 arrests. A newspaper printed the names of those arrested — and the protest grew into one of the world's largest pride events.

When
24 June 1978
Where
Oxford Street and Kings Cross, Sydney, Australia
Facts checked
2026-11-15

See it on the map →

01

What is the background to this event?

In the 1970s, sex between men was a crime in New South Wales. Gay men and lesbians faced police harassment, and could lose their jobs or homes if their sexuality became known.

  • The first gay rights demonstration in Australia was held in Sydney in October 1971, organised by the Campaign Against Moral Persecution (CAMP).1
  • Gay liberation groups organised further protests for law reform and against discrimination.1
  • In 1978, Sydney activists planned events as part of an international day of gay solidarity, commemorating the Stonewall riots in New York in 1969.1

In their own words

03

What happened?

On 24 June 1978, about 500 people took part in a morning march from Town Hall to Martin Place, the largest gay protest in Australia until then. That night, a street party set off from Taylor Square along Oxford Street with a small sound truck.1

  • The organisers had police permission. As people left the bars to join in, the crowd grew to around 1,500.1
  • Police then withdrew the permit, arrested the truck driver, Lance Gowland, and tried to stop the procession reaching Hyde Park.1
  • The crowd marched on to Kings Cross, where police attacked and arrested 53 people. Many reported being beaten in the cells.1

Most of the charges were eventually dropped. But the Sydney Morning Herald published the names of those arrested, outing them to their families and employers. Many lost their jobs.1

Protests continued over the following weeks, and more people were arrested outside the courts.

04

What happened next?

  • 1979: a second parade took place the next year, with around 3,000 people and no arrests. That year, the New South Wales government repealed the law under which the 1978 arrests had been made.1
  • Decriminalisation: sex between men was decriminalised in New South Wales in 1984.1
  • Growth: the Mardi Gras moved to summer and became an annual parade, now watched by hundreds of thousands of people.1
  • Apologies: in 2016, the Sydney Morning Herald apologised for publishing the names, and the New South Wales Parliament and police apologised to the '78ers.
05

What do others say?

At the time

Police and many politicians saw the parade as a disturbance of public order. Activists saw the police response as persecution of a peaceful, permitted protest.

Today

  • Protest or party? Some activists feel the Mardi Gras has become a commercial celebration and lost its political edge; others see its celebration as political in itself.1
  • Police in the parade: uniformed police now march in the parade. Many in the community object to this, given the history of 1978.1
  • Who is included? In 2019, members voted against changing the name to include other groups, which disappointed many bisexual people.1

07

How is it remembered?

  • The '78ers lead the Mardi Gras parade each year.
  • Sydney hosted WorldPride in 2023, the first held in the Southern Hemisphere.1
08

Why does it matter?

The first Mardi Gras turned a small local protest into a national issue. Police violence and public outing created momentum for law reform, as the organisers later recalled.1

  • It shows how state repression can backfire and build support for a movement.
  • It shows the harm done by publicly exposing people's sexuality.
  • Mardi Gras is now one of the world's best-known pride events.

Questions to think about

  1. Why do you think the police withdrew their permission for the parade?
  2. Why was the publication of the names of those arrested so harmful?
  3. Should police march in pride parades today?
  4. Can a celebration also be a protest?

What other events are like this?

  • 1969The Stonewall Uprising — A routine police raid on a gay bar met resistance, and several nights of protest gave rise to the modern LGBTQ+ liberation movement.
  • 1990Africa's First Pride March — A Black gay activist, tried for treason by the apartheid state, led Africa's first pride march — and helped make South Africa the first country to protect gay rights in its constitution.
  • 1987ACT UP and the Fight Against AIDS — Facing a deadly epidemic and government neglect, AIDS activists used direct action and expert knowledge to change how drugs are tested and approved.
