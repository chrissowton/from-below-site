The Durban Strikes of 1973

Under apartheid it was illegal for Black workers to strike. In 1973, around 60,000 of them did so anyway, at more than 100 firms in Durban — reviving the Black trade union movement that would help bring down apartheid.

When
9 January – March 1973
Where
Durban, South Africa

01

What is the background to this event?

In 1970, about 80 per cent of private-sector jobs in South Africa paid Black African workers below the poverty line, which in Durban was R18 a week. Inflation was rising.1

  • Under the Native Labour (Settlement of Disputes) Act of 1953, Black African workers were not legally "employees". They could not join registered trade unions, and strikes were illegal.1
  • Instead, they had only works committees supervised by the state. By January 1973, Durban had just two.1
  • Strikers risked arrest, imprisonment and, for migrant workers, deportation to the "homelands".1

In their own words

03

What happened?

At 3am on 9 January 1973, migrant workers at the Coronation Brick and Tile works were woken by colleagues and told to meet at a sports field instead of going to work.1

  • The strikes spread from firm to firm. Workers at the Frame Group's textile mills, some paid R5 to R9 a week, demanded R20. By the end of January, every Frame textile factory in Natal had stopped.1
  • On 5 February, 3,000 municipal workers struck; the next day there were 13,000.1
  • Around 60,000 Black workers at more than 100 firms took part between January and March.1
  • The strikes had no visible leaders. Workers gathered in crowds and refused to elect representatives who could be arrested.2
  • Most employers gave wage increases. Police were present throughout but made relatively few arrests — 353 by the end of the year.1

By the end of 1973, an estimated 100,000 Black and Indian workers had taken some form of industrial action.1

04

What happened next?

  • New unions: independent Black unions grew in Natal and beyond, leading to the Federation of South African Trade Unions (FOSATU) in 1979.1
  • COSATU: in 1985, the Congress of South African Trade Unions was founded and became a central force in the struggle against apartheid.1
  • Reform: after the Wiehahn Commission, Black unions were legally recognised in 1979.
  • Repression: Rick Turner was banned in 1973 and murdered in 1978; his killers were never found.
05

What do others say?

At the time

The government blamed agitators; the police said they found no evidence of any organisation behind the strikes.1

Debates since

  • Spontaneous or organised? Historians debate the role of student activists, workers' advice bureaux and existing networks among migrant workers.2
  • The "Durban Moment": some historians link the strikes to a wider flowering of radical ideas in Durban, including Black Consciousness.
  • Violence: the strikes were overwhelmingly peaceful on both sides, which helped them succeed.1

07

How is it remembered?

  • The strikes are commemorated by COSATU and South African unions as the start of the modern labour movement.
08

Why does it matter?

The Durban strikes broke a decade of silence after the crushing of resistance in the 1960s, and showed that Black workers could win through collective action.

  • They were a form of mass civil disobedience.1
  • They show the economic power of workers who had no political rights.
