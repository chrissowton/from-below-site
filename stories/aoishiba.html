Disability rights

Aoi Shiba no Kai and the Kawasaki Bus Struggle

A radical group of people with cerebral palsy refused to be pitied. They protested against the killing of disabled children — and occupied buses that refused to carry wheelchair users.

When
1957 – 1977
Where
Kawasaki and Yokohama, Kanagawa, Japan
Facts checked
2026-11-16

See it on the map →

01

What is the background to this event?

In post-war Japan, disabled people were often kept at home or in institutions. Many people saw disability as a tragedy, and disabled people as objects of pity and charity.

  • The Eugenic Protection Law of 1948 allowed the forced sterilisation of people with certain disabilities and illnesses. It remained in force until 1996.2
  • Buses and trains were not accessible: wheelchair users had to be lifted on board by helpers, and could be refused travel if they came alone.1
  • Aoi Shiba no Kai (the "Green Grass Association") was founded in Tokyo in 1957 by people with cerebral palsy (CP). At first it focused on friendship and welfare, but it became increasingly political.1
02

Who were the key people?

  • Yokota Hiroshi

    A poet and activist with cerebral palsy, who wrote the group's famous 1970 declaration.

  • Yokozuka Kōichi

    A leader of the Kanagawa branch and later national chair.

  • Hara Kazuo

    A filmmaker whose documentary Goodbye CP (1972) followed members of the group.

  • The CP Women's Association

    Women members who formed their own group in 1974 to address the discrimination they faced as disabled women.

03

What happened?

In 1966, members formed a radical branch in Kanagawa Prefecture, around Kawasaki and Yokohama.1

  • Protesting the killing of disabled children: in May 1970, a mother in Yokohama killed her severely disabled child. Many people sympathised with the mother, and a campaign asked for leniency. Aoi Shiba protested, demanding that the crime be judged like any other. They argued that sympathy for such killings implied that disabled lives were worth less.1
  • A declaration: in 1970, Yokota Hiroshi published a declaration that the group would "assert ourselves aggressively", rejecting "love and justice" offered by able-bodied people and choosing endless confrontation over easy compromise.2
  • The Kawasaki bus struggle: after wheelchair users were refused travel on buses in Kawasaki in 1976, on 12 April 1977 about 60 people with cerebral palsy and their helpers occupied around 30 buses at Kawasaki station, staying on board until late at night.1

Some protesters used force: they broke windows and damaged buses, and sat in front of buses to stop them moving. The action was widely reported and heavily criticised, but it put accessibility on the national agenda.1

04

What happened next?

  • Accessible transport: bus companies did not change immediately, but the struggle helped push Japan towards accessible transport. A Transportation Barrier-Free Act was passed in 2000, and low-floor buses are now standard.
  • The eugenics law: Aoi Shiba also campaigned against changes to the Eugenic Protection Law. The law was finally replaced in 1996, and in July 2024 Japan's Supreme Court ruled that it had been unconstitutional.2
  • Independent living: the movement inspired disabled people to leave institutions and live independently in the community.
05

What do others say?

At the time

Many people sympathised with exhausted parents and saw Aoi Shiba's protests as heartless. Aoi Shiba argued that society's sympathy for such killings showed that disabled people were seen as an "existence which should not exist".

Commentators' debates

  • Tactics: the damage done during the Kawasaki bus struggle was widely condemned, and the group itself was divided over confrontational tactics. Supporters argue that polite requests had been ignored for years.
  • Women and sexism: the film Goodbye CP was later criticised from a feminist perspective for focusing on men's sexuality, including accounts of sexual violence. Women members formed their own group to address issues such as forced hysterectomies.1
  • Relations with helpers: the group's suspicion of able-bodied helpers led to splits with supporters' organisations.1

07

How is it remembered?

  • Aoi Shiba's declaration is still widely studied in Japanese disability studies.
  • Survivors of forced sterilisation won compensation and a government apology after the 2024 Supreme Court ruling.
08

Why does it matter?

Aoi Shiba no Kai was one of the most radical disability movements in the world. It insisted that disabled people were not objects of pity, and challenged a society that saw them as a burden.

  • It raised hard questions about whose lives are valued, from eugenics to "mercy" killings.
  • It showed how direct action could force accessibility onto the political agenda.
  • It remains relevant after the 2016 Sagamihara attack, when 19 disabled people were murdered by a man who claimed their lives were worthless.2

Questions to think about

  2. Was it right to protest against sympathy for parents who killed their disabled children?

  4. What does accessible transport mean for disabled people's freedom?
