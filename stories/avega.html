The Genocide Widows of Rwanda

After the genocide against the Tutsi in 1994, fifty widows founded an association to help each other survive. Survivors' groups led by women went on to demand justice, health care and recognition of sexual violence as a crime of genocide.

01

What is the background to this event?

Between April and July 1994, Hutu extremist militias, soldiers and many ordinary people killed Tutsi across Rwanda, along with Hutu who opposed the killing. Estimates of the number killed range from about 500,000 to more than a million.

  • Rape and sexual violence were used systematically against Tutsi women.
  • The genocide ended when the Rwandan Patriotic Front (RPF) took control of the country. RPF forces also killed civilians during and after the war, which is less often discussed in Rwanda.
  • Many survivors were women who had lost their husbands and children, often living with injuries, trauma and HIV.1

03

What happened?

In October 1995, 50 women who had survived the genocide but lost their husbands founded AVEGA Agahozo ("to dry one's tears").1

  • The association set out to support widows and orphans, provide health care and housing, and educate orphans.1
  • It also aimed to fight for justice and to keep alive the memory of the victims.1
  • Its founders argued that survivors needed support, restitution and reparations before reconciliation could be expected of them.1
  • Women survivors testified before the International Criminal Tribunal for Rwanda. In 1998, in the case of Jean-Paul Akayesu, the tribunal ruled for the first time that rape could be an act of genocide.2
  • Survivors' organisations also took part in Rwanda's community gacaca courts, which tried hundreds of thousands of genocide cases between 2002 and 2012.2

04

What happened next?

  • Growth: AVEGA grew to tens of thousands of members, with regional centres offering medical and trauma care.
  • Assistance fund: the government created a fund to support genocide survivors, including school fees for orphans.
  • Women in public life: after the genocide, women took a leading role in rebuilding Rwanda; since 2008, women have been a majority in parliament.
05

What do others say?

At the time

Survivors' groups argued that the world had failed to stop the genocide and was failing to support its victims.3

Debates since

  • Gacaca: supporters say the community courts delivered justice on a vast scale; critics, including human rights groups, raised concerns about fair trial standards.2
  • Other victims: some historians and human rights groups argue that killings of civilians by RPF forces have not been properly investigated; the Rwandan government rejects comparisons with the genocide.
  • Survivors' voices: some survivors feel pressure to forgive and reconcile before their needs have been met.1

07

How is it remembered?

  • Rwanda holds a national period of mourning, Kwibuka, each year from 7 April.
  • The Kigali Genocide Memorial is the resting place of more than 250,000 victims.
08

Why does it matter?

The widows' movement shows how survivors of the worst crimes organised themselves to survive, demand justice and shape how genocide is remembered.

  • Their testimony changed international law on sexual violence.
  • They insisted that the needs of survivors should come before calls for reconciliation.

After 25 years of campaigning, Habré became the first former head of state convicted by another country's courts for crimes against humanity.
