The Mau Movement and Black Saturday

Samoans organised a non-violent movement for self-rule under the slogan 'Samoa mo Samoa' — Samoa for the Samoans. New Zealand police opened fire on a peaceful procession.

When
1926 – 1936 (Black Saturday: 28 December 1929)
Where
Apia, Samoa
Facts checked
2026-11-16

01

What is the background to this event?

In 1900 the Samoan islands were divided between Germany and the United States. Western Samoa became a German colony. Resistance to German rule was led by Lauaki Namulau'ulu Mamoe, whose movement, the Mau a Pule, was suppressed in 1909; he and other leaders were exiled to the Mariana Islands.1

At the start of the First World War, in 1914, New Zealand occupied Western Samoa, and from 1920 governed it under a League of Nations mandate. New Zealand rule was marked by a catastrophe:1

  • In November 1918, New Zealand authorities allowed the ship Talune, with passengers sick with influenza, to dock in Apia. The epidemic spread through the islands within a week. Around a fifth of the population died — while American Samoa, which imposed a quarantine, had no deaths. The administrator refused an American offer of help.
  • New Zealand administrators interfered in Samoan customs and the authority of the matai (family chiefs), banishing chiefs and stripping them of their titles.
02

Who were the key people?

  • Tupua Tamasese Lealofi III

    A paramount chief and leader of the Mau, shot on Black Saturday. He died the next day.

  • Olaf Frederick Nelson

    A wealthy Samoan-Swedish merchant who helped found the Mau in 1926 and was exiled by New Zealand.

  • The Women's Mau

    Women, including the wives of exiled and imprisoned leaders, who led the movement when men were in hiding or in prison.

  • Stephen Allen

    The New Zealand administrator of Samoa at the time of Black Saturday.

  • The Mau

    Tens of thousands of Samoans — most of the adult male population at its height — who supported the movement.

In their own words

03

What happened?

In 1926, Olaf Nelson and other leaders organised public meetings in Apia, and the Samoan League — O le Mau — was formed. The word mau means "firmly held opinion" or "testimony", and the movement's motto was Samoa mo Samoa, "Samoa for the Samoans".1

The Mau was committed to non-violence. Its members practised civil disobedience:1

  • They refused to pay taxes, boycotted imported goods, and organised their own events in parallel with official ones.
  • Supporters wore a uniform of a navy-blue lavalava with a white stripe, which the administration later banned.
  • When the administrator arrested Mau members, so many came forward to be arrested that the jails could not hold them; offered pardons, those arrested demanded to be tried and then refused to plead.
  • New Zealand exiled Nelson and other leaders. A petition sent to New Zealand quoted a Samoan proverb: "We are moved by love, but never driven by intimidation."

On 28 December 1929 — remembered as Black Saturday — the Mau organised a peaceful procession through Apia to welcome home a returning leader. When New Zealand police tried to arrest a man in the procession, a scuffle broke out. Police opened fire on the crowd, including with a machine gun from the police station. Up to eleven Samoans were killed, including Tupua Tamasese Lealofi III, who had been trying to calm the crowd; one New Zealand constable was also killed.1 Before he died, Tamasese urged his people not to seek revenge:

"My blood has been spilt for Samoa. I am proud to give it. Do not dream of avenging it, as it was spilt in maintaining peace."Tupua Tamasese Lealofi III, 29 December 1929, as recorded by the Mau
04

What happened next?

  • Repression: after Black Saturday, the Mau was declared a "seditious organisation". Men fled to the bush, pursued by marines; the Women's Mau continued to organise in their place.
  • Recognition: after the election of a Labour government in New Zealand in 1935, the Mau was recognised as a legitimate organisation, and Olaf Nelson was allowed to return in 1936.
  • Independence: Western Samoa became the first Pacific Island nation to regain its independence, in 1962.
  • Apology: in 2002, New Zealand's Prime Minister, Helen Clark, formally apologised to Samoa for the influenza epidemic, the shootings of Black Saturday, and the banishment of Samoan leaders.
05

Why does it matter?

The Mau was one of the most sustained non-violent independence movements in the colonial world, and it ultimately led to Samoa becoming the first independent Pacific Island nation. It drew on Samoan traditions of chiefly authority and community, and included women as leaders.

  • It shows how colonised peoples used disciplined non-violent resistance years before it became famous through Gandhi's campaigns.
  • Black Saturday and the 1918 epidemic reveal the human cost of colonial neglect and violence.
  • The 2002 apology is an example of a state acknowledging its colonial wrongs.

What do others say?How it was seen at the time, and where historians disagree

At the time

New Zealand officials blamed the unrest on "agitators", especially Olaf Nelson, and claimed that ordinary Samoans were content. A New Zealand inquiry exonerated the police. Mau supporters, and some New Zealand critics, argued that the shootings were unprovoked.

Historians' debates

  • Who led the Mau? Some historians emphasise the role of the part-European business elite, such as Nelson; others the authority of Samoan chiefs and the participation of ordinary Samoans and women.
  • What happened on Black Saturday? Accounts disagree about how the shooting began and how many died; estimates of the Samoan dead range from eight to eleven.
  • Colonial paternalism: historians have explored how New Zealand saw itself as a benevolent guardian, while its administrators misunderstood and undermined Samoan society.
How do we know?The evidence, and a source from the time
  • New Zealand government records and the reports of the inquiries into the shootings.
  • Photographs by Alfred John Tattersall of Mau leaders, processions and the funeral of Tamasese.
  • Samoan oral histories, songs and family accounts.

Reading it

A Mau demonstration in 1929, the year New Zealand police fired on a procession. Uniforms and orderly ranks were a statement: the Mau was disciplined and peaceful, and could not fairly be treated as a mob.

How is it remembered?Memorials, anniversaries and arguments over memory
  • Tamasese's tomb at Lepea, near Apia, is a place of remembrance.
  • The 2002 apology was accompanied by the gift of scholarships for Samoan students.
  • The Mau is commemorated in Samoa as a founding chapter in the story of independence.
