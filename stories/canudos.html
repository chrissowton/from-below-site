Canudos: The Backlands Commune

In the drought-stricken backlands, thousands of poor people followed a wandering preacher to build a community of their own. The new Brazilian Republic sent four armies to destroy it.

When
1893 – 1897
Where
Canudos (Belo Monte), Bahia, Brazil
Facts checked
2026-11-16

See it on the map →

01

What is the background to this event?

The sertão — the dry backlands of north-eastern Brazil — was one of the poorest regions in the Americas. Most people were landless farmers and cattle workers, many of African and Indigenous descent.1

  • Slavery was abolished in Brazil only in 1888. In 1889, a military coup overthrew the emperor and created a republic.1
  • For the people of the sertão, the only change the republic brought was higher taxes.1
  • Antônio Vicente Mendes Maciel, known as Antônio Conselheiro ("the Counsellor"), a wandering lay preacher, attracted followers from about 1874 by preaching salvation to the poor and repairing churches and cemeteries.1
02

Who were the key people?

  • Antônio Conselheiro

    The preacher who founded the community. He died, probably of dysentery, in September 1897, shortly before the end.

  • The people of Canudos

    Thousands of poor farmers, cowhands, formerly enslaved people and Indigenous people who settled there.

  • Colonel Antônio Moreira César

    The commander of the third expedition, who was killed in the attack.

  • Euclides da Cunha

    A journalist who reported on the war and later wrote Os Sertões (Rebellion in the Backlands, 1902).

In their own words

03

What happened?

In 1893, Conselheiro and his followers settled at Canudos, on the Vaza-Barris River in Bahia, and named it Belo Monte ("Beautiful Hill").1 It grew rapidly into one of the largest towns in Bahia, with thousands of houses. Residents farmed, raised goats, prayed and shared resources, and refused to pay the republic's taxes.

Local landowners and the Church saw the community as a threat, and the press branded its people as monarchist fanatics plotting to overthrow the republic.1 The government sent four military expeditions:1

  • In November 1896, a force of about 100 soldiers was attacked and driven back.
  • In January 1897, a second expedition of more than 500 men was also defeated.
  • In March 1897, Colonel Moreira César attacked with 1,300 troops and artillery. He was killed, and his army fled in panic.
  • Finally, more than 8,000 soldiers with machine guns and heavy artillery besieged the town, starving its people.

There was violence on both sides: the defenders of Canudos burned ranches around the town and killed retreating soldiers. But the army's final assault was a massacre. Canudos fell on 5 October 1897. Prisoners who had been promised their lives were killed in front of their families, and all 5,200 houses were destroyed.1 Estimates of the dead range from 5,000 to about 30,000.1

04

What happened next?

  • Desecration: Conselheiro's body was dug up and his head was taken to the medical school in Bahia to be studied for signs of "degeneracy".1
  • Only a few survivors: when resistance ended, only about 150 survivors remained in the town; women and children who had surrendered earlier were taken away by the army.1
  • The reservoir: in the late 1960s, the site of Canudos was flooded by the Cocorobó dam. In times of drought, the ruins reappear.
  • Literature: da Cunha's Os Sertões became a classic of Brazilian literature, and the war inspired Mario Vargas Llosa's novel The War of the End of the World (1981).
05

What do others say?

At the time

The government and press portrayed Canudos as a monarchist plot and its people as primitive fanatics. Da Cunha, who began by sharing these views, ended his book by calling the destruction a crime.

Historians' debates

  • Monarchist threat? Historians agree that Canudos posed no real threat to the republic. Conselheiro opposed the republic, especially civil marriage, but there was no conspiracy.1
  • Religious community or social revolt? Some historians see Canudos as a millenarian movement; others, such as Robert Levine, emphasise its social and economic character as a refuge for the poor.
  • Da Cunha's views: his account is the main source, but it is shaped by the scientific racism of his time, which saw the mixed-race people of the backlands as backward.1
  • The death toll: estimates vary widely, from 5,000 to about 30,000.1

07

How is it remembered?

  • The Canudos State Park in Bahia preserves parts of the battlefield.
  • Canudos is remembered in Brazilian literature, film and music, and by social movements of the rural poor.
08

Why does it matter?

Canudos was one of the deadliest civil conflicts in Brazilian history. A community of the rural poor, seeking to live by their own rules, was destroyed by a republic that claimed to stand for progress.

  • It shows how the poor and racially marginalised can be labelled as dangerous fanatics.
  • It raises questions about land, poverty and religion that remain alive in north-eastern Brazil.
  • Landless movements in Brazil today, such as the MST, have looked to Canudos as an inspiration.
