Gdeim Izik and the Sahrawi Struggle

Thousands of Sahrawis pitched tents in the desert to protest against poverty and discrimination in Moroccan-controlled Western Sahara. The camp's destruction led to deadly violence on both sides — and long prison sentences.

When
October – November 2010
Where
Gdeim Izik, near El Aaiún (Laayoune), Western Sahara

See it on the map →

01

What is the background to this event?

Western Sahara was a Spanish colony until 1975. When Spain withdrew, Morocco and Mauritania took control, and the Sahrawi independence movement, the Polisario Front, fought a war against them.

  • Mauritania withdrew in 1979. Morocco built a long defensive wall (the berm) and controls most of the territory.
  • A UN ceasefire in 1991 promised a referendum on self-determination, which has never been held.
  • Tens of thousands of Sahrawis live in refugee camps in Algeria. Morocco regards the territory as part of Morocco and offers autonomy.
  • Sahrawis in Moroccan-controlled areas complained of unemployment, poverty and discrimination.1
02

Who were the key people?

  • The camp's residents

    Sahrawi families, young people and the unemployed from El Aaiún and other towns.

  • Nayem Elgarhi

    A 14-year-old shot dead by Moroccan forces near the camp on 24 October 2010.

  • Aminatou Haidar

    A leading Sahrawi human rights campaigner, whose 2009 hunger strike drew international attention.

  • Moroccan security forces

    Police, gendarmes and auxiliary forces who dismantled the camp; several were killed.

03

What happened?

On the night of 9 October 2010, Sahrawis put up tents at Gdeim Izik, 12 km south-east of El Aaiún.1

  • The camp grew from a few hundred tents to several thousand. By November, around 5,000 people were living there, by some estimates; other estimates were much higher.1
  • The main demands were jobs, housing and an end to discrimination. Some protesters also called for independence.1
  • On 24 October, Moroccan forces fired on a vehicle trying to reach the camp, killing 14-year-old Nayem Elgarhi. Morocco said shots had been fired from the vehicle; the Polisario Front denied it.1
  • Early on 8 November, Moroccan forces dismantled the camp. Morocco said no firearms were used; some young protesters fought back with stones, knives and gas canisters.1
  • Rioting spread to El Aaiún, where homes, shops and public buildings were burned.1

Morocco said 11 members of its security forces were killed, as well as two civilians. Sahrawi groups said several Sahrawis were killed and many injured, and reported mass arrests and torture. The total death toll remains disputed.12

04

What happened next?

  • Trials: in 2013, a Moroccan military court sentenced 25 Sahrawis for the deaths of the security officers, nine of them to life imprisonment. A civilian court retried them in 2017, with similar sentences.2
  • Torture claims: the defendants and human rights groups said confessions were obtained under torture; Morocco denies this.2
  • The conflict: the 1991 ceasefire broke down in 2020. In the same year, the United States recognised Moroccan sovereignty.
05

What do others say?

At the time

Morocco said criminals and separatists had used the camp's residents as human shields and murdered its officers. Sahrawi organisations and the Polisario Front said a peaceful protest had been crushed.1

Debates since

  • Violence on both sides: members of the security forces were killed, some reportedly after being attacked with knives; Sahrawi civilians were also killed, injured and detained.12
  • The start of the Arab Spring? Most historians date the Arab Spring from Tunisia in December 2010, but some see Gdeim Izik as a forerunner.1
  • Sovereignty: Morocco, the Polisario Front and foreign governments disagree about the territory's status.
06

The evidence

  • Photographs and video from the camp and from El Aaiún.
  • Court records and the reports of trial observers.2
07

How is it remembered?

  • Sahrawi activists commemorate the camp each October; Moroccan authorities commemorate the members of the security forces who were killed.
08

Why does it matter?

Gdeim Izik showed how a peaceful protest over jobs and housing could become part of a long struggle over self-determination — and how quickly it could turn violent.

  • Some, including Noam Chomsky, have suggested it was the first protest of the Arab Spring.1
  • Western Sahara is often called Africa's last colony.

Questions to think about

  1. Why did Sahrawis choose to protest by building a camp?
  2. Why do accounts of the deaths differ so much?
  3. Were the trials fair?
  4. Should the people of Western Sahara have a referendum on their future?

What other events are like this?

  • 1921Abd el-Krim and the Republic of the Rif — Berber mountain tribes inflicted one of the worst defeats on a European army in colonial history and founded a republic. Spain and France needed hundreds of thousands of troops — and poison gas — to crush it.
  • 2010The Tunisian Revolution — A street vendor's desperate protest in a small town sparked a month of uprising that toppled a dictator of 23 years and began the Arab Spring.
  • 1984The Kanak Struggle: 'Les Événements' — The Indigenous Kanak people of New Caledonia boycotted elections and set up a provisional government. Four years of violence on all sides ended in a historic peace agreement — and a debate about independence that continues.
