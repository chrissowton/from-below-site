The vote & democracy

The Chartists on Kennington Common

With revolution spreading across Europe, the Chartists gathered on a south London common to send a giant petition for the vote to Parliament. The government swore in 100,000 special constables to stop them.

When
10 April 1848
Where
Kennington Common, London, United Kingdom

01

What is the background to this event?

Chartism was a working-class movement for political reform in Britain, named after the People's Charter of 1838.1

  • The Charter called for six reforms, including a vote for every man over 21 and a secret ballot.1
  • Parliament had already rejected two huge petitions, in 1839 and 1842.1
  • After the revolution in Paris in February 1848, Chartist activity increased.1

In their own words

03

What happened?

The Chartists called a mass meeting on Kennington Common for 10 April 1848, after which a procession would take a third petition to Parliament.1

  • Parliament revived an old law limiting the number of people who could present a petition, and passed a new law against sedition.1
  • The government recruited 100,000 special constables.1
  • Feargus O'Connor addressed a large crowd — estimates reach 50,000 — and called off the procession.31
  • The meeting was peaceful; the petition went to Parliament in three cabs.1
04

What happened next?

  • Repression: later in 1848, some Chartists drilled and armed; William Cuffay was convicted of "conspiring to levy war" and transported to Australia.1
  • Decline: Chartism declined after 1848.1
  • Victory later: Britain eventually adopted all but one of the Charter's six points.1
05

Why does it matter?

Kennington Common shows how Britain avoided revolution in 1848 — by force and by fear as much as by consent.

  • The Chartists' demands became the basis of British democracy.
  • Later in 1848, William Cuffay was convicted of 'conspiring to levy war' and transported to Australia.1
  • Britain eventually adopted all but one of the Charter's six points.1

What do others say?How it was seen at the time, and where historians disagree

At the time

The government and press presented 10 April as a triumph of order over revolution; Chartists saw intimidation.1

Debates since

  • A fiasco? Older histories mocked the meeting as a failure; historians such as Dorothy Thompson have taken Chartism more seriously as a mass movement.1
  • Violence: the meeting itself was peaceful; some Chartists later prepared for armed resistance, and the state used new laws and transportation against them.1

What is it?
Photograph (daguerreotype)
Made by, and when
William Edward Kilburn, 10 April 1848

One of the earliest photographs ever taken of a large crowd. Kilburn photographed the rally for the royal family; the picture lay forgotten in the Royal Collection until the 1980s.

Reading it

This is one of the earliest photographs ever taken of a large crowd, made for the royal family and forgotten until the 1980s. It records the scale of the meeting in a way no report could, but not what anyone said or felt. Whether the crowd looks threatening depends very much on who is looking.
