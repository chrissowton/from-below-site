The Sicilian Vespers

At the hour of evening prayer on Easter Monday, a quarrel outside a Palermo church set off a rising against the island's French rulers. Within weeks, French rule in Sicily was over.

When
Easter 1282
Where

01

What is the background to this event?

In 1266, the French prince Charles of Anjou had conquered the Kingdom of Sicily with the support of the pope.1

  • His government was exploitative, and excluded native Sicilians from power.1

In their own words

03

What happened?

The rising began with an incident in Palermo at Easter 1282 and quickly spread across most of Sicily.1

  • According to tradition, a French soldier insulted a Sicilian woman outside a church at the time of Vespers (evening prayer), and was killed by her husband.
  • Within six weeks, about 13,000 French men and women had been killed or expelled.1
  • Charles lost control of the island.1
  • The Sicilians offered the crown to Peter III of Aragon.1

04

What happened next?

  • War: the Aragonese intervention began the War of the Sicilian Vespers, which lasted until 1302.1
  • Division: Sicily was ruled from Aragon, while the Angevins kept southern Italy.
05

What do others say?

At the time

The pope supported Charles and excommunicated the rebels and Peter of Aragon.

Debates since

  • Spontaneous or planned? Some historians argue the rising was prepared by Aragonese and Byzantine agents; others stress popular anger.2
  • Violence: the killing of thousands of French people, including women, was a massacre of civilians as well as a revolt.1

07

How is it remembered?

  • In the nineteenth century the Vespers became a symbol of the Italian Risorgimento: Verdi wrote an opera, and Hayez painted the scene.
08

Why does it matter?

The Vespers is one of the most famous popular risings against foreign rule in European history.

  • It also shows how a popular revolt can lead to rule by another foreign power.

Questions to think about

  1. Why did Sicilians resent Angevin rule?
  2. How did a single incident set off a revolt across the island?
  3. How should we judge the killing of French civilians?
  4. Why did the rebels turn to another foreign king?
09

What other events are like this?

  • 1378The Revolt of the Ciompi — The wool workers of Florence, excluded from the guilds that ran the city, seized the government — and for a few weeks a wool-carder sat at the head of the republic.
  • 1520The Revolt of the Comuneros — The cities of Castile rose against a foreign-raised young king and his heavy taxes. They set up their own councils, and the movement turned against the great nobles before it was crushed.
  • 1358The Jacquerie — After the French nobility lost a war and their king, peasants north of Paris rose against the lords who still demanded their labour and money. Their name became the word for a peasant revolt.
