ACT UP and the Fight Against AIDS

Facing a deadly epidemic and government neglect, AIDS activists used direct action and expert knowledge to change how drugs are tested and approved.

When
1987 – 1990s
Where
New York City and Rockville, Maryland, USA

See it on the map →

01

What is the background to this event?

The first cases of what became known as AIDS were reported in the United States in 1981. By 1987 tens of thousands of Americans had died, a large proportion of them gay men, as well as drug users, people with haemophilia, and growing numbers of women and people of colour.

The response of governments and institutions was widely seen as inadequate:

  • President Ronald Reagan did not give a major speech on AIDS until 1987.
  • Many politicians and religious leaders presented AIDS as a consequence of immoral behaviour.
  • Only one drug, AZT, had been approved, at a cost of around $10,000 a year — one of the most expensive drugs ever sold at the time.
  • The Food and Drug Administration (FDA) approval process could take many years, while people were dying within months.

Existing organisations such as the Gay Men's Health Crisis concentrated on care and support. Some activists felt that political action was needed.

02

Who were the key people?

  • Larry Kramer

    A writer and co-founder of the Gay Men's Health Crisis, whose speech in March 1987 led to the founding of ACT UP. He died in 2020.

  • The Treatment and Data Committee

    Activists who taught themselves the science of drug trials and became expert negotiators with researchers and regulators.

  • The Women's Caucus

    Members who campaigned to change the official definition of AIDS so that it included illnesses common in women.

  • Gran Fury

    An artists' collective within ACT UP that created powerful posters and graphics.

  • The members

    Thousands of people, many of them living with HIV, who took part in meetings and actions in New York and in chapters across the world.

03

What happened?

ACT UP (the AIDS Coalition to Unleash Power) was formed on 12 March 1987 at the Lesbian and Gay Community Services Center in New York, after a speech by Larry Kramer. According to one participant, Kramer asked the audience: "Do we want to start a new organization devoted to political action?" The answer was "a resounding yes", and around 300 people met two days later to form the group.1

ACT UP combined confrontational protest with detailed research:1

  • Its first action, on Wall Street in March 1987, targeted the drug company Burroughs Wellcome and the FDA; in March 1988 over 100 people were arrested at a second Wall Street demonstration.
  • On 11 October 1988, between 1,100 and 1,500 activists shut down the FDA's headquarters in Rockville, Maryland, in "Seize Control of the FDA", chanting "Hey, hey, FDA, how many people have you killed today?"
  • On 14 September 1989, seven activists chained themselves to a balcony in the New York Stock Exchange with a banner reading "SELL WELLCOME". Days later, Burroughs Wellcome cut the price of AZT from around $10,000 to $6,400 a year.
  • On 10 December 1989, the "Stop the Church" protest at St Patrick's Cathedral targeted Cardinal John O'Connor's opposition to safer-sex education and condom distribution.

Activists later reflected that after the FDA action, "government agencies dealing with AIDS, particularly the FDA and NIH, began to listen to us, to include us in decision-making, even to ask for our input."1

04

What happened next?

  • Faster access to drugs: the FDA introduced "parallel track" access to experimental drugs and, in 1992, an accelerated approval process. People with AIDS gained seats on research committees.
  • Women and AIDS: after a campaign led by ACT UP's Women's Caucus and others, the Centers for Disease Control expanded its definition of AIDS in 1993 to include conditions affecting women, giving many access to benefits and treatment.
  • New treatments: in 1996 combination antiretroviral therapy transformed HIV from a death sentence into a manageable condition for those who could get treatment.
  • A global struggle: activists went on to campaign for affordable drugs in Africa and elsewhere, where millions still lacked access to treatment.
05

What do others say?

At the time

Many politicians, church leaders and commentators condemned ACT UP's tactics as offensive and counter-productive, especially the disruption of a mass at St Patrick's Cathedral, during which a communion wafer was crumbled. Some gay rights organisations also worried that its confrontational approach would alienate potential allies.

Historians' debates

  • Did it work? Historians such as Steven Epstein have shown how activists gained real influence over research; some scientists argued that faster approval risked releasing ineffective or unsafe drugs.
  • Inside tensions: ACT UP experienced divisions between those focused on drug research and those emphasising wider social issues such as housing, race and the rights of women and drug users.
  • Whose story? Sarah Schulman's history emphasises the role of women, people of colour and the many small affinity groups, rather than a few famous leaders.

07

How is it remembered?

  • The documentaries How to Survive a Plague (2012) and United in Anger (2012) brought ACT UP's story to a new generation.
  • The French film 120 BPM (2017) dramatised ACT UP Paris.
  • ACT UP chapters still exist, and the group's methods are taught to new generations of activists.
08

Why does it matter?

ACT UP changed how medicine is done. A group of patients and activists, many of them dying, forced government agencies and drug companies to listen to them, and changed the rules for testing and approving new drugs. It also showed how anger, grief and creativity could be turned into effective political action.

  • Its model of "patient expertise" has influenced activism on breast cancer, rare diseases and other conditions.
  • Its graphic design and street theatre — such as SILENCE = DEATH — set new standards for protest.
  • It remains an essential part of LGBTQ+ history and of the history of public health.

Questions to think about

  1. Is it ever justified to disrupt a religious service in protest? Why or why not?
  2. How did people without medical training become experts on drug research?
  3. Why do you think governments were so slow to respond to AIDS?
  4. What lessons might ACT UP offer for responses to other health crises?
