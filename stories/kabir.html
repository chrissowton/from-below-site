Kabir, the Weaver Poet

A weaver from Varanasi mocked the priests and mullahs alike and sang that God belonged to no caste or religion. Threatened by both Hindus and Muslims in his lifetime, he was claimed by both after his death.

When
15th century
Where
Varanasi (Benares), India
Facts checked

01

What is the background to this event?

In fifteenth-century north India, Hindu and Muslim religious authorities held great power, and caste ruled social life.

  • Kabir was raised in a family of Muslim weavers in Varanasi, a holy city of Hinduism.1
  • He became a disciple of Ramananda, a teacher who welcomed people of all castes.1
  • His dates are uncertain: some historians favour 1398–1448, others 1440–1518.1

03

What happened?

Kabir composed songs and short verses in the everyday language of the people, which were sung and passed on by word of mouth.

  • He criticised what he saw as the empty rituals of both Hinduism and Islam.1
  • He taught that truth was found by those who live righteously and give up the ego.1
  • He rejected caste and the authority of priests.
  • He was threatened by both Hindus and Muslims for his views.1
  • After his death, Hindus and Muslims both claimed him; legend says they found only flowers under his shroud, and divided them.1

04

What happened next?

  • The Kabir Panth: a community of followers continues today.1
  • Sikhism: many of Kabir's verses are included in the Guru Granth Sahib, the Sikh scripture.1
05

What do others say?

At the time

Religious authorities on both sides saw him as a heretic.1

Debates since

  • Which Kabir? Many verses attributed to him were composed later; scholars debate which are authentic.2
  • Religion or social reform? Some see him as a mystic, others as a radical social critic.
  • Non-violence: his was a movement of words and songs.
06

The evidence

  • Collections of his verses: the Bijak, the Kabir Granthavali and the Guru Granth Sahib.2

Primary source: Painting

What is it?
Painting
Made by, and when
Mughal artist, 17th century

A painting of Kabir with two followers, made under the Mughal emperors more than a century after his death. By then his songs were being collected in Sikh scripture and sung by followers across northern India.

Questions to ask of this source

  1. How does the artist show Kabir: as a saint, a teacher or a working man?
  2. Why might a Muslim imperial court be interested in a poet who mocked both Hindus and Muslims?
  3. How were Kabir's songs passed down if he never wrote them?
07

How is it remembered?

  • Kabir's songs are performed across India and Pakistan, and his shrine at Maghar is shared by Hindus and Muslims.
08

Why does it matter?

Kabir gave a voice to working people and challenged religious authority and caste in a way that still resonates.

  • His songs are sung by Dalit, folk and protest movements today.

Questions to think about

  1. Why did Kabir criticise both Hindu and Muslim authorities?
  2. Why was it significant that he was a weaver?
  3. Why did both religions claim him after his death?
  4. Why are his songs still sung today?
