The General Strike against Uruguay's Coup

Within hours of the president dissolving parliament, workers occupied factories across the country. For fifteen days, the general strike defied the new dictatorship.

When
27 June – 11 July 1973
Where
Montevideo and across Uruguay
Facts checked
2026-11-16

See it on the map →

01

What is the background to this event?

Uruguay was once called "the Switzerland of South America" for its stable democracy and welfare state. By the late 1960s, economic crisis brought strikes, student protests and repression.

  • The Tupamaros, a Marxist urban guerrilla group, carried out bank robberies, kidnappings and killings, including of police officers and a US official, Dan Mitrione, in 1970.
  • The government declared states of emergency and, in 1971–72, gave the army control of the fight against the guerrillas. By 1972, the Tupamaros had been defeated, many through torture.
  • The trade union federation, the Convención Nacional de Trabajadores (CNT), had long pledged to call a general strike if there were a coup.

03

What happened?

On 27 June 1973, President Juan María Bordaberry dissolved parliament and imposed rule with a junta of military generals. The official reason was to crush the Tupamaros.1

  • Within hours, the CNT called a general strike. Workers occupied factories throughout the country, and many ports, banks and shops closed.1
  • On 2 July, the government dissolved the CNT, and two days later it allowed employers to sack workers who did not return to work.1
  • Soldiers and police cleared occupied factories, often repeatedly, as workers re-occupied them.
  • On 9 July, a huge demonstration in central Montevideo was dispersed by police and troops.

The strike ended after fifteen days, with nearly all unions broken and most union leaders in jail, dead or exiled to Argentina.1

04

What happened next?

  • Dictatorship: the military dictatorship lasted until 1985. Uruguay had one of the highest rates of political imprisonment in the world, and torture was widespread. Hundreds of people were killed or disappeared, many in Argentina as part of Operation Condor.
  • Return of democracy: unions and parties were illegal until a series of general strikes in 1984 helped force the military to accept civilian rule.1
  • Former prisoners: José Mujica, a former Tupamaro who spent about 14 years in prison, became president of Uruguay in 2010.
05

What do others say?

At the time

The government claimed the coup was needed to defeat subversion. The unions argued that it was an attack on democracy and workers' rights.

Historians' debates

  • Guerrillas and the coup: historians debate how far the Tupamaros' violence — including killings and kidnappings — provoked the military's rise; others argue the military used it as a pretext, since the guerrillas were already defeated by 1972.
  • Could the strike have won? Some argue the strike was doomed without support from the army or parties; others that it laid the foundation for later resistance.
  • Justice: an amnesty law for military crimes was upheld in referendums in 1989 and 2009, but has since been challenged in the courts.

07

How is it remembered?

  • A square in Montevideo is named after the general strike of 1973.
  • Each year on 20 May, a silent march in Montevideo remembers the disappeared.
08

Why does it matter?

The general strike of 1973 was one of the longest general strikes against a coup in history. It showed that the dictatorship faced opposition from the start.

  • It shows how workers can resist an attack on democracy.
  • It raises questions about the cycle of guerrilla violence and state repression.

Open questions

  1. Why did the unions respond to the coup with a general strike?
  2. Why do you think the strike could not stop the dictatorship?
  3. How did guerrilla violence and state repression feed each other?
  4. How should societies deal with crimes committed under dictatorships?
09

What other events are like this?

  • 1977The Mothers of the Plaza de Mayo — The mothers of Argentina's 'disappeared' walked in silent circles in front of the presidential palace, in open defiance of a military dictatorship.
  • 1919The Winnipeg General Strike — For six weeks, 30,000 workers brought Canada's third-largest city to a halt to win the right to collective bargaining. It ended with 'Bloody Saturday' — and transformed Canadian politics.
  • 1973The Athens Polytechnic Uprising — Students occupied their university and broadcast a call for 'Bread, Education, Freedom' against Greece's military dictatorship. Before dawn on the third day, a tank crashed through the gates.
