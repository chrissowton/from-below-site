The Treachery of the Blue Books: Wales Answers Back

Three English officials who spoke no Welsh reported that the Welsh language and Welsh chapels were holding the people back, and called the Welsh dirty, lazy and immoral. Wales answered with fury, and the reports became a turning point in Welsh identity.

When
1846 – 1848
Where

01

What is the background to this event?

In the 1830s and 1840s, Wales saw riots in Merthyr Tydfil and Tredegar, the Rebecca Riots and the Chartist march on Newport.1

  • The government believed the answer was education — in English.1
  • Most people in Wales spoke only Welsh. Education was not compulsory; most schools were run by churches and charities, and many Welsh speakers learned to read in Nonconformist Sunday schools.1
  • In some schools, children caught speaking Welsh were made to wear a wooden token, the 'Welsh Not', sometimes followed by a beating. It was not government policy, but many teachers and parents believed children should learn English.2

In their own words

03

What happened?

In 1846, the government sent three commissioners to report on education in Wales. They spoke no Welsh; Welsh-speaking assistants visited many of the schools.1

  • The reports were published in November 1847 in three blue-covered volumes.1
  • They found schools badly lacking and blamed the Welsh language and Nonconformity. They described the Welsh as dirty, ignorant, lazy and immoral, often repeating the views of landowners and Anglican clergy.1
  • Wales reacted with fury, and the anger lasted for years. Ieuan Gwynedd challenged the reports' statistics; Henry Richard and others made speeches; Welsh journals published angry replies; and radical leaders held mass meetings in protest.1
  • A satirical play called the reports Brad y Llyfrau Gleision, 'the treachery of the Blue Books', recalling a legendary massacre of the Britons by the Saxons. The name stuck.1
04

What happened next?

  • An admission: in 1848, Commissioner Symons admitted in a letter that some witnesses had been prejudiced and some allegations too extreme.1
  • No action: the inquiry did not lead to government action; the anger was mainly at what it said about Welsh morals.1
  • Identity: according to the historian John Davies, the reaction strengthened the idea that the chapel people were the true Welsh.1
  • The Welsh Not: evidence of its use runs to the start of the 20th century.2
05

Why does it matter?

The Blue Books are seen today as a moment when the British state told the Welsh that their language was a problem. The anger they provoked helped shape a Welsh identity built around language and chapel.1

  • In 1848, one of the commissioners admitted that some witnesses had been prejudiced and some allegations too extreme.1
  • According to the historian John Davies, the reaction strengthened the idea that the chapel people were the true Welsh.1
  • The reports are still known in Wales as Brad y Llyfrau Gleision, the Treachery of the Blue Books.1

What do others say?How it was seen at the time, and where historians disagree

At the time

The commissioners presented their reports as an impartial inquiry; Welsh critics saw an attack on their language, religion and character.1

Debates since

  • Colonial? The writer Simon Brooks calls the reports "colonial diktat"; others stress that the reports also praised the Welsh hunger for education.1
  • The Welsh Not: it was not government policy but was used by teachers, at a time when many Welsh people believed children would benefit from learning English.2

Reading it

The heading — 'Evils of the Welsh Language' — says it all. Reading the commissioners' own words shows why the reports caused such anger. A government report like this includes the voices of clergy and officials it chose to hear; the people being judged had little say in it.
