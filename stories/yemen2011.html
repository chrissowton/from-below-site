Yemen's Revolution and Change Square

Young Yemenis built a tent city outside Sanaa University and demanded the end of 33 years of rule by Ali Abdullah Saleh. A journalist and mother of three, Tawakkol Karman, became the first Arab woman to win the Nobel Peace Prize.

When
January 2011 – February 2012
Where
Change Square, Sanaa, and across Yemen

See it on the map →

01

What is the background to this event?

Ali Abdullah Saleh had ruled North Yemen from 1978, and united Yemen from 1990. Yemen was the poorest country in the Arab world, with high unemployment and widespread corruption.1

  • In January 2011, inspired by Tunisia, students and activists began to protest.1
  • The government had proposed changes to the constitution that critics feared would allow Saleh to rule for life.1
02

Who were the key people?

  • Tawakkol Karman

    A journalist and founder of Women Journalists Without Chains, who led early protests.

  • The young people of Change Square

    Students, unemployed graduates, tribespeople and women who camped for months.

  • Ali Abdullah Saleh

    President of Yemen, 1978–2012.

  • Ali Mohsen al-Ahmar

    A senior general who defected to the protesters in March 2011.

03

What happened?

On 3 February 2011, Tawakkol Karman called a "Day of Rage". Tens of thousands protested in Sanaa, Aden and Taiz.1

  • Protesters built a tent city outside Sanaa University, which they named Change Square.1
  • On 18 March, gunmen in plain clothes fired on protesters in Sanaa, killing 52 people. The massacre, known as the Friday of Dignity, led to mass defections from the army and government.1
  • Fighting broke out in Sanaa between government forces and the armed supporters of the powerful al-Ahmar tribal family.1
  • On 3 June, an attack on the presidential compound wounded Saleh and killed several others.1
  • On 23 November, Saleh signed a deal brokered by the Gulf Cooperation Council, handing power to his deputy in return for immunity from prosecution.1
  • Many protesters rejected the immunity deal.1

Estimates of the number of people killed during the uprising range from several hundred to about 2,000, including protesters, soldiers and armed tribesmen.12

04

What happened next?

  • Transition: Abdrabbuh Mansour Hadi was elected unopposed in February 2012, and Saleh stepped down after 33 years.1
  • Nobel Prize: in 2011, Tawakkol Karman shared the Nobel Peace Prize with Ellen Johnson Sirleaf and Leymah Gbowee of Liberia.3
  • War: a national dialogue followed, but in 2014 the Houthi movement, allied with Saleh, seized Sanaa. A Saudi-led coalition intervened in 2015, and Yemen fell into a devastating civil war. Saleh was killed by the Houthis in 2017.
05

What do others say?

At the time

Saleh's government blamed the opposition parties and foreign interference; protesters insisted the movement was independent.1

Debates since

  • Violence: the protest camps were largely peaceful, but the uprising also involved armed fighting between government forces and tribal militias, and armed groups took advantage of the chaos.1
  • Hijacked? Many young activists argue their revolution was taken over by established parties, generals and tribal leaders.
  • Justice: no one has been convicted for the Friday of Dignity killings in a fair trial.2

07

How is it remembered?

  • Tawakkol Karman's megaphone is displayed in the Nobel Prize Museum in Stockholm.
08

Why does it matter?

Yemen's revolution brought together people from very different backgrounds and removed a long-ruling president — but its hopes were overtaken by war.

  • It showed the leading role of women in the Arab Spring.
  • It raises hard questions about why revolutions succeed or fail.

Questions to think about

  1. Why did so many Yemenis join the protests?
  2. Why did the Friday of Dignity massacre change the course of events?
  3. Was the immunity deal a price worth paying?
  4. Why did the revolution end in civil war?
09

What other events are like this?

  • 2010The Tunisian Revolution — A street vendor's desperate protest in a small town sparked a month of uprising that toppled a dictator of 23 years and began the Arab Spring.
  • 1964Sudan's October Revolution — When police killed a student at a university debate about the war in the south, Sudanese professionals, workers and students launched a general strike that brought down a military dictatorship in days.
  • 1954Bahrain's National Union Committee — After clashes between Sunni and Shia Bahrainis, reformers from both communities came together to demand an elected assembly, trade unions and an end to British control. Their leaders were exiled to a remote Atlantic island.
