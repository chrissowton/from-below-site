The 1959 Oil Workers' Strike in Fiji

Workers at the oil companies in Suva went on strike, and the dispute exploded into rioting in the capital. For a moment, indigenous Fijian and Indo-Fijian workers stood together — until the chiefs intervened.

When
December 1959
Where
Suva, Fiji

See it on the map →

01

What is the background to this event?

In the 1950s, Fiji was a British colony. Its population was divided between indigenous Fijians and Indo-Fijians, descendants of indentured labourers brought from India.

  • Colonial rule depended on Fijian chiefs and kept the communities apart.
  • Wages were low and the cost of living was rising.
  • The Wholesale and Retail General Workers' Union organised workers of both communities.1

03

What happened?

In December 1959, James Anthony and Apisai Tora led a militant strike of workers at the oil companies.1

  • The strike drew support from both Fijian and Indo-Fijian workers.1
  • Strikers and their supporters clashed with police in Suva, and the dispute turned into rioting, with shops and property damaged.
  • The colonial authorities brought in police and troops and imposed a curfew.
  • The Fijian chiefs opposed the strike and urged Fijian workers to return to work.1

04

What happened next?

  • Division: the chiefs and colonial government worked to keep Fijian and Indo-Fijian workers apart.2
  • Independence: Fiji became independent in 1970.
  • Tora later moved between multi-racial politics and Fijian ethnic nationalism, and was jailed for his role in the 2000 coup.1
05

What do others say?

At the time

The authorities blamed agitators for the riots; the union blamed low wages and the employers.2

Debates since

  • Violence: the rioting involved damage to property and clashes with police; the details and casualties are poorly recorded.
  • A lost chance? Historians such as Brij Lal see 1959 as a moment when multi-ethnic labour politics might have taken a different path.2
06

The evidence

  • Colonial Office reports and the Fijian press.
07

How is it remembered?

  • The strike is remembered by Fiji's labour movement as an early example of unity across communities.
08

Why does it matter?

The 1959 strike was a rare moment when workers from Fiji's two main communities acted together.

  • It shows how class solidarity can challenge colonial divisions — and how fragile it can be.

Questions to think about

  1. Why did colonial rule keep Fiji's communities apart?
  2. Why did the chiefs oppose the strike?
  3. Why did the strike turn into rioting?
  4. Why has class solidarity in Fiji been so hard to sustain?
09

What other events are like this?

  • 1937The Butler Riots: Trinidad's Oilfield Strike — Oil workers led by the preacher 'Buzz' Butler struck against low wages and racism. The violence that followed killed police and strikers alike — and the island's labour movement was born.
  • 1935The Copperbelt Strike of 1935 — When the colonial government raised the tax on African mineworkers, they walked out at three of the Copperbelt's great mines. Police shot six people dead — but the strike is remembered as the birth of Zambian nationalism.
  • 1947The All-Malaya Hartal — Malay radicals, Chinese and Indian groups united behind a 'People's Constitution' for an independent, democratic Malaya with equal citizenship for all. On one day, they shut down the country.
