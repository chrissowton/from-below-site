Tiananmen Square, 1989

Students and workers occupied the heart of Beijing for seven weeks, calling for freedom of speech and an end to corruption, until the army cleared the streets with tanks and gunfire.

When
15 April – 4 June 1989
Where
Beijing, China
Facts checked

01

What is the background to this event?

After the death of Mao Zedong in 1976, China's new leader, Deng Xiaoping, launched economic reforms that opened the country to markets and foreign investment. The reforms brought rapid growth, but also new problems:1

  • Inflation soared in 1988: official figures reported that consumer prices in Beijing rose by 30 per cent between 1987 and 1988, alarming salaried workers.
  • Reforms weakened the Mao-era "iron rice bowl" of guaranteed lifetime employment, and corruption and privilege among officials and their families caused widespread anger.
  • A "New Enlightenment" movement among intellectuals promoted ideas of democracy and humanism, and students had already protested for political reform in 1986–87.

The Communist Party leadership was divided between reformers, associated with General Secretary Hu Yaobang and Premier Zhao Ziyang, and conservatives. After the 1986–87 protests, Hu was forced to resign, accused of being too "soft".1

03

What happened?

Hu Yaobang died on 15 April 1989. Students gathered in Tiananmen Square, in the heart of Beijing, to mourn him and to call for political reform, freedom of the press and an end to corruption.1

The movement grew rapidly:1

  • On 26 April, the People's Daily published an editorial, reflecting Deng Xiaoping's views, which branded the movement as anti-party "turmoil". It backfired: the next day, some 50,000–100,000 students marched through Beijing.
  • On 13 May, hundreds of students began a hunger strike in the square, timed to coincide with the visit of the Soviet leader Mikhail Gorbachev. The hunger strike won enormous public sympathy, and by the afternoon some 300,000 people had gathered.
  • Workers, journalists, teachers and citizens joined in, and protests spread to some 400 cities across China.

On 19 May, Zhao Ziyang visited the hunger strikers in the square and, with tears in his eyes, told them "We came too late." It was his last public appearance.1 The next day, 20 May, the government declared martial law, and as many as 300,000 troops were sent to Beijing. But hundreds of thousands of Beijing residents blocked the troops' advance, peacefully, for two weeks.1 On 30 May, art students erected a ten-metre statue, the "Goddess of Democracy", facing Mao's portrait.1

On the night of 3–4 June, the army advanced into central Beijing along the city's main roads, firing on crowds who tried to block them. Much of the killing took place not in the square but along Chang'an Avenue, especially near Muxidi, about five kilometres to the west. Early on 4 June, after negotiations led by the singer Hou Dejian, the remaining students left the square.1 Estimates of the death toll range from several hundred to several thousand, with thousands more wounded.1 On 5 June, an unknown man stood in front of a column of tanks on Chang'an Avenue, briefly halting them — an image that became one of the most famous photographs of the century.1

04

What happened next?

  • Repression: thousands of people were arrested, and some workers were executed. Many student leaders and intellectuals escaped abroad with the help of a secret network in Hong Kong known as Operation Yellowbird.
  • Economic reform, political control: political reform was halted, but from 1992 Deng accelerated market-oriented economic reforms under strict party control.
  • Silence: the events remain one of the most heavily censored topics in China. Online, people use code names such as "May 35th" to refer to them.
  • Hong Kong: for thirty years, tens of thousands of people gathered every 4 June for a candlelight vigil in Victoria Park, Hong Kong. The vigil was banned from 2020, and its organisers were prosecuted.
  • Liu Xiaobo: one of the hunger strikers of June 1989, the writer Liu Xiaobo, was awarded the Nobel Peace Prize in 2010 while in prison, and died in custody in 2017.
05

What do others say?

At the time and since

The Chinese government described the protests first as a "counter-revolutionary rebellion", later as "turmoil", and now as "the political turmoil between the spring and summer of 1989". It argues that the use of force ensured the stability that made China's later economic growth possible. Protesters, human rights organisations and most historians describe the killings as a massacre.

Historians' debates

  • How many died? The Chinese government has given a figure of around 200–300 deaths, including soldiers. Other estimates, from the Chinese Red Cross (later withdrawn), diplomats and researchers, range from several hundred to several thousand.
  • Students or workers? Early accounts focused on the students. Historians now stress the role of workers and ordinary Beijing residents, who made up many of those killed and who were punished most severely.
  • What did the protesters want? Many students called for reform within the system — dialogue, press freedom, an end to corruption — rather than the overthrow of the Communist Party. Historians debate how far "democracy" meant Western-style multi-party government.
06

The evidence

  • Photographs and video taken by Chinese and foreign journalists, and by the protesters themselves.
  • Diplomatic cables, since declassified, from embassies in Beijing.
  • The Tiananmen Papers, documents said to come from internal Communist Party records; their authenticity has been debated.
  • The victims' list compiled by the Tiananmen Mothers, and testimony from survivors and soldiers.

07

How is it remembered?

  • Inside mainland China, public commemoration is forbidden and the topic is censored online and in textbooks.
  • The Goddess of Democracy has been reproduced in Hong Kong, Taiwan, Canada and elsewhere; Hong Kong's June 4th Museum was closed in 2021.
  • Memorials and annual vigils are held by Chinese communities around the world, and in Taiwan.
08

Why does it matter?

The protests of 1989 were the largest popular movement in the history of the People's Republic of China. For seven weeks, students, workers and citizens of Beijing and hundreds of other cities demanded a say in how they were governed. Their suppression shaped the course of modern China.

  • The crackdown set the pattern for China's combination of economic opening and political control.
  • The image of Tank Man has become a worldwide symbol of individual courage against state power.
  • The struggle to remember — by the Tiananmen Mothers, exiles and people in Hong Kong — has itself become a form of resistance.

What other events are like this?

  • 1980The Gwangju Uprising — Students and citizens in Gwangju rose against a military coup. Paratroopers killed hundreds, but the uprising became the foundation of South Korean democracy.
  • 1980The Gdańsk Shipyard Strike and Solidarity — A strike over the sacking of a crane operator grew into the first independent trade union in the Soviet bloc, with ten million members.
  • 1986The People Power Revolution — More than two million unarmed Filipinos filled a Manila highway to protect rebel soldiers and end Ferdinand Marcos's twenty-year rule.
