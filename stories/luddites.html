The Luddites

Skilled textile workers, signing their letters 'Ned Ludd', smashed the machines that were cutting their wages. The government sent 12,000 troops against them and made machine-breaking a hanging offence.

When
1811 – 1816
Where
Nottinghamshire, the West Riding of Yorkshire and Lancashire, England
Facts checked
2026-11-22

See it on the map →

01

What is the background to this event?

In the early 19th century, much of England's textile work was done by skilled workers in their own homes or small workshops.1

  • The wars with Napoleon brought high unemployment and inflation between 1810 and 1812.1
  • Manufacturers began using new machinery and cheaper practices to get around the customary standards of the trades, cutting wages and the quality of goods.1
  • The historian Malcolm Thomis argues that machine-breaking was one of the very few tactics workers could use to put pressure on employers.1

In their own words

03

What happened?

The movement began in Arnold, Nottinghamshire, on 11 March 1811. Over the next two years it spread through the Midlands, Yorkshire and Lancashire.1

  • Luddites drilled at night on the moors and raided workshops to smash particular machines: wide knitting frames in the Midlands, steam-powered looms in Lancashire, and shearing frames in Yorkshire.1
  • They also held public demonstrations and sent letters to employers and officials explaining their demands and threatening further action. Some letters called for a minimum wage and an end to child labour.1
  • In Yorkshire, croppers broke the frames with a hammer they called 'Enoch', after the man who made the machines.1
  • Mill owners shot at Luddites. A group led by George Mellor killed the mill owner William Horsfall, who had boasted that he would ride up to his saddle in Luddite blood.1
  • The government sent some 12,000 troops — more, the historian Eric Hobsbawm wrote, than Wellington had taken to Portugal in 1808.1
04

What happened next?

  • A hanging offence: in 1812, Parliament made machine-breaking a capital crime. Lord Byron spoke against it in the House of Lords.1
  • Trials: at a mass trial in York in January 1813, more than 60 men were charged; 30 were acquitted, but others were hanged or transported. The harsh sentences quickly ended the movement.1
  • Afterwards: machine-breaking returned in the countryside in the Swing Riots of 1830.1
05

What do others say?

At the time

Employers and the government saw criminal violence and a threat of revolution; the Luddites' letters presented their actions as defending the customs of their trades.1

Debates since

  • Against technology? Historians such as Malcolm Thomis argue that machines were simply "a conveniently exposed target"; the Luddites attacked employers, not progress.1
  • Violence: Luddites destroyed machinery and killed at least one mill owner; employers and soldiers shot Luddites, and the state hanged or transported others.1
06

The evidence

  • Luddite letters to employers and officials, trial records, government reports, and prints and ballads of the time.1

07

How is it remembered?

  • The Luddites are remembered in folk songs such as "The Cropper Lads".1
08

Why does it matter?

Today 'Luddite' means someone who fears technology. But the Luddites were skilled workers defending their wages and standards; as the historian Eric Hobsbawm put it, machine-breaking was "collective bargaining by riot".1

Open questions

  1. Were the Luddites against machines, or against the way machines were being used?
  2. Why might workers without votes or unions turn to breaking machines?
  3. Why did the government respond so harshly?
  4. What can the Luddites tell us about debates over new technology today?
09

What other events are like this?

  • 1830The Swing Riots — Farm labourers across southern England smashed the threshing machines that took their winter work, and sent threatening letters signed by a mythical 'Captain Swing'. The state answered with hangings and transportation.
  • 1844The Silesian Weavers' Uprising — Hand weavers starving on falling piece rates marched on the mansions of the merchants who paid them. Prussian troops shot eleven of them dead, and the rising became a symbol for poets, playwrights and Karl Marx.
  • 1834The Tolpuddle Martyrs — Six farm labourers formed a society to resist wage cuts and were sentenced to transportation to Australia.
