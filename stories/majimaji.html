The Maji Maji War

Forced to grow cotton for German colonial rulers, peoples across southern Tanzania united behind a sacred water medicine and rose in one of Africa's largest anti-colonial wars.

When
July 1905 – August 1907
Where
Southern German East Africa (Tanzania)

See it on the map →

01

What is the background to this event?

From the 1880s, Germany conquered the territory it called German East Africa — most of present-day Tanzania, Rwanda and Burundi. German rule was imposed through violence, and Africans were subjected to taxes, forced labour and harsh punishments, including flogging.

  • In 1902 the governor, Count Gustav Adolf von Götzen, ordered villages in the south to grow cotton for export. Each village had to produce a quota, although the crop needed more water than much of the region could provide.1
  • Villagers were forced to work on communal cotton fields under the supervision of African agents (akidas), for pay so low that it was often refused. The work took them away from their own food crops.1
  • A drought in 1905 made matters worse.1

In the Matumbi hills, a spirit medium called Kinjikitile Ngwale, who lived at Ngarambe, claimed to be possessed by a spirit called Hongo. He gave his followers a sacred medicine — water (maji in Swahili) mixed with castor oil and millet seeds — which he said would turn German bullets into water.1

02

Who were the key people?

  • Kinjikitile Ngwale

    The spirit medium whose maji medicine inspired the rising. He was arrested and hanged by the Germans in August 1905.

  • Songea Mbano

    A leader of the Ngoni people who joined the rising. He was hanged in 1906.

  • Count Gustav Adolf von Götzen

    Governor of German East Africa, who introduced the cotton scheme and directed the suppression of the rising with a scorched-earth policy.

  • The fighters

    Matumbi, Ngindo, Pogoro, Mbunga, Ngoni and many other peoples, who put aside earlier divisions to fight together.

03

What happened?

At the end of July 1905, Matumbi villagers uprooted cotton plants in protest. On 31 July they marched on Samanga, destroying the cotton crop and a trading post.1 Kinjikitile was arrested and hanged, but the movement spread rapidly across southern German East Africa, carried by messengers distributing the maji:1

  • Rebels attacked German posts, missions and plantations, and killed German officials, settlers and missionaries, as well as Africans who worked for the colonial regime.
  • On 16 August a rebel force destroyed the German post at Ifakara, opening the way to the fort at Mahenge.
  • Several thousand Maji Maji warriors attacked Mahenge, which was defended by a German officer and sixty African soldiers armed with machine guns. They were driven back with heavy losses.
  • The Ngoni people, with an army of around 5,000, joined the rising. On 21 October German troops with machine guns attacked their camp; according to the sources, the retreating Ngoni threw away their bottles of medicine, crying that "the maji is a lie".

Governor von Götzen was reinforced by troops and marines from Germany. Unable to defeat the rebels in the field, the Germans turned against the population itself. Their forces destroyed villages, crops and food stores across the region, deliberately causing famine.1 Leaders were hunted down and executed; Ngoni chiefs were hanged at Songea in 1906. Resistance continued as guerrilla war until August 1907.1

Between 75,000 and 300,000 people died during the war and the famine that followed — the great majority of them from starvation.1

04

What happened next?

  • The Great Hunger: the famine (ukame) devastated southern Tanzania. Whole districts were depopulated, and the region's economy never fully recovered.
  • Reform: shocked by the war, and by the genocide of the Herero and Nama in German South West Africa at the same time, the German government introduced reforms in colonial administration; forced communal cotton growing was abandoned.
  • The end of German rule: Britain took over the territory as Tanganyika after the First World War. It became independent in 1961 and joined with Zanzibar to form Tanzania in 1964.
  • Apology: in 2023, Germany's President Frank-Walter Steinmeier visited the Maji Maji memorial museum at Songea and asked for forgiveness for the crimes of German colonial rule.
05

What do others say?

At the time

German officials described the war as an outburst of "witchcraft" and "fanaticism". Governor von Götzen's own history of the rebellion (1909) famously made no mention of the cotton scheme he had introduced.

Historians' debates

  • Proto-nationalism? John Iliffe and other historians of the 1960s argued that the maji united different peoples in an early form of mass nationalism. Later historians have questioned this, stressing local differences and the fact that some groups fought against the rebels.
  • Religion and the maji: historians have debated the role of religion; recent work by Jörg Haustein argues that claims that Islam was a major factor are not supported by the evidence.
  • Whose story? Newer research, such as Maji Maji: Lifting the Fog of War, has paid more attention to women, ordinary fighters and the victims of famine, rather than only the leaders.

07

How is it remembered?

  • The Maji Maji Memorial Museum at Songea marks the place where Ngoni leaders were hanged.
  • Ebrahim Hussein's play Kinjeketile (1969) is a classic of Swahili literature.
  • In Berlin, a street in the "African Quarter" of Wedding has been renamed Maji-Maji-Allee, replacing a name that honoured a German colonialist.
08

Why does it matter?

The Maji Maji War was one of the largest and most widespread risings against colonial rule in Africa. Peoples who spoke different languages and had often been rivals united against a common oppressor, inspired by a shared belief in the power of the maji.

  • Tanzanian nationalists, including Julius Nyerere, later saw it as the first stirring of Tanzanian national unity.
  • The deliberate use of famine against civilians shows the extreme violence of colonial conquest.
  • It is part of a wider history of German colonial crimes that Germany has only recently begun to acknowledge.

Questions to think about

  2. How could a belief in the maji unite such different peoples?

What other events are like this?

  • 1900Yaa Asantewaa and the War of the Golden Stool — When a British governor demanded to sit on the sacred Golden Stool, the queen mother Yaa Asantewaa led the Asante in a last war against colonial rule.
  • 1915The Chilembwe Uprising — A Baptist minister educated in the United States led a short-lived rising against forced labour, racism and the recruitment of Africans to die in Britain's war.
  • 1916The 1916 Central Asian Revolt (Ürkün) — When the Tsar ordered Central Asian men to be conscripted for labour behind the front lines, the region rose in revolt. The repression, and the flight across the mountains to China, cost well over a hundred thousand lives.
