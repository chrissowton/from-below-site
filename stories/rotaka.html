Anti-colonial & freedom

The Rotaka

Farmers in the south and then students in the capital rose against a president who kept Madagascar tied to France. When police shot students, the government fell within days.

When
April 1971 – May 1972
Where
Antananarivo and Toliara, Madagascar

See it on the map →

01

What is the background to this event?

Madagascar won independence from France in 1960, but French influence remained strong under President Philibert Tsiranana.1

  • In 1969, foreigners controlled 95% of modern industry.1
  • The economy went into recession in the late 1960s.1
  • Monja Jaona, leader of the MONIMA party in the south, presented himself as the champion of ordinary people against neo-colonial rule.1
02

Who were the key people?

  • Monja Jaona

    Southern opposition leader who led the farmers' protest; jailed.

  • Students of Antananarivo

    Medical, university and school students who led the protests of 1972.

  • President Philibert Tsiranana

    Who resigned in 1972.

  • General Gabriel Ramanantsoa

    Who led the transitional government.

03

What happened?

In April 1971, armed farmers in Toliara in the south protested against the government. The protest was violently crushed, and MONIMA was banned.1

  • Many farmers were sent to the prison island of Nosy Lava, and Jaona was jailed.1
  • Medical students in Antananarivo began protesting, and the protest spread to 5,000 students.1
  • In April 1972, secondary school students joined them, demanding changes to the colonial-era curriculum and the dismissal of teachers from France.1
  • On 13 May 1972, security forces shot student protesters in Antananarivo.1

Sources

  1. Wikipedia, 'Rotaka', on the background, the farmers' protest, the student protests, the shootings and Tsiranana's fall. Link ↗
04

What happened next?

  • Fall of the government: within days, Tsiranana resigned, and General Ramanantsoa formed a transitional government.1
  • "Malagasisation": the new government reduced French influence in schools, the economy and the military.
  • Music: Madagascar's most popular band, Mahaleo, began as high school students performing at protests.1
05

What do others say?

At the time

Tsiranana called Jaona a communist; Jaona called himself a nationalist.1

Debates since

  • Revolution or coup? The protests forced Tsiranana out, but power passed to the army, not to the protesters.1
  • Violence: the southern farmers were armed and clashed with police; the government's crushing of their protest, and the shooting of students in the capital, killed many more.1
06

The evidence

  • Newspaper reports and later interviews with participants.1
07

How is it remembered?

  • The songs of Mahaleo keep the memory of 1972 alive.1
08

Why does it matter?

The rotaka ended Madagascar's first republic and its close dependence on France.

  • It was led by farmers and by students, many of them still at school.

Questions to think about

  1. Why did independence not end French influence?
  2. Why did students demand changes to what they were taught?
  3. How did the crushing of the farmers' protest affect public opinion?
  4. Why did the army take power after the protests?
09

What other events are like this?

  • 1947The Malagasy Uprising of 1947 — Armed mostly with spears, Malagasy nationalists rose against French colonial rule. The repression killed tens of thousands — and France kept silent about it for more than half a century.
  • 1990Benin's National Conference — Strikes by unpaid teachers, students and civil servants forced Benin's military ruler to call a national conference. The delegates declared themselves sovereign, took power peacefully — and inspired democratic movements across Africa.
  • 1991Mali's March Revolution — Students and democracy campaigners marched against 23 years of military rule. Soldiers fired on them — and then other soldiers refused, overthrew the dictator, and handed power to an elected government.
