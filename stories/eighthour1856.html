Eight Hours: The Stonemasons of Melbourne

Building workers in Sydney and Melbourne downed tools and marched on Parliament to demand an eight-hour day — and won it, decades before most workers anywhere in the world.

When
1855 – 1856
Where
Melbourne, Victoria (Australia)
Facts checked

01

What is the background to this event?

The demand for an eight-hour working day went back to the British reformer Robert Owen, who by 1817 had coined the slogan "Eight hours' labour, Eight hours' recreation, Eight hours' rest".1

  • In the 1850s, the gold rushes brought rapid growth and a building boom to the Australian colonies.1
  • The Melbourne movement was led by veteran Chartists, who had campaigned for democracy in Britain.1

03

What happened?

On 18 August 1855, the Stonemasons' Society in Sydney gave employers six months' notice that masons would then work only eight hours a day.1

  • Masons building two Sydney churches did not wait: they struck at once and won the eight-hour day.1
  • When the ultimatum ran out in February 1856, a two-week strike won the eight-hour day for Sydney masons, though with lower wages.1
  • In Melbourne, on 21 April 1856, stonemasons building the University downed tools and marched to Parliament House with other building workers.1
  • The government agreed that workers on public works would have an eight-hour day with no loss of pay.1
  • On 12 May 1856, about 700 people from 19 trades marched in celebration.1
04

What happened next?

  • Spreading: by 1858 the eight-hour day was firmly established in Victoria's building industry, and by 1860 it was widely worked in the colony.1
  • A holiday: the Sydney masons' victory dinner of 1 October 1855 is still marked by the Labour Day holiday in New South Wales.1
  • Worldwide: the First International adopted the demand for an eight-hour day in 1866, and it became a central aim of labour movements around the world.1
05

What do others say?

At the time

Employers opposed the shorter day; in Sydney, masons won it only by accepting lower wages, while in Melbourne public-works employees kept their pay.1

Debates since

  • Who was first? Melbourne's march is the best remembered, but Sydney masons on two church sites had won the eight-hour day months earlier, in 1855.1

Britain, by 1817

Owen's slogan travelled with emigrant workers and Chartists to Australia, where the stonemasons put it into practice. Its three equal parts became the emblem of the movement, and appear on the Melbourne monument.

07

How is it remembered?

  • The Eight Hour Day monument stands in Melbourne, and Labour Day remains a public holiday in Australian states.1
08

Why does it matter?

The eight-hour day — eight hours for work, eight for rest, eight for what we will — is one of the labour movement's best-known victories. Australian building workers were among the first to win it.1

Open questions

  1. Why might building workers have been well placed to win shorter hours?
  2. Why did the Melbourne workers march to Parliament rather than only strike?
  3. What did the eight-hour day change in workers' lives?
  4. Why did it take so much longer for most workers to win it?

What other events are like this?

  • 1886The Haymarket Affair — At a rally for the eight-hour day, someone threw a bomb at the police. Eight anarchists were convicted without evidence that any of them threw it, and four were hanged. Their deaths gave the world May Day.
  • 1889The Great Dock Strike: 'The Dockers' Tanner' — Casual dock labourers, hired by the hour at the dock gates, shut down the Port of London for four weeks — and won. Their victory opened the way for unions of unskilled workers across Britain.
  • 1834The Tolpuddle Martyrs — Six farm labourers formed a society to resist wage cuts and were sentenced to transportation to Australia.
