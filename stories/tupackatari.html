Túpac Katari, Bartolina Sisa and the Siege of La Paz

An Aymara coca trader and his wife led tens of thousands in a siege of the Spanish city of La Paz. Thousands died of starvation; the leaders were executed. But the Aymara say: 'I will return, and I will be millions.'

When
March – October 1781
Where
El Alto and La Paz, Upper Peru (Bolivia)

See it on the map →

01

What is the background to this event?

In the eighteenth century, Indigenous people in the Andes suffered under Spanish colonial rule: forced labour in the mines (the mita), heavy tribute, and the forced sale of goods by corrupt officials (the reparto).

  • In 1780, Túpac Amaru II launched a great rebellion near Cusco.1
  • Further south, among the Aymara people, Tomás Katari had led protests in Potosí.1
  • Julián Apasa, an Aymara coca and cloth trader from Ayo Ayo, took the name Túpac Katari in honour of these leaders.1
02

Who were the key people?

  • Túpac Katari (Julián Apasa)

    An Aymara trader who led the rebellion and was executed in November 1781.

  • Bartolina Sisa

    His wife, a commander of the siege, executed in 1782.

  • Gregoria Apaza

    His sister, a leader in the rebellion, also executed in 1782.

  • The people of La Paz

    Spanish, mestizo and Indigenous residents trapped by the siege.

03

What happened?

In 1781, Túpac Katari raised an army of some 40,000 and on 13 March laid siege to the Spanish city of La Paz.1

  • Katari and Bartolina Sisa set up their court in El Alto, on the plateau above the city, and maintained the siege for 109 days, from March to June.1
  • Bartolina Sisa was a commander of the siege, and Gregoria Apaza also led forces.1
  • The siege was lifted in June by troops from Buenos Aires. The rebels returned in August, joined by Andrés Túpac Amaru, a nephew of Túpac Amaru II.1

About 20,000 people died during the siege, many from starvation, before it finally ended in October.1 The rebels also killed Spaniards and others in the countryside they saw as collaborators.

The Spanish offered amnesty to rebels who surrendered, and many accepted.1 Túpac Katari was betrayed and captured on 9 November 1781.1 On 13 November, he was executed by being pulled apart by horses.1

04

What happened next?

  • Executions: Bartolina Sisa and Gregoria Apaza were captured and executed in September 1782.
  • Repression: Spain banned Indigenous dress, titles and symbols linked to the rebellions.
  • A symbol: in the twentieth century, Katarista movements of Aymara peasants revived his memory, and he became a symbol of Indigenous resistance in Bolivia.
  • Recognition: 5 September, the day of Bartolina Sisa's execution, is marked as the International Day of Indigenous Women.
05

What do others say?

At the time

The Spanish saw the rebels as savages bent on killing all Spaniards. The rebels sought to end colonial abuses and, for some, Spanish rule itself.

Historians' debates

  • Aims: Sinclair Thomson argues that Aymara communities sought self-rule — "we alone will rule" — not just reform.
  • Violence: the siege caused mass starvation in La Paz, and rebels killed Spaniards, mestizos and Indigenous people seen as collaborators; Spanish repression killed many thousands more.
  • The famous words: Katari's last words, "I will return, and I will be millions", are part of popular memory; historians note they are not recorded in contemporary sources.
06

The evidence

  • Spanish diaries of the siege and colonial trial records.
  • Aymara oral tradition.
07

How is it remembered?

  • Bolivia's first communications satellite, launched in 2013, was named Túpac Katari.
  • Monuments to Túpac Katari and Bartolina Sisa stand in El Alto and elsewhere.
08

Why does it matter?

The rebellion of Túpac Katari was one of the largest Indigenous uprisings in the colonial Americas. Its memory remains central to Aymara and Bolivian identity.

  • It shows the leading role of Indigenous women, such as Bartolina Sisa and Gregoria Apaza.
  • It raises hard questions about the violence of a siege that starved a city.
