Guinea's General Strike of 2007

When Guinea's ailing president freed two men accused of corruption, the trade unions called a general strike. Weeks of shutdown and protest, met with deadly force and martial law, forced him to share power.

When
10 January – 27 February 2007
Where
Conakry and towns across Guinea

01

What is the background to this event?

Lansana Conté had ruled Guinea since a military coup in 1984. By 2006 he was seriously ill, prices were rising fast, and corruption was widespread.1

  • The unions had already held general strikes in 2006 over wages and the cost of rice and fuel.1
  • In December 2006, Conté personally went to a prison to free Mamadou Sylla, a businessman accused of embezzling public funds, and a former minister.1

03

What happened?

On 10 January 2007, the two main trade union federations, the USTG and the CNTG, called a general strike. They were soon joined by other unions and opposition parties.1

  • Shops, markets, transport and the mining industry shut down. Demands grew from the release of Sylla to Conté's resignation.1
  • Security forces fired on demonstrators. At least 17 people were killed in the first weeks of the strike, and more in the protests that followed.1
  • On 23 January, union leaders Rabiatou Sérah Diallo, Ibrahima Fofana and Yamadou Touré were arrested, then released.1
  • On 27 January, the unions agreed to suspend the strike in return for the appointment of a new prime minister.1
  • When Conté named his ally Eugène Camara, the strike resumed on 12 February. Conté declared martial law.1
  • On 26 February, Conté named Lansana Kouyaté, from a list proposed by the unions, as prime minister. The strike ended the next day.1

Human Rights Watch put the total number of people killed by the security forces at around 137, with many more wounded. Some protesters also attacked government buildings and property.2

04

What happened next?

  • Kouyaté was dismissed by Conté in 2008.
  • Coup: Conté died in December 2008, and the army seized power under Moussa Dadis Camara.
  • 28 September 2009: soldiers killed more than 150 people at an opposition rally in Conakry's stadium. Camara was convicted for the massacre in 2024.
05

What do others say?

At the time

The government blamed the unions for disorder; the unions and human rights groups blamed the security forces for the deaths.2

Debates since

  • Death toll: estimates vary; Human Rights Watch's figure of about 137 is widely cited.2
  • Success or failure? The strike won a new prime minister but not a change of regime, and Conté soon regained control.

07

How is it remembered?

  • Rabiatou Sérah Diallo became one of Guinea's best-known public figures, and later led the national transition council.
08

Why does it matter?

The 2007 strike showed the power of trade unions to challenge a military-backed ruler, and the importance of women leaders such as Rabiatou Sérah Diallo.

  • It forced a sharing of power, if only for a time.
  • It revealed the willingness of the security forces to kill unarmed protesters.

Questions to think about

  1. Why did the release of Mamadou Sylla spark a general strike?
  2. Why were trade unions able to lead the opposition?
  3. Did the strike achieve its aims?
  4. Why did the security forces use such violence?

What other events are like this?

  • 2005Niger's Campaign Against the High Cost of Living — When the government of one of the world's poorest countries put VAT on flour, milk and sugar, a coalition of unions and associations brought tens of thousands into the streets — and forced a climbdown.
  • 1991Mali's March Revolution — Students and democracy campaigners marched against 23 years of military rule. Soldiers fired on them — and then other soldiers refused, overthrew the dictator, and handed power to an elected government.
  • 1964Sudan's October Revolution — When police killed a student at a university debate about the war in the south, Sudanese professionals, workers and students launched a general strike that brought down a military dictatorship in days.
