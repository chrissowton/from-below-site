The Trepča Miners' Hunger Strike

More than a thousand Albanian miners stayed underground and refused food to defend Kosovo's autonomy. Their strike won support across Yugoslavia, and forced resignations — but Serbia abolished the autonomy weeks later.

When
20 – 28 February 1989
Where
Stari Trg mine, Trepča, Kosovo
Facts checked
2026-11-15

01

What is the background to this event?

Under Yugoslavia's 1974 constitution, Kosovo, a province of Serbia with an Albanian majority, had wide autonomy.1

  • After Tito's death in 1980, Serbian politicians increasingly questioned this autonomy, especially after Albanian protests in 1981.1
  • Slobodan Milošević rose to power in Serbia, replacing provincial leaders with his loyalists in the "anti-bureaucratic revolution".1
  • In November 1988, 2,000 Albanian miners marched from the mines to Pristina, joined by about 6,000 others.1

In their own words

03

What happened?

On 20 February 1989, as the Serbian assembly prepared to reduce Kosovo's autonomy, about 1,350 miners at Trepča began a hunger strike underground.1

  • They demanded the preservation of autonomy and the resignation of the pro-Milošević leaders of Kosovo.1
  • The strike quickly won support in Slovenia and Croatia. In Belgrade, crowds protested against the Slovenian, Croatian and Albanian leaderships.1
  • After eight days, and after about 180 miners had been taken to hospital, the three leaders resigned. The strike ended.1

04

What happened next?

  • Autonomy abolished: in March 1989, the resignations were reversed and Kosovo's autonomy was abolished. Protests followed; dozens of people, mostly Albanians, were killed in clashes with police.2
  • Parallel society: in the 1990s, Albanians dismissed from public jobs built their own schools, clinics and institutions, led by Ibrahim Rugova's non-violent movement.2
  • War: in 1998–99, war broke out between Yugoslav forces and the Kosovo Liberation Army, followed by NATO bombing. Kosovo declared independence in 2008.
05

What do others say?

At the time

Serbian leaders presented the strike as Albanian separatism; supporters saw a defence of constitutional rights.1

Debates since

  • Serbs in Kosovo: many Kosovo Serbs felt under pressure in the 1980s; Serbian nationalists used their fears to justify ending autonomy.2
  • Non-violence: the strike itself was entirely peaceful. Later protests in 1989 were met with deadly force.2

07

How is it remembered?

  • The strike is commemorated in Kosovo each February.
08

Why does it matter?

The miners' strike was a dramatic act of non-violent protest at the moment Yugoslavia began to break apart.

  • It drew sympathy from other republics, revealing the divisions that would tear Yugoslavia apart.

Questions to think about

  3. Why did the victory prove so short-lived?
