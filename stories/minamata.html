Minamata: The Struggle for Justice

A chemical company poisoned a fishing community with mercury for decades. Its victims fought for recognition and justice — and changed environmental law in Japan and the world.

When
1956 – 1973
Where
Minamata, Kumamoto Prefecture, Japan
Facts checked
2026-11-15

01

What is the background to this event?

Minamata is a small city on the Shiranui Sea in south-western Japan. From 1908 it was dominated by a chemical factory owned by the Chisso Corporation:1

  • Chisso provided a quarter of the city's jobs, and at one point more than half of its tax revenue came from the company and its employees. Minamata was called Chisso's "castle town".
  • From 1932, the factory produced acetaldehyde, using mercury sulfate as a catalyst. After a change to the process in 1951, it released large quantities of highly toxic methylmercury into Minamata Bay, where it accumulated in fish and shellfish.
  • The fishing families of the bay, who ate fish every day and were among the poorest people in the region, were the first to suffer.

From around 1950, local people noticed cats convulsing and dying — they called it the "cat dancing disease". On 21 April 1956, a five-year-old girl was brought to the Chisso factory hospital with severe brain damage, and soon her sister and many neighbours were found to have the same symptoms.1 The Chisso hospital director, Hajime Hosokawa, reported a "strange disease" to the public health authorities on 1 May 1956 — the official date of its discovery.

In their own words

03

What happened?

Researchers from Kumamoto University concluded in 1959 that the disease was caused by organic mercury from the Chisso factory. In July 1959, the factory hospital's director, Hajime Hosokawa, fed factory wastewater to cats; on the 78th day, "cat 400" developed the disease. Chisso did not reveal the results and ordered him to stop his research.1

The fishermen and the patients began to fight back:1

  • In August and October–November 1959, fishing cooperatives forced their way into the factory to demand compensation; on 2 November, members rioted, causing many injuries.
  • In November 1959, patients staged a sit-in at the factory gates. In December they accepted a "sympathy money" agreement under which Chisso paid small annual sums but admitted no responsibility, and patients gave up any further claims.
  • Chisso installed a water-purification system with great ceremony; it was later shown to have been ineffective against mercury. The company continued to discharge methylmercury until 1968.

In September 1968, after a second outbreak in Niigata, the Japanese government finally acknowledged that Chisso's factory was the cause. On 14 June 1969, a group of 41 certified patients (17 already dead) in 28 families sued Chisso in the Kumamoto District Court. Their leader, Eizō Watanabe, declared:

"Today, and from this day forth, we are fighting against the power of the state."Eizō Watanabe, 14 June 19691

Supporters across Japan formed citizens' groups to raise funds and awareness. Other patients, led by Teruo Kawamoto, sought direct negotiations with Chisso, staging sit-ins at the factory and at the company's Tokyo headquarters.3 On 20 March 1973, the court ruled that Chisso had been negligent and ordered it to pay full compensation — a complete victory for the patients.1

04

What happened next?

  • New laws: Minamata and the other "Four Big Pollution Diseases" led Japan to pass strict anti-pollution laws in 1970 and to create the Environment Agency in 1971.
  • Long struggle for recognition: by 2001, 2,265 people had been officially certified, and more than 10,000 had received some compensation. Many thousands more were refused certification and fought for decades.
  • The state held responsible: in 2004 Japan's Supreme Court ruled that the national and prefectural governments were also liable for failing to stop the pollution. A further settlement for uncertified victims was reached in 2010.
  • A global treaty: in 2013 the Minamata Convention on Mercury, an international treaty to control mercury pollution, was signed in Kumamoto and Minamata.
05

What do others say?

At the time

Chisso denied responsibility for years, and its supporters — including many workers and residents who depended on the company — blamed the victims or suggested other causes, such as explosives dumped after the war. Patients and their families faced discrimination in their own community. The national government, which saw the chemical industry as vital to economic growth, delayed acting.

Historians' debates

  • Who was responsible? Historians such as Timothy George show how Chisso, local and national government and scientists who defended the company all contributed to the delay.
  • Who counts as a victim? The strict criteria used to certify patients have been widely criticised for excluding thousands of people with milder or atypical symptoms.
  • Democracy: George argues that the Minamata struggle was a test of postwar Japanese democracy, and that it helped to make it more participatory.

The evidence

  • Medical and scientific research, including the work of the Kumamoto University Research Group and of Masazumi Harada.
  • Court records of the lawsuits, and Chisso's internal records disclosed during them.
  • The testimony of patients, recorded by Michiko Ishimure and in Noriaki Tsuchimoto's documentary films.
  • The photographs of W. Eugene Smith and Aileen Mioko Smith.

07

How is it remembered?

  • The Minamata Disease Municipal Museum and a memorial to the victims stand on land reclaimed from the polluted bay.
  • Noriaki Tsuchimoto's documentaries, beginning with Minamata: The Victims and Their World (1971), and the film Minamata (2020) have told the story.
  • W. Eugene Smith's most famous photograph, Tomoko and Mother in the Bath (1972), has been withdrawn from circulation at the request of Tomoko's family, raising questions about how suffering should be shown.
08

Why does it matter?

Minamata is one of the worst cases of industrial pollution in history, and one of the most important struggles for environmental justice. Poor fishing families, ignored and stigmatised for years, forced a powerful company and the state to accept responsibility.

  • It showed how economic growth could be achieved at the cost of the health and lives of the poorest communities.
  • The victims' struggle helped to build Japan's citizens' movements and environmental law.
  • Its name is now attached to a global treaty on mercury — a lasting reminder of its lessons.
