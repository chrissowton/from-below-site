The Athens Polytechnic Uprising

Students occupied their university and broadcast a call for 'Bread, Education, Freedom' against Greece's military dictatorship. Before dawn on the third day, a tank crashed through the gates.

When
14 – 17 November 1973
Where
National Technical University of Athens, Greece

See it on the map →

01

What is the background to this event?

In April 1967, a group of army colonels seized power in Greece. The "Regime of the Colonels" banned political parties, censored the press, and imprisoned, tortured and exiled thousands of opponents.

  • Students became the centre of resistance. In February 1973, law students barricaded themselves inside the Law School in Athens, protesting against forced conscription of student activists.1
  • The police used brutal methods, including torture, against the growing student movement.1
  • The regime's attempt at controlled "liberalisation" in 1973 opened a little space for protest.
02

Who were the key people?

  • The students of the Polytechnic

    Who called themselves the 'Free Besieged', after a poem about the siege of Missolonghi.

  • Workers and young people of Athens

    Who joined the occupation and the street protests.

  • The radio station team

    Students who broadcast from the Polytechnic, calling on the people to rise.

  • Georgios Papadopoulos and Dimitrios Ioannidis

    Leaders of the junta; Ioannidis overthrew Papadopoulos days after the uprising.

03

What happened?

On 14 November 1973, students at the Athens Polytechnic went on strike and began protesting against the junta, while the authorities at first stood by.1

  • An assembly formed spontaneously and decided to occupy the Polytechnic. The rallying cry was "Bread — Education — Freedom!"1
  • Thousands of Athenians poured in to support the students. Workers and farmers joined, and the occupation became an open revolt against the junta.1
  • The students built a radio transmitter and broadcast appeals to the people of Athens.
  • The occupation was driven largely by independent and anarchist students, bypassing the main left-wing student organisations; the Communist Youth initially called some of the radicals provocateurs.1

In the early hours of 17 November, the army moved in. A tank crashed through the main gate of the Polytechnic, and soldiers and police cleared the building.1 According to research published in 2004 by the National Hellenic Research Foundation, at least 24 people were killed in and around the uprising, by the army and police.1

04

What happened next?

  • A harder junta: days later, Brigadier Dimitrios Ioannidis overthrew Papadopoulos and imposed an even harsher regime.
  • The fall of the junta: in July 1974, the junta's backing for a coup in Cyprus led to a Turkish invasion of the island. The regime collapsed, and democracy was restored in Greece.1
  • A long shadow: the uprising marked a break between young Greeks and the traditional left, and saw the revival of Greek anarchism.1 The repression also inspired the formation of the terrorist group 17 November, which killed more than 20 people between 1975 and 2000.1
05

What do others say?

At the time

The junta blamed communist and anarchist agitators. The students saw themselves as defending freedom and democracy.

Historians' debates

  • The death toll: for years there were claims of hundreds of deaths; the 2004 study documented at least 24 deaths, though some argue the true figure was higher.1
  • Who led it? Historians such as Kostis Kornetis emphasise the role of independent and anarchist students rather than the organised left.1
  • Did it bring down the junta? Some historians argue that the uprising was a turning point; others that the Cyprus crisis was decisive.
06

The evidence

07

How is it remembered?

  • 17 November is commemorated each year in Greece, with schools closed and a march to the US embassy (because of US support for the junta).
  • The twisted gate of the Polytechnic is preserved on the campus.
08

Why does it matter?

The Polytechnic uprising is the defining symbol of resistance to the Greek dictatorship. Students' courage helped undermine the junta and inspired the restoration of democracy.

  • It shows the role of universities as spaces of freedom.
  • It also shows how the memory of repression can be invoked to justify violence, as the terrorist group 17 November did.

Questions to think about

09

What other events are like this?

  • 1973The Thai Student Uprising of 14 October 1973 — When thirteen activists were arrested for handing out leaflets calling for a constitution, half a million people filled the streets of Bangkok and brought down a military dictatorship.
  • 1974The Carnation Revolution — Young officers overthrew Europe's oldest dictatorship with barely a shot fired. Then the people took to the streets with carnations — and kept going, occupying land, factories and homes.
  • 1980The Gwangju Uprising — Students and citizens in Gwangju rose against a military coup. Paratroopers killed hundreds, but the uprising became the foundation of South Korean democracy.
