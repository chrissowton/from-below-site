The Women to Drive Movement

For almost three decades, Saudi women defied the world's only ban on women driving — getting behind the wheel, filming themselves and petitioning the king. The ban was lifted in 2018, but many of the campaigners were in prison.

When
1990 – 2018
Where
Riyadh, Jeddah and across Saudi Arabia

01

What is the background to this event?

Saudi Arabia banned women from driving from 1957. Until 2018 it was the only country in the world where women could not legally drive.1

  • Under the male guardianship system, women needed a male relative's permission for many activities, including travel and some kinds of work.1
  • Families employed hundreds of thousands of foreign chauffeurs to drive women.1

In their own words

03

What happened?

On 6 November 1990, 47 women drove their cars through Riyadh in protest. They were arrested, and religious officials called for harsh punishment, but they were released.1

  • In 2007, Wajeha al-Huwaider and Fawzia al-Uyyouni submitted a petition with 1,100 signatures to King Abdullah.1
  • On International Women's Day 2008, al-Huwaider posted a video of herself driving, which attracted international attention.1
  • In 2011, inspired by the Arab Spring, Manal al-Sharif and others launched the Women2Drive campaign. About 70 women were documented driving in June 2011.1
  • A woman in Jeddah, Shaima Jastania, was sentenced to ten lashes for driving; the sentence was overturned.1
  • Another day of driving was called for 26 October 2013; the government warned that the law would be enforced.1
  • On 26 September 2017, King Salman ordered that women be allowed to drive from June 2018.1
04

What happened next?

  • Arrests: in May 2018, weeks before the ban ended, leading campaigners were detained, including Loujain al-Hathloul, Eman al-Nafjan and Aziza al-Yousef.1
  • The ban ends: women were allowed to drive from 24 June 2018, while many campaigners remained in detention.1
  • Al-Hathloul was released in February 2021 after nearly three years, but was banned from travel. She and her family said she had been tortured, which the authorities denied.3
  • Guardianship: some guardianship rules were eased in 2019.
05

What do others say?

At the time

Supporters of the ban said it protected women and society; campaigners said it denied women independence and dignity.1

Debates since

  • Reform from above or below? The government presented the change as part of Crown Prince Mohammed bin Salman's modernisation; campaigners and human rights groups credit decades of activism.3
  • Peaceful protest: the campaign was entirely non-violent.

How is it remembered?

  • The women of 1990 are remembered by campaigners as pioneers.
08

Why does it matter?

The campaign shows how small, repeated acts of defiance — and the internet — could challenge a law in one of the world's most restrictive states.

  • It won its main demand.
  • The arrests show that the government wanted the reform to appear as a gift from above, not a victory from below.

What other events are like this?

  • 1952Fatima Ahmed Ibrahim and the Sudanese Women's Union — A schoolgirl who led a strike for science lessons founded one of Africa's largest women's movements. In 1965 she became the first woman elected to Sudan's parliament.
