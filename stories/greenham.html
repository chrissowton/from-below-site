Peace & anti-war

The Greenham Common Women's Peace Camp

Women camped outside an air base for nineteen years to protest against nuclear weapons, inventing new forms of protest along the way.

When
5 September 1981 – September 2000
Where
RAF Greenham Common, Berkshire, England
Facts checked

01

What is the background to this event?

In December 1979 NATO decided to station new American nuclear missiles in Western Europe, including cruise missiles at RAF Greenham Common in Berkshire and RAF Molesworth. The decision came at a time of rising tension in the Cold War, and many people in Europe feared that it made nuclear war more likely and turned their countries into targets.

The campaign against nuclear weapons revived across Europe:

  • The Campaign for Nuclear Disarmament (CND), which had first flourished in the late 1950s, grew rapidly again.
  • Hundreds of thousands of people marched in London, Bonn, Amsterdam and other cities.
  • Many women argued that they had a particular stake in peace and a particular way of protesting, connected to the feminist movement of the 1970s.

  • Helen Thomas

  • The Greenham women

    Tens of thousands of women who lived at the camp or visited it over nineteen years.

In their own words

03

What happened?

On 5 September 1981, a group called Women for Life on Earth arrived at Greenham Common after marching from Cardiff. Some chained themselves to the base fence. When their march failed to attract the attention they had hoped for, some women decided to stay — and the peace camp was born.2 In early 1982 the camp became women-only.

Over the following years the women developed imaginative forms of protest:2

  • On 12 December 1982, around 30,000 women joined hands to surround the nine-mile perimeter of the base in "Embrace the Base", decorating the fence with photographs, toys and other personal objects.
  • On New Year's Day 1983, a small group climbed the fence and danced on the missile silos that were under construction.
  • On 1 April 1983, around 70,000 protesters formed a fourteen-mile human chain linking Greenham with the nuclear weapons establishments at Aldermaston and Burghfield.
  • In December 1983, 50,000 women again encircled the base, and many cut down sections of the fence.

The women were repeatedly evicted by the local council and the authorities — in September 1982 and April 1984, among other occasions — but each time they returned.2 Despite the protests, the first cruise missiles arrived at Greenham in November 1983. Camps grew up at each of the base's gates, each with its own character.2

04

What happened next?

  • Disarmament: in December 1987 the United States and the Soviet Union signed the Intermediate-Range Nuclear Forces (INF) Treaty, which eliminated ground-launched cruise missiles. The last missiles left Greenham in 1991.
  • The camp continued: women stayed to protest against the Trident nuclear programme and other issues, and the camp did not close until September 2000.
  • The common restored: the base was closed, and in 2000 Greenham Common was returned to public use as common land. A commemorative peace garden was created at the site of the original camp.
05

Why does it matter?

Greenham was one of the longest-lasting and most influential protest camps in history. It brought the issue of nuclear weapons into the homes and conversations of millions of people, and its creative, non-violent methods inspired peace and environmental movements around the world.

  • Some historians, such as Martin Shaw, argue that the protests contributed significantly to the INF Treaty.
  • It became a focus for the women's movement and a space where many women discovered their political voice.
  • Its methods — living at the site, decorating fences, symbolic trespass — have been taken up by later protest camps.

What do others say?How it was seen at the time, and where historians disagree

At the time

The government argued that nuclear deterrence kept the peace, and that the missiles were necessary because the Soviet Union had deployed its own intermediate-range missiles. Much of the tabloid press attacked the Greenham women as dirty, unpatriotic or naïve, and local residents' groups campaigned for their removal.

Historians' debates

  • Did Greenham work? Some historians credit the peace movement with helping to create the political climate for the INF Treaty; others argue that the decisive factors were the policies of Mikhail Gorbachev and Ronald Reagan.
  • Women-only: the decision to exclude men was controversial at the time, both within the peace movement and among feminists.
  • Divisions: historians have explored tensions between different camps and gates over politics, class and approach.
How do we know?The evidence, and a source from the time
  • Photographs, banners, letters and diaries collected by the women themselves, many now held in the Women's Library at the London School of Economics.
  • Extensive press coverage — much of it hostile.
  • Court records of the many trials of Greenham women.
  • Oral history projects, such as Greenham Women Everywhere.

Reading it

'Embrace the Base': thousands of women link hands around the nine-mile fence. Encircling a nuclear base with bodies rather than weapons was the whole argument in one image. Much of the press preferred to describe the women as dirty or unwomanly than to engage with what they were saying.

How is it remembered?Memorials, anniversaries and arguments over memory
  • A commemorative peace garden at the site of Yellow Gate was opened in 2002.
  • The Helen Thomas Memorial Peace Garden remembers the activist killed in 1989.
  • Reunions and anniversary events have brought former campers together.
