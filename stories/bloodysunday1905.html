Workers & unions

Bloody Sunday, St Petersburg

Tens of thousands of workers and their families, led by a priest and carrying icons, marched to ask the tsar for help. Soldiers opened fire, and the revolution of 1905 began.

When
22 January 1905
Where
Narva Gate and the Winter Palace, St Petersburg, Russia

See it on the map →

01

What is the background to this event?

After the emancipation of the serfs in 1861, a new urban working class grew up in Russia's cities, living and working in harsh conditions.1

  • Father Georgy Gapon, an Orthodox priest, led a workers' assembly in St Petersburg.
  • In January 1905, strikes spread through the city's factories.
  • Gapon and the workers drew up a petition to the tsar asking for better conditions and political rights.

03

What happened?

On Sunday 22 January 1905 (9 January in the old calendar), columns of workers marched towards the Winter Palace to present their petition to the tsar.1

  • Soldiers of the Imperial Guard fired on them at bridges and other entry points to the city centre.1
  • Gapon's column was fired on near the Narva Gate; around forty people were killed or wounded there.1
  • Officials recorded 96 dead and 333 injured; anti-government sources claimed more than 4,000 dead; moderate estimates are around 1,000 killed or wounded.1

04

What happened next?

  • Revolution: outrage spread, and mass strikes broke out across the empire's industrial centres. Bloody Sunday is seen as the start of the 1905 Revolution.1
  • Reform: by October 1905, the tsar was forced to promise a parliament (the Duma) and civil rights.2
  • 1917: historians see Bloody Sunday as one of the key events leading to the Russian Revolution of 1917.1
05

What do others say?

At the time

The government blamed agitators; workers across Russia saw a massacre of peaceful petitioners.1

Debates since

  • Casualties: the numbers remain uncertain; Trotsky claimed hundreds were killed and many secretly buried.1
  • Gapon: his workers' assembly had been allowed by the police, and he was later accused of working with them; he was killed by revolutionaries in 1906.2
  • Violence: the marchers were unarmed; the killing was done by the state.1
06

The evidence

  • The workers' petition and police reports.2
  • Eyewitness accounts and paintings, including Vladimir Makovsky's.
07

How is it remembered?

  • Bloody Sunday was commemorated in the Soviet Union and is remembered as a turning point in Russian history.
08

Why does it matter?

Bloody Sunday destroyed many workers' belief that the tsar would protect them.

  • It shows how state violence against peaceful protest can spark revolution.

09

What other events are like this?

  • 1819The Peterloo Massacre — Tens of thousands of working people gathered peacefully to demand the vote. Within ten minutes, cavalry had cleared the field with sabres.
  • 1921The Kronstadt Rebellion — The sailors who had helped bring the Bolsheviks to power demanded free elections and free speech. The Red Army crossed the ice to crush them.
  • 1962The Novocherkassk Strike and Massacre — When the price of meat and butter went up on the same day that their pay was cut, Soviet locomotive workers went on strike. Soldiers opened fire, and the state kept the massacre secret for thirty years.
