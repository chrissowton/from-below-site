Disability rights

The Disability Caravan to La Paz

Disabled Bolivians travelled 379 km to La Paz on crutches and in wheelchairs, demanding a monthly payment of 500 bolivianos. For months they camped outside the seat of government, facing police barriers.

01

What is the background to this event?

In 2016, people with severe disabilities in Bolivia received a payment of 1,000 bolivianos a year. People with mild and moderate disabilities received nothing.1

  • Laws required public and private employers to give 4 per cent of jobs to disabled people, but these were not enforced.1
  • Disabled people had marched to La Paz before, in 2004 and later years.1

03

What happened?

From January 2016, disabled people held vigils in Cochabamba, demanding a monthly payment of 500 bolivianos (about US$70). Some hung themselves in their wheelchairs from a bridge to draw attention.1

  • A caravan then set out for La Paz. Disabled people walked or travelled 379 km on crutches and in wheelchairs over 35 days.1
  • On arrival, they were not allowed into the Plaza Murillo, the square outside the seat of government. They camped nearby for about three months.1
  • Protesters clashed with police when they tried to break through the metal barriers around the square, and later hung themselves in wheelchairs from a bridge in La Paz.1
  • The government refused the payment, saying it could destabilise the economy, and signed a separate agreement focused on jobs with another group of disabled people.1
  • Human rights groups and the press criticised the treatment of the protesters by police.2
04

What happened next?

  • A partial win: in 2017, President Morales signed Law 977, granting a monthly payment of 250 bolivianos — half of what the protesters demanded.1
  • The UN: activists presented a shadow report to the UN, which made recommendations to Bolivia, including on the use of force.13
05

Why does it matter?

The caravan made disabled people visible in Bolivian politics and won a new payment.

  • It showed that disabled people's protests can be powerful even against a popular government.
  • It raised questions about how left-wing governments treat social movements.
  • Activists presented a shadow report to the UN, which made recommendations to Bolivia, including on the use of force.13

What do others say?How it was seen at the time, and where historians disagree

At the time

The government said the payment was unaffordable and accused some protesters of being aggressive; protesters said the government was ignoring the poorest.1

Debates since

  • Policing: critics say the police response to disabled protesters was disproportionate; the government defended keeping the square clear.12
  • Divided movement: the government's agreement with a different group of disabled people split the movement.1

Reading it

Disabled protesters in a La Paz street during their long campaign. Many had travelled a long way to reach the seat of government, and simply being there, in public, was part of the protest: a demand to be seen by a state that had overlooked them.

How is it remembered?Memorials, anniversaries and arguments over memory
