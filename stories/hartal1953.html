The Ceylon Hartal of 1953

When the government nearly tripled the price of rice, the left called a day of strikes and civil disobedience. The country stopped, the cabinet met on a warship — and the prime minister resigned.

When
12 August 1953
Where
Colombo and western and southern Ceylon (Sri Lanka)
Facts checked
2026-11-16

See it on the map →

01

What is the background to this event?

Ceylon became independent from Britain in 1948, governed by the United National Party (UNP).1

  • Since the Second World War, people had relied on a subsidised rice ration, the staple of their diet.1
  • In the 1952 election, the UNP promised to keep the price of rice at 25 cents a measure.1
  • Soon after, the government faced an economic crisis, as the Korean War raised world rice prices.1
  • In July 1953, it cut the rice subsidy, raising the price from 25 to 70 cents. It also withdrew free school meals and raised postal and railway fares.1
02

Who were the key people?

  • N. M. Perera

    Leader of the Trotskyist Lanka Sama Samaja Party (LSSP), which led the hartal.

  • Workers and peasants

    Who stopped work and blocked roads and railways.

  • Dudley Senanayake

    The prime minister, who resigned after the hartal.

03

What happened?

The cuts provoked a strong public outcry, and protests were met with baton charges and tear gas.1 The LSSP and other left parties called for a hartal — a day of strikes and civil disobedience — on 12 August 1953.1

  • It was the first mass political action in Ceylon after independence, and was primarily a protest of the working class, without divisions of caste, ethnicity or religion.1
  • Across the Western, Southern and Sabaragamuwa provinces, workers stayed away from work, shops closed and transport stopped.1
  • Protesters also sabotaged and destroyed public infrastructure — cutting telephone lines, blocking roads and damaging railway lines — to halt the government.1

The government declared a state of emergency, and the police opened fire in several places. At least 10 people were killed.1 Fearing a revolution, the cabinet met aboard a British warship in Colombo harbour.

04

What happened next?

  • Resignation: Prime Minister Dudley Senanayake resigned in October 1953.1
  • Political change: the hartal weakened the UNP and helped prepare the way for the victory of S. W. R. D. Bandaranaike in 1956.
  • The left: the hartal was the high point of the LSSP's influence; it later entered coalition governments with the Sri Lanka Freedom Party.
05

What do others say?

At the time

The government argued that the subsidy was ruining the country's finances. The left argued that the poor should not pay for the crisis.

Historians' debates

  • Revolution or protest? Some on the left saw the hartal as a near-revolutionary moment; others see it as a protest that succeeded in changing government policy.
  • Violence: the sabotage of infrastructure was part of the protesters' strategy, while the police shootings caused the deaths.1
  • Unity: historians note that the cross-ethnic unity of 1953 contrasts with the language conflicts that began in 1956.

The evidence

07

How is it remembered?

  • The hartal is commemorated each August by left-wing parties in Sri Lanka, and is invoked in protests over the cost of living.
08

Why does it matter?

The hartal of 1953 was the first great popular challenge to an elected government in independent Sri Lanka. It showed that people would defend the welfare state they had won.

  • It is a key event in the history of food subsidies and protest in the Global South.
  • It united workers across ethnic and religious lines — a unity that soon broke down.

Questions to think about

  2. Why did the left choose a hartal rather than an ordinary strike?
  3. Was the sabotage of infrastructure justified?
  4. Why did the hartal lead to the prime minister's resignation?
09

What other events are like this?

  • 1947The All-Malaya Hartal — Malay radicals, Chinese and Indian groups united behind a 'People's Constitution' for an independent, democratic Malaya with equal citizenship for all. On one day, they shut down the country.
  • 1956The Galle Face Satyagraha and the 'Sinhala Only' Act — When Ceylon's parliament made Sinhala the only official language, Tamil leaders sat in peaceful protest outside. A mob attacked them — and the violence that followed was the first step on the road to civil war.
  • 1937The Butler Riots: Trinidad's Oilfield Strike — Oil workers led by the preacher 'Buzz' Butler struck against low wages and racism. The violence that followed killed police and strikers alike — and the island's labour movement was born.
