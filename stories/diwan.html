Language & culture

Diwan: Schools for the Breton Language

Five children and a handful of parents opened the first school in Brittany to teach entirely in Breton — a language French schools had spent a century trying to stamp out.

When
1977 – present
Where
Lampaul-Ploudalmézeau, Brittany, France

See it on the map →

01

What is the background to this event?

Breton is a Celtic language, closely related to Welsh and Cornish, spoken in western Brittany for some 1,500 years. At the beginning of the twentieth century it had around a million speakers.

  • The French Republic regarded a single national language as essential to equality and unity, and was unwilling to tolerate regional and minority languages in schools.1
  • From the late nineteenth century, children caught speaking Breton at school were often punished, and made to wear a humiliating object known as "the symbol".
  • Many parents stopped passing Breton on to their children, believing that French was the key to success. By the 1970s, most Breton speakers were elderly.
  • In the 1960s and 1970s, a Breton cultural revival — in music, publishing and politics — inspired young people to reclaim the language.

03

What happened?

In 1977, a few parents created the first Diwan school — diwan means "seed" in Breton — at Lampaul-Ploudalmézeau (Lambaol-Gwitalmeze), near Brest, because the centralised French state schools would not teach in Breton.1 It opened in May 1977 with just five children, and a second school opened in Quimper in September.1

The Diwan method is "immersion":1

  • From ages two to six, Breton is the only language of instruction.
  • French is introduced later; by the age of ten, French and Breton are taught at the same level, and pupils go on to learn English and another language.
  • The schools are free of charge and open to all, whether or not families speak Breton at home.

The schools grew through the efforts of parents and volunteers, often with very little money. The first secondary class opened in 1988, and a lycée (upper secondary school) in Carhaix in 1997. By 2019–20, 4,307 pupils attended 48 Diwan primary schools and eight secondary schools across Brittany.1 Since 2008, Diwan has organised Ar Redadeg, a non-stop relay race through Brittany in support of the Breton language.1

04

What happened next?

  • Legal battles: in 1992, France added to its constitution that "the language of the Republic is French". Attempts to bring Diwan schools into the public education system were blocked by the Conseil d'État in 2002.
  • The 2021 ruling: in 2021, the French parliament passed the Molac Law to protect regional languages. The Constitutional Council struck down the provision allowing immersion teaching in public schools, as contrary to the constitution. Diwan schools, which are private schools under contract with the state, continue, but the ruling caused protests across Brittany, the Basque Country, Corsica and elsewhere.
  • Other paths: bilingual Breton–French classes have also grown within public and Catholic schools.
05

What do others say?

At the time and since

Supporters of the French tradition of a single public language argue that immersion in a regional language undermines equality and national unity, and that French must remain the language of public schooling. Supporters of Diwan argue that a language cannot survive without being taught, and that bilingual children do well in French too.

Commentators' debates

  • Can schools save a language? Researchers note that school immersion produces new speakers, but that many do not use Breton outside school; the language also needs to be used in families, work and media.
  • The constitution: legal scholars debate whether France's constitution should be changed to recognise regional languages, and whether France should ratify the European Charter for Regional or Minority Languages, which it signed in 1999 but has not ratified.
  • Who speaks Breton? Some native speakers find the standardised Breton learned in schools different from the dialects they grew up with.

07

How is it remembered?

  • Diwan celebrates its anniversaries with festivals and the Ar Redadeg relay race, in which runners carry a baton containing a secret message in Breton across Brittany.
  • The punishment of children for speaking Breton at school is remembered in many families and in Breton literature.
08

Why does it matter?

Diwan is a powerful example of a language movement built from below. Parents who were told that their language had no future created, with almost no support from the state, an education system that has produced a new generation of Breton speakers.

  • It challenges the idea that a single national language is necessary for equality and citizenship.
  • It shows the importance of children and schools for the survival of endangered languages.
  • The legal battles over Diwan raise questions about the rights of minority languages in a centralised state.

Questions to think about

  1. Why do you think the French state was so reluctant to allow teaching in Breton?
  2. Why did parents decide to create their own schools?
  3. Can a language survive if it is only spoken at school?
  4. Should a national constitution protect minority languages?

What other events are like this?

  • 1960The Ikastolas: Basque Schools under Franco — When Franco's dictatorship banned the Basque language, teachers and parents ran secret classes in their homes. The schools they founded — the ikastolas — kept Basque alive.
  • 1983Pūnana Leo: Bringing Back Hawaiian — When fewer than fifty children still spoke Hawaiian, families opened 'language nests' — preschools that were at first illegal — and began the revival of a language that had been banned from schools for ninety years.
