The Oromo Protests

A plan to expand Ethiopia's capital into the farmland around it set off years of protests among the Oromo, the country's largest people. Hundreds were killed — and in 2018 the prime minister resigned.

When
April 2014 – February 2018
Where
Oromia region, Ethiopia

01

What is the background to this event?

Since 1991, Ethiopia had been ruled by the Ethiopian People's Revolutionary Democratic Front (EPRDF), led by the Tigray People's Liberation Front.1

  • Many Oromo and Amhara people, who together make up a majority of the population, felt marginalised.1
  • The Addis Ababa Master Plan proposed expanding the capital into the surrounding Oromia region. Oromo farmers feared they would lose their land and be displaced.1

In their own words

03

What happened?

Protests began on 25 April 2014 and resumed on 12 November 2015, when students and farmers in the town of Ginchi went on strike.1

  • Protests spread to about 400 places across Oromia. The government restricted the internet, and security forces shot, beat and arrested protesters.1
  • The Master Plan was cancelled on 12 January 2016, after about 140 protesters had been killed, but the protests continued, now demanding political reform and an end to killings and land seizures.1
  • In the summer of 2016, protests spread to the Amhara region. In early August, Reuters reported that at least 90 protesters had been shot dead in three days.1
  • On 21 August 2016, Feyisa Lilesa crossed his wrists above his head as he finished the Olympic marathon, bringing the protests to a world audience.4
  • On 2 October 2016, at the Irreecha festival in Bishoftu, attended by an estimated two million people, police used tear gas and rubber bullets, and a stampede followed. The regional government confirmed 52 deaths; rights groups and the opposition gave figures up to nearly 300.1
  • The government declared a state of emergency on 8 October 2016, banning, among other things, crossing the wrists above the head.1

According to Human Rights Watch, at least 500 people had been killed by October 2016.1

04

What happened next?

  • Resignation: on 15 February 2018, Prime Minister Hailemariam Desalegn resigned in response to the fallout from the protests and unrest.5
  • Reform: his successor, Abiy Ahmed, released thousands of political prisoners and unbanned opposition groups.6
  • War: but ethnic and political tensions grew, and in 2020 the Tigray War began.6
05

Why does it matter?

The Oromo protests forced a change of leadership in one of Africa's most powerful states. They show how a dispute about land and a city's boundaries could become a movement about identity, rights and power.15

  • Prime Minister Hailemariam Desalegn resigned in February 2018 in response to the unrest.5
  • His successor released thousands of political prisoners and unbanned opposition groups.6
  • Tensions grew again, and in 2020 the Tigray War began.6

What do others say?How it was seen at the time, and where historians disagree

At the time

Protesters demanded land rights and an end to killings and political marginalisation; the government restricted the internet and declared a state of emergency.1

Debates since

  • Land or identity? The protests began over land, but became bound up with questions of ethnic power in Ethiopia's federal system.1
  • Violence: figures for deaths differ widely. Human Rights Watch estimated at least 500 by October 2016; for the Irreecha festival alone, official and opposition figures range from 52 to nearly 300.1
How do we know?The evidence, and a source from the time
  • Reports by Human Rights Watch and Amnesty International, news agency reports and videos shared online despite internet restrictions.1
