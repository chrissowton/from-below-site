Peace & anti-war

Women of Liberia Mass Action for Peace

Christian and Muslim women dressed in white sat in the sun, prayed, and finally barricaded a peace conference until the men agreed to end Liberia's civil war.

When
2003
Where
Monrovia, Liberia, and Accra, Ghana
Facts checked

01

What is the background to this event?

Liberia was founded in the nineteenth century by freed African Americans, whose descendants (Americo-Liberians) dominated the country's politics for more than a century. In 1980 a military coup led by Samuel Doe overthrew the old elite. In 1989 Charles Taylor launched a rebellion, beginning a civil war that, with a brief pause, lasted fourteen years.1

The war was among the most brutal of its time:

  • Around 250,000 people were killed and many more were displaced.
  • All sides used child soldiers, and rape was used as a weapon of war on a massive scale.
  • Women kept their families alive, hiding sons and husbands from armed groups and walking miles to find food and water.1

Charles Taylor was elected president in 1997, but by 2003 the country was again at war, with rebel groups (LURD and MODEL) advancing on the capital, Monrovia. Taylor had been indicted for war crimes by the Special Court for Sierra Leone.

In their own words

03

What happened?

In 2003, women from Christian churches and Muslim organisations came together to form the Women of Liberia Mass Action for Peace, led by Leymah Gbowee, Comfort Freeman and Asatu Bah Kenneth — a rare alliance across religious lines in Liberia.1 Gbowee declared that the women would "take the destiny of Liberia into their own hands". Their statement continued:

"In the past we were silent, but after being killed, raped, dehumanized, and infected with diseases, and watching our children and families destroyed, war has taught us that the future lies in saying NO to violence and YES to peace! We will not relent until peace prevails."Statement of the Women of Liberia Mass Action for Peace, 20031

Their tactics were non-violent and highly visible:12

  • Dressed in white to symbolise peace, thousands of women sat, prayed and sang every day for months at the fish market in Monrovia, on the route taken by President Taylor's convoy.
  • They organised a "sex strike", refusing sexual relations with their husbands until peace was achieved, which attracted international attention.
  • In April 2003 they secured a meeting with Taylor, and pressed him to attend peace talks.

In June 2003, peace talks between the government and the rebel groups began in Accra, Ghana. A delegation of Liberian women, joined by Liberian refugees in Ghana, went to Accra to keep up the pressure. When the talks stalled in July, women dressed in white surrounded the building, blocked the doors and windows, and refused to let the delegates leave until they reached an agreement.1 When security officers threatened to arrest her, Gbowee threatened to strip — an act that in Liberian culture would bring a curse on the men who saw it.2

The women's action helped to break the deadlock. Taylor resigned on 11 August 2003 and went into exile in Nigeria, and on 18 August the parties signed the Accra Comprehensive Peace Agreement, ending the war.12

04

What happened next?

  • Disarmament: the women continued their work, helping to persuade fighters to hand in their weapons during the UN-supervised disarmament programme.
  • Elections: they campaigned for women to register and vote in the 2005 elections, which were won by Ellen Johnson Sirleaf — Africa's first elected female head of state.
  • Justice: Charles Taylor was handed over for trial in 2006. In 2012 the Special Court for Sierra Leone convicted him of aiding and abetting war crimes and crimes against humanity in Sierra Leone, and sentenced him to fifty years in prison.
  • Recognition: in 2011 Leymah Gbowee and Ellen Johnson Sirleaf shared the Nobel Peace Prize with the Yemeni activist Tawakkol Karman, "for their non-violent struggle for the safety of women and for women's rights to full participation in peace-building work".
05

What do others say?

At the time

Some politicians and fighters dismissed the women's protests, and some religious leaders were uneasy about the sex strike. International media were often more interested in the sex strike than in the women's sustained organising.

Commentators' debates

  • How much did the women achieve? Some analysts argue that the war ended mainly because of military pressure from the rebels, the indictment of Taylor and international pressure, including from the United States and West African states; others argue that the women were crucial in breaking the deadlock at Accra.
  • Who gets the credit? Gbowee has stressed that the movement was collective. Some participants have felt that international attention focused too heavily on a few leaders.
  • What changed afterwards? Under Sirleaf, new laws were introduced on rape and women's rights, but critics, including Gbowee herself, later argued that progress on corruption, justice and women's lives was too slow.

07

How is it remembered?

  • Pray the Devil Back to Hell won the award for best documentary at the Tribeca Film Festival in 2008 and brought the story to a global audience.
  • The women's white T-shirts became a symbol taken up by women peace activists across West Africa.
  • The Women Peace and Security Network Africa (WIPSEN-Africa), co-founded by Gbowee in 2006, continues the work.
08

Why does it matter?

The Liberian women's movement shows how people with no weapons and little formal power can help to end a war. By uniting across religious divides, using their moral authority as mothers, and refusing to go away, they made it impossible for men with guns to ignore them.

  • It is one of the best-known examples of women's peace-building, and is often cited in support of UN Security Council Resolution 1325 on women, peace and security.
  • It inspired similar women's movements in Côte d'Ivoire, Sudan and elsewhere.
  • It helped to bring about the election of Africa's first female head of state.
