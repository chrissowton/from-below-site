Narmada Bachao Andolan — Save the Narmada

A movement of Adivasi villagers, farmers and activists challenged one of the world's largest dam projects, forcing the World Bank to withdraw and asking who pays the price of 'development'.

When
1985 – present
Where
The Narmada valley, Gujarat, Madhya Pradesh and Maharashtra, India

See it on the map →

01

What is the background to this event?

India planned a vast system of dams on the Narmada River, including 30 major dams. The largest, the Sardar Sarovar Dam in Gujarat, was meant to bring water and electricity to drought-prone regions.1

  • The dams would flood the lands of tens of thousands of families, many of them Adivasi (indigenous) people.1
  • The World Bank agreed to lend money for the Sardar Sarovar project in 1985.1
02

Who were the key people?

  • Medha Patkar

    A social scientist who left her doctoral studies to found and lead the movement.

  • Baba Amte

    A veteran social reformer who moved to the Narmada valley to support the movement.

  • Adivasi villagers of the valley

    Whose lands and villages faced submergence.

  • Arundhati Roy

    A novelist who wrote in support of the movement and was jailed briefly for contempt of court in 2002.

03

What happened?

In 1985, Medha Patkar visited the dam area and found that displaced people were being compensated only for standing crops, not for the loss of their land and homes.1

  • She organised the people of the valley, and the Narmada Bachao Andolan (NBA) was formed.1
  • The movement used court cases, rallies, marches and hunger strikes.1
  • A 36-day march through the valley was stopped by police, who arrested marchers.1
  • Villagers vowed to drown rather than leave as the waters rose (the "jal samarpan").2
  • Under pressure, the World Bank commissioned an independent review, the Morse Report, which was highly critical. The Bank withdrew from the project in 1993.2
  • The NBA, with Medha Patkar and Baba Amte, received the Right Livelihood Award in 1991.1

04

What happened next?

  • The Supreme Court: in 2000, India's Supreme Court allowed construction to continue, subject to resettlement conditions.
  • Completion: the Sardar Sarovar Dam was completed and inaugurated in 2017.
  • Resettlement: the movement continues to campaign for the rights of displaced people, many of whom say they have not been properly resettled.
05

What do others say?

At the time

Supporters of the dam, including the Gujarat government, argued that it would bring drinking water and irrigation to millions in drought-prone areas. The NBA argued the costs were hidden and the displaced ignored.2

Debates since

  • Development: the dam now supplies water to many towns and villages in Gujarat; critics argue the benefits are smaller than promised and the costs fell on the poorest.
  • Numbers displaced: estimates vary widely depending on who is counted.2
  • Non-violence: the movement was committed to non-violence; it faced arrests and police force.1
06

The evidence

  • The Morse Report (1992).
  • Court records of the Supreme Court cases.
  • Films such as Anand Patwardhan's A Narmada Diary (1995).
07

How is it remembered?

  • Arundhati Roy's essay "The Greater Common Good" (1999) brought the struggle international attention.
08

Why does it matter?

The Narmada struggle became a global symbol of resistance to large dams and to development that displaces the poor.

  • It forced the World Bank to rethink its lending for big dams.
  • It contributed to the World Commission on Dams (1998–2000).

Questions to think about

  1. Who benefits from big dams, and who pays the cost?
  2. Why did the World Bank withdraw?
  3. Was the movement a success or a failure?
  4. How should governments balance the needs of different communities?
09

What other events are like this?

  • 1974The Chipko Movement — Village women in the Himalayas placed themselves between the loggers and the forest, and forced a ban on commercial felling.
  • 1971The Sanrizuka Struggle against Narita Airport — When the government chose their farmland for Tokyo's new airport without asking them, farmers and student radicals fought for decades. Police and protesters died, and the airport opened years late — with one runway.
  • 2016Berta Cáceres and the Fight for the Gualcarque River — Lenca communities blockaded the road to a dam on their sacred river for more than a year, forcing the world's largest dam builder to pull out. Their leader, Berta Cáceres, won the Goldman Prize — and was murdered a year later.
