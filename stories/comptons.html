The Compton's Cafeteria Riot

Three years before Stonewall, trans women and drag queens in San Francisco fought back against constant police harassment at an all-night cafeteria — the start of organised trans activism in the city.

When
August 1966
Where
Tenderloin, San Francisco, United States
Facts checked
2026-11-16

See it on the map →

01

What is the background to this event?

In the 1960s, the Tenderloin district of San Francisco was home to many poor and marginalised people, including many trans women and drag queens.1

  • Many trans women could not find work and survived through sex work. They faced violence, including murders.1
  • Police regularly arrested trans women for "female impersonation" and other offences.1
  • Gene Compton's Cafeteria, open all night, was one of the few places they could gather. The management called the police to remove them.1
  • Local groups, including Glide Memorial Methodist Church and the youth organisation Vanguard, had begun to organise.1

03

What happened?

One night in August 1966 — the exact date is unknown — police were called to the cafeteria. When an officer grabbed a trans woman, she threw her coffee in his face.12

  • Customers fought back, throwing dishes and furniture and breaking the cafeteria's windows.2
  • The fighting spilled into the street, where drag queens and trans women fought back against the police with high heels and heavy bags.1
  • A police car was damaged and a newsstand burned.2
  • The next night, protesters picketed the cafeteria, and its new windows were broken again.2

04

What happened next?

  • Services: in the years after the riot, San Francisco set up some of the first support services for trans people, including a health and peer support network.
  • Activism: the riot marked the beginning of trans activism in San Francisco.1
  • Forgotten: the riot was little reported at the time and almost forgotten until Stryker's research in the 1990s.2
05

What do others say?

At the time

The riot received almost no press coverage, and police records of it have not been found.2

Debates since

  • Evidence: the date and details rely on later memories, an article in a 1972 programme and a few other sources.2
  • Violence: the riot involved property damage and fighting with police, in response to years of arrests and abuse.1
06

The evidence

  • Oral history interviews collected by Susan Stryker.
  • Records of Vanguard and Glide Church.
07

How is it remembered?

  • A plaque was placed at the site for the 40th anniversary in 2006.
  • In 2017, part of the Tenderloin was named the Compton's Transgender Cultural District.
08

Why does it matter?

Compton's shows that trans women, many of them poor and working-class, were among the first to resist police harassment — years before Stonewall.

  • It linked queer liberation to poverty and policing.
  • It shows how history from below can be lost and recovered.

Questions to think about

  1. Why was an all-night cafeteria so important to trans women in the Tenderloin?
  2. Why was the riot forgotten for so long?
  3. Why is Stonewall better known than Compton's?

  • 1897The Scientific-Humanitarian Committee — Founded in Berlin in 1897, the world's first organisation for the rights of gay, lesbian and trans people petitioned parliament for decades to abolish the law against sex between men — until the Nazis destroyed its home.
