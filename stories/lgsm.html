Lesbians and Gays Support the Miners

During the great miners' strike, a group of lesbians and gay men in London raised money for a mining community in South Wales. The solidarity was returned: miners marched at Pride, and their union backed gay rights.

When
1984 – 1985
Where
London and the Dulais Valley, Wales
Facts checked
2026-11-15

See it on the map →

01

What is the background to this event?

In 1984, the National Union of Mineworkers (NUM) began a year-long strike against pit closures. The government of Margaret Thatcher had the union's funds seized by the courts.1

  • Supporters were encouraged to "twin" with mining communities and give to them directly.1
  • Lesbians and gay men faced hostility from the press, the police and the government.
02

Who were the key people?

  • Mark Ashton

    A gay Communist activist who co-founded LGSM; he died of AIDS in 1987.

  • Mike Jackson

    Co-founder of LGSM.

  • Dai Donovan

    A miner from the Dulais Valley who spoke at LGSM events.

  • Siân James

    A miner's wife from the Swansea Valley who became an MP.

  • Lesbians Against Pit Closures

    A women-only group formed by lesbians who split from LGSM.

In their own words

03

What happened?

At the London Lesbian and Gay Pride march in 1984, Mark Ashton and Mike Jackson collected money for the miners. They then founded LGSM.1

  • The group met at the Gay's the Word bookshop in London, then in a gay pub in Islington.1
  • The London group was twinned with the miners' support group in the Neath, Dulais and Swansea valleys of South Wales.1
  • It raised around £22,500 — including from a "Pits and Perverts" benefit concert headlined by Bronski Beat in December 1984.1
  • Members visited the mining villages, and miners visited London.1
  • By 1985, eleven LGSM groups existed across the UK.1

04

What happened next?

  • Pride 1985: miners' groups marched at the front of the London Lesbian and Gay Pride march.1
  • Labour: at the 1985 Labour Party conference, the NUM's block vote helped pass a resolution supporting lesbian and gay rights.1
  • The strike: the miners returned to work in March 1985 without winning their demands.
  • Mark Ashton died in 1987. A fund for people with HIV is named after him.1
05

What do others say?

At the time

Some in the mining villages, and some in the gay community, were suspicious of the alliance. The tabloid press mocked it.1

Debates since

  • The film: the 2014 film Pride made LGSM famous; some members noted that it simplified events and gave less attention to lesbians.1
  • Lasting change? historians debate how far the alliance changed attitudes beyond those directly involved.
06

The evidence

  • LGSM's minutes, letters, press cuttings, badges and banner, preserved in an archive.1
  • Photographs and interviews with members and miners.
07

How is it remembered?

  • The film Pride (2014) dramatised the story.1
  • A plaque to Mark Ashton was placed at Gay's the Word bookshop in 2017.
08

Why does it matter?

LGSM is an example of solidarity between very different communities facing the same government.

  • It helped change attitudes in the labour movement.
  • It shows that support can flow both ways.
