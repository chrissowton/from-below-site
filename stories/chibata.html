Civil rights & anti-racism

The Revolt of the Lash

Black sailors in Brazil's navy were still being flogged, 22 years after the abolition of slavery. Led by João Cândido, they seized the navy's new battleships and turned their guns on the capital.

When
22 – 26 November 1910
Where
Guanabara Bay, Rio de Janeiro, Brazil
Facts checked

01

What is the background to this event?

Brazil abolished slavery only in 1888, the last country in the Americas to do so. In 1889, a coup overthrew the monarchy and created a republic.

  • In the early twentieth century, Brazil bought powerful new battleships, the dreadnoughts Minas Geraes and São Paulo, to become a naval power.1
  • Elite white officers commanded mostly Black and mixed-race sailors, many forced into the navy on long contracts.1
  • Officers still punished sailors by flogging, even though the practice had been banned in most other countries and in the rest of Brazil.1
  • Sailors who had trained in Britain saw British sailors who were no longer lashed and were treated as citizens.1

In their own words

03

What happened?

Sailors aboard Minas Geraes had been planning a revolt for years. The trigger was the flogging of a sailor, Marcelino Rodrigues Menezes, with 250 lashes for bringing alcohol on board.1

  • At about 10 p.m. on 22 November 1910, perhaps 1,500 to 2,000 sailors rose up.1
  • On Minas Geraes, the ship's captain and several loyal and rebel crewmen were killed in the fighting.1
  • By midnight, the rebels controlled both dreadnoughts, a new cruiser and a coastal defence ship — more firepower than the rest of the navy.1
  • They sent a letter to the president demanding an end to what they called the "slavery" practised by the navy.1
  • The rebel ships fired on forts and naval bases around the bay. One shell hit a house, killing two children; João Cândido and his crew later paid for their burial from their own wages.1

Congress, led by Senator Rui Barbosa, passed an amnesty for the rebels and abolished corporal punishment in the navy.1 On 26 November, the sailors surrendered the ships.

04

What happened next?

  • Betrayal: despite the amnesty, many sailors were quickly discharged, and many original mutineers were jailed or sent to work in the rubber regions of the Amazon.1
  • The second revolt: in December 1910, a revolt of marines on Ilha das Cobras was crushed. More than 600 sailors were imprisoned there, and many died, some in a cell where quicklime produced deadly heat.1
  • João Cândido: he survived imprisonment, was expelled from the navy and lived in poverty as a fish seller. He died in 1969.
  • Recognition: in 2008, Brazil granted João Cândido a posthumous amnesty, and a statue of him was unveiled in Rio de Janeiro.
05

What do others say?

At the time

The government and officers saw the mutiny as a grave threat to order. Many in the public sympathised with the sailors' demand to end flogging.

Historians' debates

  • Race: Zachary Morgan and others emphasise the racial dimension of the revolt, linking it to slavery and the Atlantic world.
  • Violence: the mutineers killed officers and loyal sailors and their bombardment killed two children; the state's later revenge — imprisonments, deaths and deportations — was far greater.1
  • Memory: the Brazilian navy long resisted honouring João Cândido; recognition came only in the twenty-first century.

How is it remembered?

  • The song "O Mestre-Sala dos Mares" by João Bosco and Aldir Blanc (1975) celebrated João Cândido, and was censored by the military dictatorship.
  • A statue of João Cândido stands in Rio de Janeiro.
08

Why does it matter?

The Revolt of the Lash was a struggle against the survival of slavery's violence after abolition. Black sailors won the formal end of flogging in the navy.

  • It shows how racial hierarchy persisted in Brazil after slavery.
  • It raises questions about promises — such as the amnesty — that are broken once protesters lay down their arms.
