The Revolt of the Batavi

A people of the Rhine delta who supplied Rome with soldiers turned their weapons on the empire. Led by a prince who had served Rome for decades, they destroyed two legions before making peace.

When
69 – 70
Where
Nijmegen and the Rhine delta, Netherlands
Facts checked
2026-11-16

See it on the map →

01

What is the background to this event?

The Batavi lived in the Rhine delta, in what is now the Netherlands. They paid no taxes to Rome, but provided soldiers instead.1

  • Batavian auxiliary troops were famous horsemen and swimmers.
  • Julius Civilis, a Batavian prince, had served in the Roman army for 25 years. He was twice arrested on charges of rebellion.1
  • In 69, the "Year of the Four Emperors", Rome was in civil war, and a harsh new levy of Batavian soldiers caused anger.1
02

Who were the key people?

  • Julius Civilis

    Batavian prince and Roman auxiliary officer who led the revolt.

  • Veleda

    A seeress of the Bructeri who supported the revolt.

  • Quintus Petillius Cerialis

    The Roman general who ended it.

In their own words

03

What happened?

Civilis used the Roman civil war as cover, claiming at first to support Vespasian.1

  • The Batavi were joined by other Germanic and Gallic peoples, and by Batavian auxiliary troops.1
  • The rebels besieged the legionary fortress of Vetera (Xanten). Two legions were destroyed, and others went over to the rebels.1
  • In 70, Vespasian, now emperor, sent Cerialis with a large army. After several battles the rebels were defeated.1
  • Civilis negotiated a peace, and his fate is unknown.1
04

What happened next?

  • Roman rule restored: a legion was stationed at Nijmegen, and the Batavi continued to supply troops.1
05

Why does it matter?

The Batavian revolt shows how soldiers trained by an empire could turn against it.

  • It also shows how the past can be reused for national myths.
  • Rome restored its rule, stationed a legion at Nijmegen, and went on recruiting Batavian troops.1
  • During the Dutch revolt against Spain, the Batavians were celebrated as forerunners of Dutch freedom.1
  • Rembrandt painted The Conspiracy of Claudius Civilis for Amsterdam's town hall.

What do others say?How it was seen at the time, and where historians disagree

At the time

Tacitus, our main source, gives speeches to Civilis attacking Roman slavery, but also portrays him as ambitious.3

Debates since

  • Freedom or ambition? Historians debate whether Civilis sought independence or power within the Roman system.1
  • Violence: the rebels killed surrendering legionaries after Vetera, according to Tacitus; Roman forces devastated Batavian lands.3
How do we know?The evidence, and a source from the time
  • Tacitus's Histories.3
  • Archaeology at Nijmegen and other sites on the Rhine.

Primary source: Tombstone

What is it?
Tombstone
Made by, and when
Rome, AD 54–68

The tombstone of Indus, 'of the Batavian nation', a member of the emperor Nero's personal bodyguard, set up by his brother. Batavians served Rome as soldiers, and the revolt of 69 was led by men who knew the Roman army from the inside.

Reading it

Indus served in Nero's personal bodyguard, and his brother proudly recorded him as 'of the Batavian nation'. The stone shows how close the Batavians were to Roman power — trusted enough to guard an emperor. That closeness is the key to the revolt of 69, led by men who knew the Roman army from the inside.

How is it remembered?Memorials, anniversaries and arguments over memory
  • During the Dutch revolt against Spain (1568–1648), the Batavians were celebrated as forerunners of Dutch freedom.1
  • Rembrandt painted The Conspiracy of Claudius Civilis for Amsterdam's town hall.
