The Great Dock Strike: 'The Dockers' Tanner'

Casual dock labourers, hired by the hour at the dock gates, shut down the Port of London for four weeks — and won. Their victory opened the way for unions of unskilled workers across Britain.

When
12 August – 14 September 1889
Where
The Port of London, England
Facts checked

01

What is the background to this event?

London's dockers were casual labourers. Each morning, crowds gathered at the dock gates hoping a foreman would pick them for a few hours' work.1

  • They earned about five pence an hour, with a small bonus, the 'plus', for finishing jobs early — which employers often refused to pay.1
  • The manager of the Millwall Docks told Parliament that many came to work without having eaten for 24 hours.1
  • In 1888, the matchwomen at Bryant and May had won their strike, and London's gasworkers had organised a union. The dockers took confidence from both.1

In their own words

03

What happened?

On 12 August 1889, a dispute over the 'plus' at the West India Docks began a walkout. By 22 August the whole port had stopped.1

  • Estimates of the number of strikers range from 100,000 to 130,000, including stevedores, carpenters, lightermen and many other trades.1
  • The unions demanded at least four hours' work for anyone hired, fair hiring at set times, and sixpence an hour — the 'dockers' tanner'.1
  • The strikers held daily marches with brass bands and banners into the City of London, and mass meetings on Tower Hill. The peaceful processions won wide public sympathy.1
  • Money poured in: some £16,000 from the British public and unions, and about £30,000 from Australia, where newspapers backed the dockers.1
  • With the port paralysed, the Lord Mayor and Cardinal Manning brought both sides together. On 14 September, the dockers won most of their demands, including the tanner.1

04

What happened next?

  • New Unionism: the strike was a landmark victory for unions of unskilled and casual workers, such as the Dock, Wharf, Riverside and General Labourers' Union.1
  • Abroad: it inspired a dock strike in Rotterdam the same year.1
  • Changing minds: the strikers' discipline challenged middle-class ideas that the poor were disorderly and impossible to organise.1
05

What do others say?

At the time

The dock companies tried to break the strike and resist the unions; the strikers and their supporters argued for a living wage and fair hiring.1

Debates since

  • A turning point? Historians such as Eric Hobsbawm see the strike as marking a transformation of the British labour movement.1
  • Violence: the strike was notable for being peaceful.1

The evidence

How is it remembered?

  • The 'dockers' tanner' became a famous symbol of the strike's victory.1
08

Why does it matter?

The dockers were thought to be impossible to organise. By winning, they showed that the lowest-paid and most casual workers could build unions too.1

  • 1888The Matchwomen's Strike — About 1,400 women and girls walked out of a match factory, won their demands in three weeks, and founded the largest union of women in Britain.
  • 1886The Haymarket Affair — At a rally for the eight-hour day, someone threw a bomb at the police. Eight anarchists were convicted without evidence that any of them threw it, and four were hanged. Their deaths gave the world May Day.
  • 1834The Tolpuddle Martyrs — Six farm labourers formed a society to resist wage cuts and were sentenced to transportation to Australia.
