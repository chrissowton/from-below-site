The Banana Massacre

Some 25,000 banana workers struck against the United Fruit Company. Soldiers set up machine guns around the town square and opened fire on workers and their families. Gabriel García Márquez made it unforgettable.

When
12 November – 6 December 1928
Where
Ciénaga, Magdalena, Colombia
Facts checked
2026-11-16

See it on the map →

01

What is the background to this event?

In the early twentieth century, the American United Fruit Company dominated the banana-growing zone around Santa Marta on Colombia's Caribbean coast.

  • Workers were hired through subcontractors, so the company could deny that they were its employees and avoid labour laws.1
  • Many were paid in coupons that could only be spent in company stores.1
  • A strike a decade earlier had brought little change.1

In October 1928, the workers presented nine demands:1

  • an end to hiring through subcontractors;
  • collective insurance and compensation for accidents at work;
  • hygienic housing and a six-day week;
  • higher pay and weekly wages;
  • the abolition of company stores and payment in coupons;
  • better hospital services.

In their own words

03

What happened?

On 12 November 1928, the banana workers went on strike. It became the largest labour movement Colombia had ever seen.1 The company refused to negotiate.1

  • US officials and United Fruit representatives described the strike as communist and subversive in telegrams to Washington.1
  • The government sent General Carlos Cortés Vargas with 700 soldiers from the interior — not local troops, who might have been related to the workers.1
  • On 5 December, about 1,500 workers and their families gathered in the town square of Ciénaga.1
  • Troops set up machine guns on the roofs around the square and closed off the streets. After a five-minute warning to disperse, they opened fire on the crowd, including children.1

General Cortés Vargas admitted to 47 deaths.1 Other estimates run into the hundreds or as high as 2,000; the true number remains unknown because of the lack of records.1

04

What happened next?

  • Gaitán: in 1929, Jorge Eliécer Gaitán investigated the massacre and denounced the army and the company in Congress, making his name as a champion of the poor. His assassination in 1948 set off the Bogotazo riots and La Violencia.
  • Repression: after the massacre, troops hunted down strike leaders, and many more workers were killed or imprisoned.
  • Literature: Gabriel García Márquez, born nearby in Aracataca in 1927, retold the massacre in One Hundred Years of Solitude (1967), in which the dead are carried away on a train and the event is officially denied.
05

What do others say?

At the time

The government and the company claimed the strike was a communist revolution. Workers and their supporters saw it as a peaceful struggle for basic rights.

Historians' debates

  • The death toll: estimates range from 47 to 2,000; García Márquez's novel suggested 3,000, a figure he later said was invented.1
  • The role of United Fruit and the US: historians debate how far the company directly asked for the army; US diplomatic telegrams show that it pressed for action against the "communist" strike.1
  • Violence: the army claimed the strikers were armed and dangerous; most accounts describe a largely peaceful crowd.

How is it remembered?

  • A monument in Ciénaga commemorates the victims.
  • The massacre is remembered across Latin America through García Márquez's novel.
08

Why does it matter?

The Banana Massacre is one of the most notorious episodes in the history of foreign corporations in Latin America. It shows how states could use their armies to protect companies against their own workers.

  • It raises questions about the power of multinational companies.
  • It shows how literature can preserve a memory that official history tried to erase.

Questions to think about

  1. Why did the workers want to be employed directly by the company?
  2. Why do you think the government sent troops from outside the region?

What other events are like this?

  • 1954The Great Banana Strike — In the original 'banana republic', 40,000 workers walked out on the American fruit companies. After 69 days, they won higher pay and the right to a union.
  • 1907The Santa María School Massacre — Thousands of striking nitrate miners and their families were shot down in and around a school in Iquique — one of the worst massacres of workers in Latin American history.
  • 1932The 1932 Uprising and La Matanza — Indigenous peasants and communists rose against a military government in coffee country. The army's revenge killed thousands and nearly wiped out a language.
