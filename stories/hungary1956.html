The Hungarian Revolution of 1956

Students, workers and soldiers rose against Hungary's Soviet-backed dictatorship and pulled down Stalin's statue. For twelve days they won — until Soviet tanks returned.

When
23 October – 10 November 1956
Where
Budapest, Hungary

01

What is the background to this event?

After the Second World War, Hungary came under Soviet control. Through intimidation and "salami tactics" — slicing away opponents one by one — the Communist Party took power, and in 1949 Hungary became a one-party People's Republic.1

  • Under Mátyás Rákosi, one of the most Stalinist leaders in Eastern Europe, the secret police (the ÁVH) imprisoned, tortured and killed opponents. Show trials targeted even loyal communists and church leaders such as Cardinal József Mindszenty.1
  • Industry and agriculture were nationalised and collectivised; living standards fell.
  • After Stalin's death in 1953, the reformist communist Imre Nagy briefly became prime minister and eased the repression, but he was removed in 1955.1
  • In February 1956, the Soviet leader Nikita Khrushchev denounced Stalin's crimes in a "secret speech". Its contents spread, and intellectuals in Hungary grew bolder. In October 1956, protests in Poland forced the Soviet leadership to accept a reformist government there.

In their own words

03

What happened?

On 22 October 1956, students at the Budapest Technical University drew up a list of sixteen demands, including the withdrawal of Soviet troops, free elections, freedom of speech and the press, and the return of Imre Nagy.1

On the afternoon of 23 October, around 20,000 protesters gathered at the statue of József Bem, a hero of the 1848 revolution, and the crowd grew to hundreds of thousands. That evening:1

  • Protesters pulled down the giant statue of Stalin in Budapest, leaving only its boots on the pedestal.
  • A student delegation entered the building of Hungarian Radio to broadcast the demands, and was detained. When the crowd outside demanded their release, ÁVH officers opened fire, killing several people. Some Hungarian soldiers sent to defend the building tore the red stars from their caps and joined the protesters.
  • Protesters cut the communist coat of arms out of the centre of Hungarian flags; the flag with a hole became the symbol of the revolution.

Soviet tanks entered Budapest on 24 October, but were met by fierce resistance from young fighters. Across the country, revolutionary committees and workers' councils took over local government and factories. Imre Nagy became prime minister.1 On 28 October a ceasefire was agreed, and Soviet troops began to withdraw from Budapest. The Nagy government disbanded the ÁVH, released political prisoners and promised free elections. On 1 November, Nagy announced that Hungary was leaving the Warsaw Pact and declared its neutrality.1

But the Soviet leadership had decided to crush the revolution. Before dawn on 4 November, Soviet forces launched a massive attack on Budapest and other cities. Nagy took refuge in the Yugoslav embassy. Fighting continued for several days; workers' councils kept up a general strike for weeks. Around 2,500 Hungarians and some 700 Soviet soldiers were killed, and around 200,000 Hungarians fled the country as refugees.12

04

What happened next?

  • Repression: under the Kádár government, tens of thousands were arrested and around 230 people were executed for their part in the revolution.
  • Nagy's execution: Imre Nagy was lured out of the Yugoslav embassy, abducted, and after a secret trial was hanged on 16 June 1958. He was buried in an unmarked grave.
  • The world looks away: Western governments, distracted by the Suez crisis, gave no practical help. The United Nations condemned the invasion but took no action.
  • Reburial: on 16 June 1989, Nagy and his fellow martyrs were reburied in Budapest before a crowd of hundreds of thousands — a key moment in the end of communism in Hungary. The Republic of Hungary was proclaimed on 23 October 1989.
05

What do others say?

At the time

The Soviet Union and the Kádár government described the revolution as a "counter-revolution" organised by fascists and Western agents. Many in the West saw it as a heroic national rising against communism. Radio Free Europe, funded by the United States, was criticised for encouraging the fighters to expect Western help that never came.

Historians' debates

  • What kind of revolution? Historians such as Bill Lomax emphasise the workers' councils and the socialist character of many demands; others stress its national and anti-communist character. It contained many different hopes.
  • Violence: some ÁVH officers and suspected communists were lynched by crowds, notably at Republic Square on 30 October. Historians debate how to weigh this violence.
  • Could it have survived? Opened archives show the Soviet leadership hesitated before deciding to invade, partly influenced by Nagy's declaration of neutrality and the Suez crisis.

How is it remembered?

  • 23 October is a national holiday in Hungary.
  • The 1956 Memorial in Budapest stands on the site of the Stalin statue; the statue's boots are preserved in replica at Memento Park.
  • Imre Nagy's grave at the New Public Cemetery in Budapest, plot 301, is a national memorial.
08

Why does it matter?

The Hungarian Revolution was the first great challenge to Soviet rule in Eastern Europe. For a few days, ordinary Hungarians — students, workers, soldiers and teenagers — overthrew a dictatorship and began to build a free society, before it was crushed by force.

  • The workers' councils showed how working people could organise their own democracy.
  • Its suppression disillusioned many communists in the West, and showed that the Soviet Union would use force to keep control of its empire.
  • Its memory helped to inspire the peaceful revolutions of 1989.
