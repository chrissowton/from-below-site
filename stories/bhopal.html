The Bhopal Gas Disaster and the Survivors' Movement

A gas leak from a US-owned pesticide factory poisoned more than half a million people in one night. For four decades, survivors — many of them poor women — have campaigned for justice, medical care and a clean-up.

01

What is the background to this event?

Union Carbide India Limited (UCIL), majority-owned by the US company Union Carbide Corporation (UCC), opened a factory in Bhopal in 1969 to make the pesticide Sevin. It used methyl isocyanate (MIC), a highly toxic gas.1

  • The factory stood next to crowded, poor neighbourhoods.1
  • Safety systems had been allowed to decline, and there had been earlier leaks and warnings.2

  • Abdul Jabbar

In their own words

03

What happened?

On the night of 2–3 December 1984, water entered a tank of MIC, causing a reaction that released a cloud of gas over the city. More than 500,000 people were exposed.1

  • The gas is heavier than air and settled close to the ground. Children, who were shorter, inhaled higher concentrations.1
  • Hospital staff had no information about the gas and no antidote. Thousands of people were dead by the morning.1
  • The official number of immediate deaths is 2,259. Other estimates put the number who died from gas-related illnesses at 8,000 or more, and some campaigners give much higher figures.1
  • A government affidavit in 2006 counted about 558,000 injuries.1

The survivors' movement

Survivors, many of them women from poor neighbourhoods, organised to demand compensation, medical care and justice. They held sit-ins, marches and hunger strikes, including long marches to Delhi.2

04

What happened next?

  • Settlement: in 1989, Union Carbide paid $470 million in a settlement agreed with the Indian government. Survivors' groups argued it was far too little.1
  • Dow: Dow Chemical bought Union Carbide in 2001, and denies responsibility for the disaster.1
  • Convictions: in 2010, seven former UCIL employees were convicted of causing death by negligence and sentenced to two years' imprisonment.1
  • Contamination: chemical waste at the site has polluted groundwater. In 2025, hundreds of tonnes of waste were removed for incineration, after decades of campaigning.
05

Why does it matter?

Bhopal is often called the world's worst industrial disaster. It raised lasting questions about the responsibility of multinational companies for harm done in poorer countries.

  • The survivors' movement shows how ordinary people, especially women, organised after disaster.
  • It helped shape environmental and corporate accountability law in India.
  • Union Carbide paid $470 million in 1989 in a settlement survivors' groups called far too little.1

What do others say?How it was seen at the time, and where historians disagree

At the time

Union Carbide suggested the leak may have been caused by sabotage by a disgruntled employee. Survivors and the Indian government blamed poor safety and cost-cutting.1

Debates since

  • Death toll: official figures and campaigners' estimates differ widely.1
  • Responsibility: US courts sent cases back to India, treating UCIL as an Indian company; critics argue the parent company controlled safety.1
  • The Indian state: critics also blame Indian authorities for allowing the factory near homes and for the settlement.2

Reading it

The factory photographed the year after the leak sits among homes — many poor families had settled right beside it. That closeness is why the gas killed so many. Who should clean up the contaminated site is a question survivors are still asking.

How is it remembered?Memorials, anniversaries and arguments over memory
  • Survivors mark the anniversary every year on 3 December, burning effigies of company executives.
  • A memorial statue stands near the factory site.
  • The Remember Bhopal Museum opened in 2014.
