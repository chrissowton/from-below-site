The February Uprising of 1934

When the authoritarian government of Engelbert Dollfuss moved to crush Austria's labour movement, socialist workers took up arms. The army shelled council housing in Vienna; within four days the rising was over, and democracy with it.

When
12 – 15 February 1934
Where
Vienna, Linz and industrial towns of Austria

See it on the map →

01

What is the background to this event?

After 1918, Austria was a democracy split between the Social Democrats, strong in the cities, and the Catholic Christian Socials, strong in the countryside.1

  • "Red Vienna" built tens of thousands of council flats, including huge estates such as the Karl-Marx-Hof.
  • Both sides had paramilitary forces: the right-wing Heimwehr, which by 1934 was openly fascist, and the Social Democrats' Republican Protection League (Schutzbund).1
  • In July 1927, police fired on crowds in Vienna, killing 89 people, including four police officers.1
  • In 1933, Chancellor Engelbert Dollfuss suspended parliament, banned the Schutzbund and began ruling by decree.
02

Who were the key people?

  • Richard Bernaschek

    Schutzbund commander in Linz, whose men fired the first shots.

  • Koloman Wallisch

    A Social Democrat leader in Styria, hanged after the rising.

  • Karl Münichreiter

    A Viennese shoemaker and Schutzbund member, hanged though wounded.

  • Engelbert Dollfuss

    Chancellor of Austria, who ordered the army to use artillery.

  • Otto Bauer and Julius Deutsch

    Social Democrat leaders, who fled abroad.

03

What happened?

Early on 12 February 1934, police came to search the Social Democrats' headquarters in Linz, the Hotel Schiff, for weapons. Schutzbund members, under Richard Bernaschek, opened fire.1

  • Fighting spread to Vienna and to industrial towns in Upper Austria and Styria.1
  • Schutzbund fighters held out in fortified council estates. Many workers did not join, and a call for a general strike largely failed.
  • Dollfuss ordered the army to shell the Karl-Marx-Hof with light artillery, endangering civilians and destroying many flats.1
  • The army, police and Heimwehr had far greater numbers and firepower, and the rising was crushed within days.1

About 350 people were killed in total, on both sides, including civilians.1 Nine socialists were hanged.2

04

What happened next?

  • Dictatorship: the Social Democratic Party and unions were banned, and in May 1934 a new authoritarian constitution created a one-party state under the Fatherland Front.1
  • Dollfuss was murdered by Austrian Nazis in a failed coup in July 1934.
  • Anschluss: in 1938, Nazi Germany annexed Austria, meeting little organised resistance from a divided country.
05

What do others say?

At the time

The government called the rising a Marxist revolt against the state; socialists abroad called it a heroic defence of democracy.2

Debates since

  • Who started it? The Schutzbund fired first in Linz, but the government had already destroyed parliamentary democracy and was dismantling the labour movement.12
  • Violence on both sides: police, soldiers and Heimwehr men were killed, as well as Schutzbund fighters and civilians caught in the fighting.1
  • "Civil war"? Austrian historians still debate the name, and the role of the Dollfuss regime.
06

The evidence

  • Photographs of shell damage to the council estates.
  • Court-martial records and the memoirs of participants.
07

How is it remembered?

  • Plaques on Vienna's council estates mark the fighting.
  • Austria's parliament formally rehabilitated the convicted socialists in 2012.
08

Why does it matter?

The February rising was one of the first armed attempts by workers to resist the rise of fascism in Europe — and its defeat helped open the way for Nazi rule.

  • It raises hard questions about when armed resistance to dictatorship is justified.

Questions to think about

  1. Why did both sides in Austria have private armies?
  2. Why did the Schutzbund take up arms?
  3. Was the government justified in shelling housing estates?
  4. How did the defeat of 1934 make the Anschluss easier?
09

What other events are like this?

  • 1936The Battle of Cable Street — When the British Union of Fascists tried to march through the Jewish East End, tens of thousands of local people blocked the way.
  • 1871The Paris Commune — After defeat in war, the working people of Paris took over their city and governed it for 72 days. The Commune ended in 'Bloody Week', when the army killed thousands — and Communards shot their hostages.
  • 1941The February Strike — After German police seized 425 Jewish men in Amsterdam, the city's tram drivers, dockers and factory workers walked out. It was the first mass public protest against the persecution of Jews in Nazi-occupied Europe.
