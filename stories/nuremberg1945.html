Nuremberg: Witnesses Against the Nazi Leaders

The victorious Allies put the surviving leaders of Nazi Germany on trial for crimes against humanity. The prosecutors relied mostly on the Nazis' own documents — but a few survivors, like the French resistance fighter Marie-Claude Vaillant-Couturier, spoke for those who could not.

When

Where
Palace of Justice, Nuremberg, Germany

01

What is the background to this event?

Between 1939 and 1945, Nazi Germany invaded much of Europe and carried out the Holocaust; in the Soviet Union alone, the war caused 27 million deaths.1

  • The Allies disagreed about what to do with the Nazi leaders: proposals ranged from a show trial to summary executions.1
  • Lawyers developed new ideas, such as 'crimes against peace' (waging aggressive war), to judge them.1
02

Who were the key people?

  • Marie-Claude Vaillant-Couturier

    A French Resistance member and photojournalist, deported to Auschwitz and Ravensbrück, who testified at the trial.

  • The International Military Tribunal

    Judges and prosecutors from France, the Soviet Union, the United Kingdom and the United States.

  • The defendants

    22 leading Nazis, including Hermann Göring.

In their own words

03

What happened?

From 20 November 1945, the International Military Tribunal tried 22 of the most important surviving leaders of Nazi Germany, as well as six Nazi organisations.1

  • Most defendants were charged with war crimes and crimes against humanity, and the Holocaust was a major focus of the trial.1
  • The American and British prosecutors relied on documents and sworn statements rather than survivors' testimony, which was then seen as less reliable. This strengthened their case but reduced public interest.1
  • Some survivors did testify. Marie-Claude Vaillant-Couturier, who had been deported to Auschwitz-Birkenau in January 1943 and later to Ravensbrück, described the camps.3
  • On 1 October 1946, twelve defendants were sentenced to death.1
04

What happened next?

  • Further trials: twelve more trials, held by the United States against lower-ranking perpetrators, focused more on the Holocaust.1
  • A precedent: the idea that leaders can be tried for crimes against humanity shaped later international law and tribunals.1
  • Vaillant-Couturier: she became a Communist member of the French parliament.3
05

Why does it matter?

Nuremberg established that even heads of state could be held to account for atrocities. But it was justice delivered by the victors, and the voices of victims were mostly heard through documents rather than in person.1

  • Twelve more trials of lower-ranking perpetrators followed, which focused more on the Holocaust.1
  • The idea that leaders can be tried for crimes against humanity shaped later international law and tribunals.1
  • Courtroom 600 in Nuremberg's Palace of Justice, where the trial was held, is now part of a memorial.

What do others say?How it was seen at the time, and where historians disagree

At the time

For the Allies, the trial was a way to prove the Nazis' crimes beyond doubt and teach a history lesson to the defeated Germans; over time, more Germans came to see it as illegitimate victors' justice.1

Debates since

  • Selective justice: the prosecution of only the defeated side, while crimes by the Allies were not tried, has drawn the most lasting criticism.1
  • Denial: in 1964, Paul Rassinier, one of the first Holocaust deniers, attacked Vaillant-Couturier's testimony.3
