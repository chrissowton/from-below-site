The Copperbelt Strike of 1935

When the colonial government raised the tax on African mineworkers, they walked out at three of the Copperbelt's great mines. Police shot six people dead — but the strike is remembered as the birth of Zambian nationalism.

When
May 1935
Where
Mufulira, Nkana and Roan Antelope (Luanshya), Northern Rhodesia
Facts checked
2026-11-16

See it on the map →

01

What is the background to this event?

The Copperbelt of Northern Rhodesia (now Zambia) held some of the richest copper deposits in the world. Large mines opened at Roan Antelope and Nkana in 1931.1

  • The British colonial government taxed Africans heavily, partly to force men to work for wages in the mines.1
  • During the Great Depression, mine employment fell from about 32,000 in 1930 to under 7,000 at the end of 1932.1
  • Africans had no trade unions and no vote.

In their own words

03

What happened?

In 1935, the colonial government raised the tax on Africans in mining towns. On 29 May 1935, African workers at the Mufulira mine went on strike.1

  • The strike spread to the Nkana mine and then to Roan Antelope at Luanshya.1
  • At Roan Antelope, a crowd gathered at the mine compound office. Police opened fire, killing six people.1
  • The strike then ended.1

04

What happened next?

  • Inquiry: a commission of inquiry blamed the tax announcement and religious agitation.1
  • 1940: African miners struck again in 1940; at Nkana, troops shot and killed 17 people.2
  • Unions: the African Mineworkers' Union was founded in 1949 and became a pillar of the independence movement.
  • Independence: Zambia became independent in 1964.
05

What do others say?

At the time

The authorities saw the strike as the work of agitators and religious fanatics; historians have stressed the workers' real grievances over tax and wages.2

Debates since

  • Organisation: historians debate how far the strike was organised and by whom, given that there were no unions.2
  • Violence: the strikers were unarmed; the deaths were caused by police gunfire.1

07

How is it remembered?

  • The strikes are remembered in Zambia as part of the struggle for independence.
08

Why does it matter?

The strike was the first organised industrial action by Africans in Northern Rhodesia, and is seen by many as the birth of African nationalism there.1

  • It led to the creation of trade unions and nationalist politics.1
  • It changed British policy on African workers in towns.1

What other events are like this?

  • 1973The Durban Strikes of 1973 — Under apartheid it was illegal for Black workers to strike. In 1973, around 60,000 of them did so anyway, at more than 100 firms in Durban — reviving the Black trade union movement that would help bring down apartheid.
  • 1907The Santa María School Massacre — Thousands of striking nitrate miners and their families were shot down in and around a school in Iquique — one of the worst massacres of workers in Latin American history.
  • 1915The Chilembwe Uprising — A Baptist minister educated in the United States led a short-lived rising against forced labour, racism and the recruitment of Africans to die in Britain's war.
