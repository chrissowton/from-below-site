The Kurdish Republic of Mahabad

For eleven months, Kurds in north-western Iran ran their own republic, teaching children in Kurdish for the first time. It ended with its president on the gallows.

When
1945 – 1947
Where
Mahabad, north-western Iran

01

What is the background to this event?

The Kurds, a people of perhaps 30 to 40 million today, were divided after the First World War between Turkey, Iran, Iraq and Syria. In each country, Kurdish language and culture were often suppressed.

  • In 1941, Britain and the Soviet Union invaded Iran. The Soviets occupied the north, and central government authority collapsed there.1
  • In the mainly Kurdish town of Mahabad, a committee of middle-class people supported by tribal chiefs took over local administration.1
  • A nationalist society, the Komala (Society for the Revival of Kurdistan), was formed in 1942, and Qazi Muhammad, a respected religious judge, became its leader.1

In their own words

03

What happened?

On 22 January 1946, Qazi Muhammad announced the formation of the Kurdish Republic in Mahabad's main square.1 It existed alongside a neighbouring Azerbaijani autonomous government, both supported by the Soviet Union.1

  • Qazi Muhammad explicitly rejected full independence and sought autonomy within Iran.1
  • The republic made Kurdish the official language. Children were taught in Kurdish for the first time, and Kurdish newspapers, magazines and books were published.
  • A women's organisation was formed, and Kurdish culture flourished.
  • Its armed force, later known as the Peshmerga, was built around Mustafa Barzani's fighters from Iraq.1

Under international pressure, including from the United Nations, Soviet troops withdrew from Iran in 1946.1 In December 1946, the Iranian army entered Mahabad without much resistance.

04

What happened next?

  • Executions: on 31 March 1947, Qazi Muhammad, his brother and his cousin were hanged in the square where the republic had been declared.
  • Barzani's march: Mustafa Barzani and his fighters made a long march to the Soviet Union, where they stayed until 1958.
  • A lasting symbol: the flag of the republic became the basis of the Kurdish flag used today, and its anthem, Ey Reqîb, became the Kurdish national anthem.
05

What do others say?

At the time

The Iranian government saw the republic as a Soviet-backed separatist threat. Kurdish nationalists saw it as the realisation of their rights.

Historians' debates

  • Soviet puppet? Some historians describe Mahabad as a Soviet puppet state; others argue that it grew from genuine Kurdish nationalism that the Soviets supported for their own reasons.1
  • Tribes and townspeople: historians note tensions between urban nationalists and tribal chiefs, some of whom switched sides when the Iranian army returned.
  • Violence: the republic fell with relatively little fighting; its end was marked by executions rather than battles.

How is it remembered?

  • 22 January is commemorated by Kurds as the anniversary of the republic.
  • Qazi Muhammad is remembered as a national martyr.
08

Why does it matter?

Although it lasted less than a year, the Mahabad republic is a central memory for Kurds across the Middle East. It was a moment when Kurds governed themselves in their own language.

  • It shows how great-power politics shaped the fate of small nations.
  • It raises questions about language rights and self-determination.
