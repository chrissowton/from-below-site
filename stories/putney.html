The vote & democracy

The Putney Debates

Ordinary soldiers and their elected representatives argued with Cromwell and the army's generals about who should have the right to vote.

When
28 October – 8 November 1647
Where
St Mary's Church, Putney, London

See it on the map →

01

What is the background to this event?

By 1647 Parliament's New Model Army had defeated King Charles I in the first English Civil War. The question was what kind of settlement should follow. The army was unusual: many of its soldiers were religious radicals, and they had been exposed to new ideas through sermons, pamphlets and discussion.

Tensions were growing on several fronts:

  • Parliament, dominated by Presbyterian moderates, wanted to disband much of the army without paying soldiers the wages they were owed.
  • Regiments elected representatives known as "Agitators" to speak for them.
  • The army's senior officers — the "Grandees", including Oliver Cromwell and his son-in-law Henry Ireton — were negotiating with the defeated king.
  • The Levellers, a civilian movement led by John Lilburne, Richard Overton and William Walwyn, called for religious toleration, equality before the law and a wider franchise. Their ideas were set out in a draft constitution, An Agreement of the People.
02

Who were the key people?

  • Colonel Thomas Rainsborough

    A naval officer, army colonel and MP who was the most eloquent advocate of a wide franchise at Putney. He was killed in 1648.

  • Edward Sexby

    A trooper and Agitator who spoke for the ordinary soldiers.

  • Henry Ireton

    Commissary-General and Cromwell's son-in-law, who argued that only men with property should vote.

  • Oliver Cromwell

    Lieutenant-General, who chaired the debates in the absence of Sir Thomas Fairfax.

  • John Wildman and Maximilian Petty

    Civilian Levellers who attended and argued for the Agreement of the People.

  • William Clarke

    The army secretary whose shorthand record preserved the debates.

03

What happened?

On 28 October 1647, the army's General Council met in St Mary's Church, Putney, with officers, Agitators and civilian Levellers present. Cromwell chaired the meetings, and the secretary William Clarke and a team of stenographers recorded the discussions.1

The most contentious question was the right to vote. Thomas Rainsborough set out the radical position:

"For really I think that the poorest he that is in England hath a life to live, as the greatest he; and therefore truly, Sir, I think it's clear, that every man that is to live under a government ought first by his own consent to put himself under that government."Colonel Thomas Rainsborough, Putney, 29 October 1647 (spelling modernised)12

Henry Ireton replied for the Grandees that no one had a right to a share in deciding the affairs of the kingdom who did not have "a permanent fixed interest in this kingdom" — that is, property in land or trade.1 Edward Sexby spoke for the soldiers:

"There are many thousands of us soldiers that have ventured our lives; we have had little propriety in the kingdom as to our estates, yet we have had a birthright."Edward Sexby, Putney, 29 October 1647 (spelling modernised)2

The debates continued until 8 November, when the senior officers, concerned about their effect on military discipline, brought them to an end.1 A compromise manifesto was put to regiments at a series of rendezvous. At Corkbush Field, near Ware, on 15 November, two regiments arrived wearing copies of the Agreement of the People in their hats. Cromwell and Fairfax suppressed this mutiny, and one soldier, Private Richard Arnold, was shot.1

04

What happened next?

  • Revolution: after the second civil war in 1648, the army purged Parliament and in January 1649 tried and executed Charles I. England became a republic.
  • The Levellers defeated: Leveller leaders were imprisoned, and a Leveller-inspired mutiny was crushed at Burford in May 1649, where three soldiers were shot.
  • The vote: the franchise remained limited to property-owners. It was not until 1918 that all adult men, and 1928 that all adult women, could vote in Britain.
05

What do others say?

At the time

The Grandees argued that giving the vote to men without property would threaten property itself, as the poor majority could vote to take it away. Ireton described near-universal manhood suffrage as leading to anarchy.

Historians' debates

  • How democratic were the Levellers? C.B. Macpherson argued that most Levellers would have excluded servants and those who received alms from the vote; others dispute this reading.
  • How representative? Historians debate how far the Agitators and Levellers spoke for the rank and file of the army.
  • Significance: the debates were unknown for more than two centuries, so some historians question how much influence they actually had on later democratic thought.
06

The evidence

  • The shorthand record made by William Clarke and his team, transcribed later. It was rediscovered in the library of Worcester College, Oxford, and published in 1891.
  • Leveller pamphlets, including The Case of the Armie Truly Stated and An Agreement of the People.
  • Army declarations, newsbooks and correspondence.

The record is incomplete and sometimes difficult to follow, and it captures only those who spoke.

07

How is it remembered?

  • St Mary's Church, Putney, displays an exhibition about the debates, and Rainsborough's words are inscribed there.
  • Levellers' Day is held each May at Burford, Oxfordshire, in memory of the soldiers executed in 1649.
  • Politicians including Tony Benn frequently cited the debates as a founding moment of English democracy.
08

Why does it matter?

The Putney Debates are one of the most remarkable records of political argument in English history: ordinary soldiers arguing face to face with their generals about the basis of political power. Rainsborough's words remain one of the clearest early statements of the idea that government rests on the consent of all the governed, rich and poor alike.

  • They anticipated the arguments of later democratic movements, from the Chartists to the suffragettes.
  • They show how war and revolution can open up questions that are normally closed.
  • The divide between Rainsborough and Ireton — between rights based on personhood and rights based on property — still shapes political argument.

Questions to think about

  1. Why did Ireton believe that only property-owners should vote? Do any similar arguments survive today?
  2. Why do you think ordinary soldiers were so influential in 1647?
  3. How should we interpret a record that was taken down in shorthand and lost for more than 200 years?
  4. What does Rainsborough's idea of 'consent' mean for democracy today?

What other events are like this?

  • 1649The Diggers of St George's Hill — In the year the king was executed, a small group of poor men and women began to dig and plant common land, declaring the earth 'a common treasury for all'.
  • 1819The Peterloo Massacre — Tens of thousands of working people gathered peacefully to demand the vote. Within ten minutes, cavalry had cleared the field with sabres.
  • 1381The Peasants' Revolt — Tens of thousands of villagers and townspeople marched on London to demand an end to serfdom. For a few days, the government of England was at their mercy.
