The Iraqi Revolution of 1920

When Britain took Iraq as a 'mandate', Sunni and Shia Muslims, tribes and townspeople rose together for independence. Britain crushed the revolt with troops and aircraft, then installed a king.

When
May – October 1920
Where
Baghdad, Rumaitha and the Euphrates, Iraq

See it on the map →

01

What is the background to this event?

After the First World War, the former Ottoman provinces were to become "mandates" governed by the victors.1

  • Many feared this was "European imperial rule by another name".1
  • In April 1920, Britain was given the mandate for Mesopotamia (Iraq).1
  • The British administration replaced Ottoman officials with British ones, introduced new land laws and taxed burials at the great cemetery of Najaf.1
  • Ayatollah Muhammad Taqi al-Shirazi issued a fatwa declaring service in the British administration unlawful.1
02

Who were the key people?

  • Ayatollah Muhammad Taqi al-Shirazi

    Senior Shia cleric who opposed British rule.

  • Sheikh Shaalan Abu al-Jun

    Tribal leader whose rescue sparked the fighting at Rumaitha.

  • Iraqi officers of the former Ottoman army

    Who joined the protests.

  • Winston Churchill

    British War Secretary, who sent reinforcements and aircraft.

03

What happened?

In May 1920, mass meetings and demonstrations began in Baghdad, at both Sunni and Shia mosques.1

  • Representatives presented the case for independence; the British civil commissioner dismissed their demands.1
  • The revolt spread to the middle and lower Euphrates, beginning with the Dhuwalim tribe at Rumaitha.1
  • Rebels attacked the Hilla–Baghdad railway and sank a British gunboat on the Euphrates.1
  • Britain sent reinforcements and Royal Air Force squadrons, which bombed rebel areas.1
  • By October 1920, Najaf and Karbala had surrendered.1

04

What happened next?

  • Deaths: between 2,050 and 10,000 Iraqis and about 1,000 British and Indian soldiers died.1
  • A king: at the Cairo Conference in 1921, Britain decided to rule indirectly, and made Faisal king of Iraq.1
05

What do others say?

At the time

British officials saw tribal unrest stirred by agitators; Iraqis saw a fight for independence.1

Debates since

  • National revolution? Some historians stress national unity; others note that some tribes sided with the British, who rewarded them.12
  • Violence on both sides: rebels killed British and Indian soldiers and officials; British forces burned villages and bombed from the air, killing thousands of Iraqis.1
06

The evidence

  • British military and RAF records.1
  • Iraqi documents and memoirs.
07

How is it remembered?

  • The revolution of 1920 ("Thawrat al-Ishrin") is celebrated in Iraq as a national struggle.
08

Why does it matter?

The 1920 revolution is seen in Iraq as the founding moment of national resistance.

  • It brought Sunni and Shia, townspeople and tribes together.1

Questions to think about

  1. Why did Iraqis see the mandate as colonial rule?
  2. How did Sunni and Shia communities co-operate?
  3. How did air power change colonial warfare?
  4. Why did Britain switch to indirect rule?
09

What other events are like this?

  • 1925The Great Syrian Revolt — Druze villagers, Damascus craftsmen and peasants from across Syria rose against French rule. France answered by shelling Damascus.
  • 1919Egypt's 1919 Revolution — When Britain exiled the nationalist leader Saad Zaghlul, Egyptians of every class and religion rose up in strikes and demonstrations — and women marched in public for the first time.
  • 1936The Palestinian General Strike and Revolt — Palestinian Arabs began a six-month general strike against British rule and Jewish immigration, which became a three-year revolt. It was crushed with great force, and violence struck Arabs, Jews and Britons alike.
