The Lomé Market Women's Revolt

When the French arrested two men who had spoken out against new taxes, thousands of market women marched on the prison, singing and dancing. Within hours the prisoners were free. The reprisals that followed were brutal.

When
24 – 25 January 1933
Where
Lomé, French Togoland (now Togo)
Facts checked
2026-11-16

See it on the map →

01

What is the background to this event?

After Germany's defeat in the First World War, its colony of Togo was divided between France and Britain. Lomé came under French rule.1

  • During the Great Depression, the French raised taxes, hitting the poorest hardest.1
  • Women ran Lomé's markets. The administration planned new market taxes and a licence to trade.1
  • A group called the Duawo acted as a go-between for the people and the official Council of Notables. In December 1932 it warned Paris that revolt was possible.1

03

What happened?

On 24 January 1933, the two Duawo leaders were arrested and sentenced to 15 days in prison. Within an hour, market women converged on the prison.1

  • Between 3,000 and 5,000 people gathered, about 80 per cent of them women.1
  • Singing and dancing, they insulted and threatened the French authorities and their African allies.1
  • At about 5pm, the prisoners were released.1
  • Protesters attacked the houses of notables, and stones broke the windows of the governor's palace; police fired warning shots.1
  • On 25 January, the railway station was attacked and trains looted.1

Reprisals

That day, 174 colonial soldiers arrived from Côte d'Ivoire. Rapes of girls aged 13 and 14 were recorded. On 3 February, one soldier shot dead twelve Togolese and wounded three; the governor called it a fit of madness.1 Whole villages and neighbourhoods were fined and sentenced to forced labour.1

04

What happened next?

  • The taxes were partly withdrawn.
  • The women held a ceremony to cleanse the markets.1
  • Independence: the revolt is often seen as the start of the independence movement. Togo became independent in 1960.1
05

What do others say?

At the time

The French blamed agitators among educated men; the women's own role was played down.2

Debates since

  • Who led? Historians such as Benjamin Lawrance stress the women's initiative and the role of religion and ritual.2
  • Violence: protesters damaged property and looted trains; the killings and rapes were carried out by colonial troops.1
06

The evidence

  • French colonial reports and court records.
  • Petitions sent to the League of Nations, which oversaw the territory.
07

How is it remembered?

  • The revolt is remembered in Togo as a founding moment of national resistance.
08

Why does it matter?

The revolt was the first open challenge to French rule in Lomé, and it was led by women.1

  • It belongs with other women's tax revolts in West Africa, such as Aba (1929) and Abeokuta (1947).
  • The reprisals show the violence that upheld colonial rule.

Questions to think about

  1. Why were market women so powerful in Lomé?
  2. Why did singing and dancing frighten the authorities?
  3. Was the punishment of whole villages just?
  4. Why are women's revolts often left out of the story of independence?
09

What other events are like this?

  • 1929The Women's War (Ogu Umunwanyi) — Tens of thousands of Igbo and Ibibio women challenged British colonial rule, using their own traditions of collective protest.
  • 1947The Abeokuta Women's Revolt — Market women taxed without representation camped outside their king's palace, singing songs mocking him, until he gave up his throne. Their leader was Funmilayo Ransome-Kuti.
  • 1949The Women's March on Grand-Bassam — When French authorities jailed leaders of the anti-colonial movement without trial, hundreds of women walked from Abidjan to the prison at Grand-Bassam to demand their release.
