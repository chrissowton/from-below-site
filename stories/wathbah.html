Al-Wathbah: The Baghdad Uprising of 1948

When Iraq's government signed a new treaty with Britain, students, workers and the urban poor of Baghdad rose up — 'the leap' — and forced it to back down.

When
January 1948
Where
Baghdad, Iraq

See it on the map →

01

What is the background to this event?

Iraq was created under British control after the First World War and became formally independent in 1932, under a monarchy. But the Anglo-Iraqi Treaty of 1930 kept British air bases in the country and tied Iraq closely to British interests; many Iraqis saw it as making Iraq a British protectorate in all but name.1

  • In 1947, the government began secret negotiations with Britain to renew the treaty in a revised form.1
  • Iraqi society was deeply unequal. Large landowners dominated the countryside, and Baghdad's outskirts were crowded with poor migrants from the south.1
  • After the Second World War, prices rose sharply and the purchasing power of workers was at a historic low; the winter of 1947–48 brought bread shortages.1
  • A growing student population and new political parties, including the Iraqi Communist Party and nationalist parties, were challenging the old elite.1
02

Who were the key people?

  • The students

    Students of the Law College and other colleges in Baghdad, who began the protests.

  • The workers and the urban poor

    Including railway workers and residents of Baghdad's poorest districts, who joined in large numbers.

  • The Iraqi Communist Party

    Which organised many of the protests; its leader, Yusuf Salman Yusuf ('Fahd'), was later executed in 1949.

  • Salih Jabr

    The prime minister, the first Shia to hold the post, who signed the new treaty and was forced to resign.

  • The Regent, 'Abd al-Ilah

    Who ruled on behalf of the young King Faisal II, and disavowed the treaty.

03

What happened?

Protests began in early January 1948, after the foreign minister made statements about the treaty. On 4 January, secondary school students marched to the Law College, where students joined them. Police broke up the protest with clubs and gunfire; many students were wounded and 39 were arrested, and the Law College was closed. On 6 January, students from all the colleges went on strike, and the authorities released the arrested students.1

On 16 January, it was announced that the government had signed a new treaty with Britain at Portsmouth. The protests exploded:1

  • Students launched a strike and demonstrations, supported by the Communist Party and nationalist parties.
  • Workers, especially railway workers, and the urban poor joined the students, and the protests spread across Baghdad.
  • The Regent announced that he would not ratify a treaty that did not meet the wishes of the people.

When the prime minister returned from London on 26 January, he called for calm. On 27 January, a large crowd tried to cross the Ma'mun Bridge into western Baghdad to join the students and the railway workers. Police fired directly into the crowd with machine guns. It is estimated that 300 to 400 demonstrators were killed in the uprising as a whole.1 Salih Jabr resigned and left the country, and the treaty was abandoned.1

04

What happened next?

  • Unrest continues: strikes and protests continued; in April, peasants rose in the village of Arbat. In May 1948, the government declared martial law after the outbreak of war in Palestine, ending the demonstrations.
  • Repression: the Communist Party was severely repressed, and its leader Fahd and others were executed in 1949.
  • More uprisings: further mass protests, known as the intifada, followed in 1952 and 1956.
  • Revolution: on 14 July 1958, army officers overthrew the monarchy; the king, the regent and Nuri al-Said were killed. The revolution was followed by decades of coups and dictatorship.
05

What do others say?

At the time

The government blamed communists and agitators, and claimed that the treaty was in Iraq's interest. The protesters saw it as a betrayal of Iraq's independence.

Historians' debates

  • Treaty or hunger? Hanna Batatu and others stress that the uprising was about poverty, inequality and the price of bread as much as about the treaty.
  • The role of the communists: historians debate how far the Communist Party led the uprising, and how far it rode a wave of spontaneous anger.
  • The 1958 revolution: historians debate whether the uprisings of 1948–1956 opened the way for democracy or for the military dictatorships that followed the 1958 revolution.
06

The evidence

  • British diplomatic reports from Baghdad.
  • Iraqi newspapers and the leaflets of the Communist Party and other organisations.
  • The poetry of Muhammad Mahdi al-Jawahiri, whose brother was killed in the uprising.
07

How is it remembered?

  • The poet Muhammad Mahdi al-Jawahiri's elegy for his brother Ja'far, killed in the uprising, became one of the most famous poems of modern Iraq.
  • Al-Wathbah has been commemorated by the Iraqi left and invoked by later protest movements.
08

Why does it matter?

Al-Wathbah ("the leap") was one of the largest popular uprisings in modern Iraqi history, and it succeeded in its immediate aim: the treaty with Britain was abandoned. It brought together students, workers and the urban poor in a movement against both foreign domination and social injustice.

  • It showed the strength of the new urban classes and political movements that would transform Iraq.
  • It weakened the monarchy and its British alliance, paving the way for the revolution of 1958.
  • It remains an important memory for the Iraqi left and for later protest movements, such as the protests of 2019.

09

What other events are like this?

  • 1919Egypt's 1919 Revolution — When Britain exiled the nationalist leader Saad Zaghlul, Egyptians of every class and religion rose up in strikes and demonstrations — and women marched in public for the first time.
  • 1905Iran's Constitutional Revolution — Merchants, clerics, craftsmen and radicals forced the Shah to grant Iran a constitution and an elected parliament — the first in the Middle East.
  • 1964Sudan's October Revolution — When police killed a student at a university debate about the war in the south, Sudanese professionals, workers and students launched a general strike that brought down a military dictatorship in days.
