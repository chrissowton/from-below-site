The Women's March on Grand-Bassam

When French authorities jailed leaders of the anti-colonial movement without trial, hundreds of women walked from Abidjan to the prison at Grand-Bassam to demand their release.

When
22 – 24 December 1949
Where
Grand-Bassam, Côte d'Ivoire
Facts checked
2026-11-16

See it on the map →

01

What is the background to this event?

After the Second World War, the African Democratic Rally (RDA), led in Côte d'Ivoire by Félix Houphouët-Boigny, campaigned against forced labour and for political rights.1

  • The French colonial authorities tried to break the party. In 1949, many of its leaders were arrested and held in the prison at Grand-Bassam without trial.1
  • The prisoners began a hunger strike.1
  • Women had been active in the RDA, organising and raising funds.1
02

Who were the key people?

  • Marie Koré

    A leader of the march, arrested and imprisoned. She died in 1953.

  • Anne-Marie Raggi

    A leader of the RDA women's movement.

  • Félix Houphouët-Boigny

    Leader of the RDA, later first president of Côte d'Ivoire.

  • Henriette Diabaté

    A historian, later a politician, who wrote the history of the march.

03

What happened?

From 22 December 1949, women set out on foot from Abidjan to Grand-Bassam, a distance of about 50 km (30 miles). To avoid being stopped, they travelled in small groups.1

  • Officially, about 500 women took part; other estimates put the number at 2,000.1
  • On 24 December, they gathered outside the prison and demanded the release of the prisoners.1
  • French soldiers dispersed them with clubs, water and tear gas. About 40 women were injured.1
  • Several women were arrested and prosecuted. The prisoners were not released.1

04

What happened next?

  • Repression: clashes between RDA supporters and the authorities continued into 1950, and people were killed in incidents across the colony, notably at Dimbokro in January 1950.
  • Compromise: in 1950, Houphouët-Boigny broke the RDA's link with the French Communist Party and began to work with France.
  • Independence: Côte d'Ivoire became independent in 1960.
05

What do others say?

At the time

The colonial authorities saw the march as communist agitation. The RDA celebrated the women's courage.1

Debates since

  • Numbers: the figure of 500 comes from official sources; supporters give higher figures.1
  • Recognition: historians such as Henriette Diabaté argued that the women's role in independence had been underestimated.2
06

The evidence

  • Colonial police and court records.
  • Interviews with participants collected by Henriette Diabaté.2
07

How is it remembered?

  • A monument to the march was unveiled in Grand-Bassam in 1999.1
  • The colonial town of Grand-Bassam is a UNESCO World Heritage Site.
08

Why does it matter?

The march is one of the best-known examples of women's anti-colonial action in West Africa.

  • It showed women acting as political leaders, not only as supporters.
  • It was entirely non-violent.

What other events are like this?

  • 1929The Women's War (Ogu Umunwanyi) — Tens of thousands of Igbo and Ibibio women challenged British colonial rule, using their own traditions of collective protest.
  • 1947The Abeokuta Women's Revolt — Market women taxed without representation camped outside their king's palace, singing songs mocking him, until he gave up his throne. Their leader was Funmilayo Ransome-Kuti.
  • 1956The Women's March on the Union Buildings — Twenty thousand women of every race marched on the seat of South Africa's government to reject the pass laws — and stood for thirty minutes in silence.
