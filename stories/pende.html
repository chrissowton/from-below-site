The Pende Revolt

When palm-oil prices collapsed and taxes rose, company agents abused Pende women and lashed the men. The revolt that followed was crushed by colonial troops, and several hundred Pende were killed.

When
May – September 1931
Where
Kwilu, Belgian Congo (now Democratic Republic of the Congo)
Facts checked
2026-11-16

See it on the map →

01

What is the background to this event?

In the Kwilu region, African workers cut palm fruit for the Huileries du Congo Belge (HCB), a subsidiary of the British firm Lever Brothers, later Unilever.1

  • When the Great Depression struck, the price paid for palm fruit was cut from 2.5 francs to 1 franc, while the head tax was raised.1
  • Recruiters pressed men to work for the company, often using violence.1

In their own words

03

What happened?

On 14 May 1931, two company agents, Burnotte and Vanhombeek, locked up Pende women in a village and raped them. At Kilamba, 47 men were lashed.1

  • Unrest spread. Some villages refused to pay tax and followed a prophetic movement that promised the departure of whites.1
  • On 8 June 1931, Matemo killed the administrator Maximilien Balot, who had come to collect taxes.1
  • A party led by the official Weekx was attacked with arrows.1
  • The colonial government sent troops. The Force Publique occupied the region, burned villages and shot villagers. The Belgian newspaper Le Soir reported the use of machine guns.1
  • On 6 September, 400 villagers were arrested, and on 9 September 14 people were killed.1

Estimates of the total number of Pende killed vary, but run to several hundred. One European, Balot, was killed.13

04

What happened next?

  • Inquiry: the violence caused some debate in Belgium, but the agents responsible were not seriously punished.
  • Forced labour continued in the Congo until the end of colonial rule.
  • Kwilu later became a centre of the 1964 Kwilu rebellion led by Pierre Mulele.
05

Why does it matter?

The Pende revolt shows how a global economic crisis, company power and colonial violence combined to devastate rural communities.

  • It links a famous European brand to colonial forced labour.
  • It shows the role of sexual violence in colonial rule.
  • Forced labour continued in the Congo until the end of colonial rule, and the agents responsible were never seriously punished.

What do others say?How it was seen at the time, and where historians disagree

At the time

Colonial officials described a savage uprising; some Belgian critics and newspapers questioned the brutality of the repression.1

Debates since

  • The death toll: records are poor, and estimates vary.3
  • Violence: the killing of Balot was the only European death. The repression killed far more Pende, including people who had taken no part.1
  • Company responsibility: Jules Marchal argued that the HCB system amounted to forced labour.3
How do we know?The evidence, and a source from the time
  • Colonial reports and inquiry records in Belgian archives.
  • Belgian newspaper reports, such as Le Soir.
  • Pende oral history.
How is it remembered?Memorials, anniversaries and arguments over memory
  • The revolt is remembered in Kwilu, but little known in Belgium.
  • Debates about restitution of Pende objects in Belgian museums have revived interest.
