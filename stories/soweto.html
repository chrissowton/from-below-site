Civil rights & anti-racism

The Soweto Uprising

Thousands of Black schoolchildren marched against being taught in Afrikaans. Police opened fire, and the uprising spread across the country.

When
16 June 1976
Where
Soweto, Johannesburg, South Africa

See it on the map →

01

What is the background to this event?

Under apartheid, Black South Africans were subjected to "Bantu Education", a separate and deliberately inferior school system designed, in the words of its architect Hendrik Verwoerd, to prepare them for a limited place in the economy. Schools in townships such as Soweto were overcrowded and under-resourced.

In 1974 the government issued the Afrikaans Medium Decree, which required Black schools to use Afrikaans and English in equal measure as languages of instruction:

  • From 1 January 1975, Afrikaans was to be used for mathematics, arithmetic and social studies from standard five (seventh grade).
  • Many teachers and pupils spoke little Afrikaans, and many regarded it as the language of the oppressor.

Students, influenced by the Black Consciousness ideas of Steve Biko and the South African Students' Organisation, began to organise. On 13 June 1976, Tsietsi Mashinini of Morris Isaacson High School proposed a meeting at which students formed an Action Committee — later the Soweto Students' Representative Council — and planned a peaceful march for 16 June.

02

Who were the key people?

  • Tsietsi Mashinini

    A student leader from Morris Isaacson High School who led students on the march. He was forced into exile and died in Guinea in 1990.

  • Hector Pieterson

    A twelve-year-old boy shot dead by police on 16 June, whose photograph became a worldwide symbol of the uprising.

  • Hastings Ndlovu

    A fifteen-year-old who was among the first students shot dead that morning.

  • Antoinette Sithole (née Pieterson)

    Hector's sister, seen running beside him in the photograph, who has spent her life telling the story.

  • Mbuyisa Makhubo

    The eighteen-year-old student who carried Hector. He was later forced to leave South Africa and disappeared.

  • Sam Nzima

    The photographer for The World newspaper who took the photograph.

  • Steve Biko

    Leader of the Black Consciousness Movement, whose ideas inspired many of the students. He died in police custody in September 1977.

In their own words

03

What happened?

On the morning of 16 June 1976, students from schools across Soweto began to march towards Orlando Stadium, where they planned to gather before delivering a memorandum of grievances to the Department of Bantu Education.1 Estimates of the number of students range from about 3,000 to 20,000.1

The students sang and carried placards with slogans including "Down with Afrikaans", "Viva Azania" and "If we must do Afrikaans, Vorster must do Zulu".1 Police blocked their route. After a confrontation in which stones were thrown, the police opened fire on the crowd.1

  • Among the first killed were Hastings Ndlovu, aged 15, and Hector Pieterson, aged 12, shot near Orlando West High School.1
  • Sam Nzima photographed Mbuyisa Makhubo carrying the dying Hector, with Hector's sister Antoinette alongside. The image was published around the world.
  • The official government figure initially claimed that 23 people had died on the first day; the number usually given for those killed in the uprising is 176, with many sources suggesting it was considerably higher.1

On 17 June, 1,500 heavily armed police officers were deployed to Soweto.1 The unrest spread to other townships and cities across South Africa in the following months, and hundreds of people were arrested, many of them tortured in detention.

04

What happened next?

  • A generation radicalised: thousands of young people left South Africa to join the liberation movements in exile, especially the African National Congress's armed wing, Umkhonto we Sizwe.
  • Repression: in 1977 the government banned Black Consciousness organisations, and Steve Biko died after being beaten in police custody.
  • International pressure: the uprising and its suppression intensified international campaigns for sanctions, boycotts and divestment.
  • Remembrance: after the end of apartheid and the first democratic elections in 1994, 16 June became a public holiday, Youth Day. The Hector Pieterson Memorial and Museum opened in Soweto in 2002.
05

What do others say?

At the time

The government blamed the uprising on agitators and communists, and claimed that police had acted in self-defence after being attacked. It initially gave a much lower death toll than independent observers.

Historians' debates

  • The death toll: the figure of 176 is widely cited for the first days, but many historians believe the true number across the months of unrest was much higher.
  • Who led it? Historians debate the influence of the Black Consciousness Movement, the ANC and local student organisations.
  • Language or more? Afrikaans was the trigger, but historians stress deeper causes such as overcrowded schools, unemployment and political repression.
  • Violence: some students attacked government buildings and individuals seen as collaborators; historians differ on how to interpret this.
06

The evidence

  • Photographs, especially Sam Nzima's image of Hector Pieterson.
  • The Cillié Commission of Inquiry (1980), set up by the government.
  • The Truth and Reconciliation Commission's hearings in the 1990s.
  • Oral testimonies from former students, collected by historians such as Sifiso Ndlovu.

07

How is it remembered?

  • 16 June is Youth Day, a public holiday in South Africa.
  • The Hector Pieterson Memorial and Museum opened near the site where he was shot.
  • Sam Nzima's photograph is one of the most widely reproduced images of the anti-apartheid struggle.
08

Why does it matter?

The Soweto uprising was a turning point in the struggle against apartheid, and it was led by schoolchildren. It shows how young people, refusing the future that had been planned for them, can transform a political situation that their elders had come to regard as unchangeable.

  • It brought a new generation into the liberation struggle.
  • It focused world attention on apartheid's brutality, increasing international isolation of the regime.
  • Internationally, 16 June is observed as the Day of the African Child.

What other events are like this?

  • 1965Bloody Sunday at the Edmund Pettus Bridge — Around six hundred marchers set out from Selma to demand the right to vote. On the Edmund Pettus Bridge, state troopers attacked them with clubs and tear gas.
  • 1819The Peterloo Massacre — Tens of thousands of working people gathered peacefully to demand the vote. Within ten minutes, cavalry had cleared the field with sabres.
  • 1969The Stonewall Uprising — A routine police raid on a gay bar met resistance, and several nights of protest gave rise to the modern LGBTQ+ liberation movement.
