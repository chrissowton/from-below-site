LGBTQ+ liberation

The Scientific-Humanitarian Committee

Founded in Berlin in 1897, the world's first organisation for the rights of gay, lesbian and trans people petitioned parliament for decades to abolish the law against sex between men — until the Nazis destroyed its home.

When
1897 – 1933
Where
Berlin, Germany
Facts checked

01

What is the background to this event?

Paragraph 175 of the German Criminal Code, in force since 1871, made sexual acts between men a crime.1

  • In 1895, the trials of Oscar Wilde in Britain showed the dangers faced by gay men across Europe.
  • Magnus Hirschfeld, a Jewish doctor in Berlin, believed that science could show homosexuality was natural, not a sickness or a sin.1
02

Who were the key people?

  • Magnus Hirschfeld

    A physician and sexologist who founded the Committee and later the Institute for Sexual Science.

  • Max Spohr

    A publisher and founding member.

  • Kurt Hiller

    A writer who took over as chair in 1929.

  • August Bebel

    Leader of the Social Democratic Party, among the first to sign the petition.

  • Ordinary members

    Up to about 700 people, in branches in about 25 cities in Germany, Austria and the Netherlands.

In their own words

03

What happened?

Hirschfeld founded the Scientific-Humanitarian Committee (WhK) in Berlin in May 1897, with the publisher Max Spohr, the lawyer Eduard Oberg and the writer Franz Joseph von Bülow. It is regarded as the first LGBT rights organisation in history.1

  • Its motto was "Through science to justice".1
  • It gathered more than 6,000 signatures on a petition to repeal Paragraph 175. Signatories included August Bebel, Albert Einstein, Hermann Hesse, Thomas Mann, Rainer Maria Rilke and Leo Tolstoy.1
  • The petition was submitted to the Reichstag in 1898, 1922 and 1925, but parliament never repealed the law.1
  • The Committee also helped defendants in criminal trials.1
  • Its petition did not call for all acts to be legal — it supported keeping the law for sex with under-16s.1
  • From 1919 it was based at Hirschfeld's Institute for Sexual Science, which also offered advice and early medical help to transgender people.2

On 6 May 1933, Nazi students raided the Institute and burned its library in a public book burning. The Committee was dissolved.12

04

What happened next?

  • Persecution: under the Nazis, Paragraph 175 was made harsher. Tens of thousands of men were convicted and thousands sent to concentration camps, where many died.
  • Exile: Hirschfeld, abroad when the Institute was destroyed, died in exile in Nice in 1935.
  • The law: West Germany kept the Nazi version of Paragraph 175 until 1969; it was finally abolished in 1994.1
05

What do others say?

At the time

Conservatives and churches defended Paragraph 175. Within the gay movement, some, such as Adolf Brand, rejected Hirschfeld's idea of a "third sex" and split away in 1906.1

Debates since

  • Science: some historians note that Hirschfeld's ideas also fed into medical theories that treated homosexuality as a condition.2
  • Limits: the petition's support for keeping some offences is a reminder that early campaigns made compromises.1

What is it?
Petition
Made by, and when
Scientific-Humanitarian Committee, Berlin, 1926

A printed petition to the German parliament calling §175, the law against sex between men, an injustice. The Committee had first petitioned in 1897 and gathered thousands of signatures, including from well-known writers, scientists and politicians.

07

How is it remembered?

  • A memorial stele stands on the site of the Institute in Berlin.
  • The Magnus Hirschfeld Society and Germany's federal Magnus Hirschfeld Foundation continue research and education.
08

Why does it matter?

The Committee was the first organised campaign for the rights of sexual minorities, decades before Stonewall.

  • It used petitions, science and public education.
  • Its destruction shows how quickly rights can be lost.

Questions to think about

  1. Why did the Committee rely on science to make its case?
  2. Why were famous signatures so important to the petition?
  3. Why did the Nazis target the Institute for Sexual Science?
  4. What can the history of the Committee teach campaigns today?

What other events are like this?

  • 1969The Stonewall Uprising — A routine police raid on a gay bar met resistance, and several nights of protest gave rise to the modern LGBTQ+ liberation movement.
  • 1978The First Sydney Mardi Gras — A night-time street party for gay rights ended with police violence and 53 arrests. A newspaper printed the names of those arrested — and the protest grew into one of the world's largest pride events.
  • 2018Striking Down Section 377 — A colonial-era law made gay sex a crime in India for 158 years. After a long campaign by activists, lawyers and ordinary queer Indians, the Supreme Court struck it down in 2018.
