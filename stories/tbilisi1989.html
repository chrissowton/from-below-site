9 April 1989: The Tbilisi Massacre

Thousands of Georgians gathered peacefully in the heart of Tbilisi to demand independence. Before dawn, Soviet soldiers attacked them with spades and gas, killing 21 people, most of them young women.

When
4 – 9 April 1989
Where
Rustaveli Avenue, Tbilisi, Georgia

01

What is the background to this event?

Georgia had been briefly independent from 1918 to 1921, before the Red Army invaded and made it part of the Soviet Union.

  • Under Mikhail Gorbachev's policies of openness (glasnost) and reform, national movements grew across the Soviet Union in the late 1980s.
  • In Georgia, an independence movement led by former dissidents such as Merab Kostava and Zviad Gamsakhurdia organised protests from 1988.1
  • In March 1989, thousands of Abkhaz, a minority people in north-west Georgia, demanded separation from Georgia. Georgian nationalists believed Moscow was encouraging this to weaken their movement.1

In their own words

03

What happened?

From 4 April 1989, tens of thousands of Georgians gathered before the House of Government on Rustaveli Avenue. Led by the Independence Committee, they held a peaceful demonstration and hunger strikes, demanding the restoration of Georgian independence and the punishment of Abkhaz secessionists.1

  • The Georgian Communist leader asked Moscow to send troops.1
  • On the evening of 8 April, as Soviet tanks appeared, Patriarch Ilia II urged the demonstrators to leave. They refused.1
  • At 3.45 a.m. on 9 April, Soviet troops surrounded the area and advanced on the crowd with batons and sharpened army spades.1
  • Soldiers beat people, including a 16-year-old girl who was chased and beaten to death. CN and CS gas were also used, causing vomiting and breathing problems.1

Twenty-one people were killed, 17 of them women, and hundreds were injured.1 General Rodionov later claimed that his soldiers had been attacked with stones and chains.1

04

What happened next?

  • Investigation: a commission of the Soviet Congress of People's Deputies, led by Anatoly Sobchak, investigated and found the army responsible, contradicting official claims.1
  • Independence: the massacre turned many Georgians against Soviet rule. On 9 April 1991, exactly two years later, Georgia declared independence.
  • Conflict: independence was followed by civil war and by wars in Abkhazia and South Ossetia, in which thousands were killed and hundreds of thousands displaced on all sides.
05

What do others say?

At the time

Soviet officials blamed "extremists" and claimed troops acted in self-defence.1 Georgians saw it as a massacre of peaceful people.

Historians' debates

  • Who gave the order? Debate continues over how far Gorbachev and the Moscow leadership were responsible.
  • Nationalism and minorities: historians note that the protest also demanded action against Abkhaz separatism; Georgian nationalism under Gamsakhurdia later alienated minorities, contributing to the wars of the 1990s.
  • Violence: the Sobchak commission found that the demonstration was peaceful and that the army's response was disproportionate.1

How is it remembered?

  • 9 April is National Unity Day, a public holiday in Georgia.1
  • A memorial stands in front of the Parliament on Rustaveli Avenue.
08

Why does it matter?

The 9 April massacre was a turning point in the collapse of the Soviet Union. It showed that Gorbachev's reforms had limits, and pushed Georgia towards independence.

  • It shows how violence against peaceful protest can strengthen a movement.
  • It also shows how nationalist movements can become entangled in conflicts with minorities.
