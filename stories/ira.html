The Abolitionist Movement in Mauritania

Mauritania was the last country in the world to abolish slavery, in 1981. A movement led by Biram Dah Abeid, himself descended from enslaved people, has used sit-ins, hunger strikes and protests to free people still held in servitude.

01

What is the background to this event?

In Mauritania, a caste system long held people of African descent, known as Haratine, in hereditary slavery to Arab-Berber (Beydane) masters.12

  • Slavery was abolished in 1981, but only made a crime in 2007.2
  • Estimates of the number of people still enslaved range widely, from about 90,000 to 600,000.1
  • Biram Dah Abeid argues that slaves are bound to their masters by poverty, illiteracy and a misreading of Islam.2

In their own words

03

What happened?

Biram Dah Abeid founded the Initiative for the Resurgence of the Abolitionist Movement (IRA) in 2008.1

  • IRA held sit-ins outside the justice ministry, hunger strikes and marches.1
  • When police released slave owners, IRA gathered outside the homes of alleged owners and demanded arrests.1
  • In 2010–11, one protest led to the first imprisonment of a slave owner by Mauritanian police. Abeid himself was jailed for three months.1
  • By 2014, IRA said it had helped put about twenty slave owners in jail, and that many more enslaved people had been freed by fearful owners.1
  • In 2012, Abeid publicly burned books of Islamic jurisprudence that he said were used to justify slavery. He was arrested, and some religious leaders called for his execution.2

04

What happened next?

  • Prizes and prison: Abeid received the UN Human Rights Prize in 2013. He was imprisoned again in 2014–16 and 2018.
  • Elections: he stood for president in 2014, 2019 and 2024, coming second in 2019 and 2024.
  • Registration: the government long refused to register IRA as an organisation.1
05

What do others say?

At the time

The government says slavery no longer exists, only its "vestiges", and accuses IRA of extremism and of dividing the nation. IRA and international human rights groups say slavery persists.12

Debates since

  • The book burning: supporters saw it as an attack on texts used to justify slavery; critics, including some anti-slavery activists, saw it as an offence to religion.2
  • Numbers: the wide range of estimates reflects disputes over what counts as slavery.1
  • Non-violence: IRA describes its methods as non-violent; some of its protests have ended in clashes with police.

How is it remembered?

  • IRA continues to campaign, and Abeid remains a leading opposition figure.
08

Why does it matter?

IRA's campaign shows that slavery is not only a thing of the past, and that people descended from enslaved people are leading the fight against it.

  • It links abolition to racial and caste discrimination.
