The Malagasy Uprising of 1947

Armed mostly with spears, Malagasy nationalists rose against French colonial rule. The repression killed tens of thousands — and France kept silent about it for more than half a century.

When
29 March 1947 – 1949
Where
Eastern Madagascar
Facts checked
2026-11-16

01

What is the background to this event?

France conquered Madagascar in 1895–96, abolished the Merina monarchy and made the island a colony. Colonial rule brought forced labour, heavy taxes, the seizure of land for French settlers and companies, and the suppression of Malagasy political life.

  • During the Second World War, Madagascar was occupied by British forces and then handed to the Free French. Requisitions of rice and labour caused great hardship.
  • After the war, Malagasy people hoped for change. Two Malagasy doctors, Joseph Raseta and Joseph Ravoahangy, were elected to the French National Assembly in Paris.1
  • In 1946, with the writer Jacques Rabemananjara, they founded the Democratic Movement for Malagasy Renewal (MDRM), which sought independence by peaceful, legal means within the French community of nations.1
  • The Malagasy deputies submitted a bill for independence to the French parliament; it was rejected.1

Secret nationalist societies, such as Panama and Jiny, prepared for armed struggle.1 As tension grew, on 27 March 1947 the three MDRM deputies issued a statement urging the public "to maintain absolute calm and coolness".1

In their own words

03

What happened?

On the night of 29 March 1947, Malagasy nationalists launched coordinated surprise attacks, armed mainly with spears, against military bases and French-owned plantations in the east of the island, including the town of Moramanga.1 A unit of Malagasy colonial troops at Diego-Suarez mutinied and joined the rebels.1

The rising spread across the eastern part of the island, and insurgents took control of large areas of forest and countryside. The nationalists killed around 550 French nationals and some 1,900 Malagasy who served with the French forces.1

From May 1947, the French counter-attacked with reinforcements, including colonial troops from Senegal and elsewhere in Africa:1

  • French forces carried out mass executions, torture, rape, the burning of entire villages and collective punishment.
  • Some Malagasy prisoners were thrown alive from aeroplanes.
  • Tens of thousands of people fled into the forests, where many died of hunger and disease.

Although the MDRM leadership had called for calm, the French authorities blamed the party for the uprising. Its deputies were arrested and stripped of their parliamentary immunity.1 By August 1948, most of the nationalist leaders had been killed or captured, and the rising had largely been crushed by December 1948; the last armed resistance was defeated in 1949.1 Estimates of the number of Malagasy people who died range from around 11,000 to more than 100,000.1

04

What happened next?

  • Trials: in 1948 the MDRM leaders were tried in Antananarivo in a trial widely criticised as unfair. Several were sentenced to death, though the sentences were commuted.
  • A lost generation: a generation of Malagasy political leaders and educated people was killed or imprisoned, creating difficulties for the country after independence.
  • Independence: Madagascar became independent in 1960. The imprisoned deputies had been amnestied in 1958.
  • Recognition: in 2005, on a visit to Madagascar, French President Jacques Chirac called the repression "unacceptable".
05

What do others say?

At the time

The French authorities presented the uprising as a plot by the MDRM, and the repression as the restoration of order. The MDRM leaders denied organising the rising and insisted that they had always sought independence by legal means.

Historians' debates

  • How many died? Early French estimates of around 89,000 deaths were later revised by some historians to fewer than 30,000, much of it from famine and disease in the forests. The true figure is unknown.
  • Who organised the rising? Most historians now agree that the secret societies, not the MDRM leadership, planned the attacks, and that the trial of the deputies was politically motivated.
  • Memory: anthropologist Jennifer Cole has studied how Malagasy communities remember — and sometimes choose not to remember — the violence of 1947.
06

The evidence

  • French military and colonial archives, many long classified.
  • The records of the 1948 trial of the MDRM leaders.
  • Oral testimonies collected by historians and anthropologists in the affected regions.
07

How is it remembered?

  • Since 1967, 29 March has been a national day of commemoration in Madagascar.
  • A museum dedicated to the uprising was opened at Moramanga in 2012, and memorials stand across the island.
  • The uprising has been the subject of Malagasy literature and film, including Raymond Rajaonarivelo's Tabataba (1988).
08

Why does it matter?

The Malagasy Uprising was one of the largest anti-colonial revolts in Africa after the Second World War, and its repression one of the bloodiest episodes of French colonial history. At a time when France was celebrating its own liberation, it used extreme violence to keep its colonies.

  • It shows how the refusal of peaceful paths to independence could lead to armed revolt.
  • It is part of a series of colonial wars and massacres — Sétif, Indochina, Algeria, Cameroon — in which France fought to keep its empire.
  • Its long silencing raises questions about how former colonial powers remember their past.

What other events are like this?

  • 1945The Sétif and Guelma Massacres — On the day the Second World War ended in Europe, Algerians marched for independence in Sétif. The colonial response killed thousands and set the course towards war.
  • 1952The Mau Mau Uprising — An armed rebellion over land and freedom was met with mass detention and torture. Decades later, the British government paid compensation to survivors.
  • 1945The Battle of Surabaya — Weeks after Indonesia declared independence, the young people of Surabaya tore the blue from a Dutch flag and then fought British and Indian troops street by street — a defeat that became a national legend.
