The Hussite Revolution

After the reformer Jan Hus was burned at the stake, Czech townspeople and peasants rose against the Church and the Emperor. Radicals founded a town where property was held in common, and peasant armies defeated five crusades.

When
1415 – 1434
Where
Prague and Tábor, Bohemia (Czech Republic)

01

What is the background to this event?

In early fifteenth-century Bohemia, the Catholic Church was rich and powerful, and many people resented its wealth and corruption.

  • Jan Hus, a priest and teacher at the University of Prague, preached in Czech against Church corruption and the sale of indulgences.
  • In 1415, Hus was summoned to the Church Council of Constance under a promise of safe conduct, tried for heresy and burned at the stake.2
  • His followers, the Hussites, demanded that ordinary people receive communion with both bread and wine — the chalice became their symbol.2

In their own words

03

What happened?

On 30 July 1419, the radical priest Jan Želivský led a procession to Prague's New Town Hall, demanding the release of Hussite prisoners. When a stone was allegedly thrown at him from the building, the crowd stormed the hall and threw the judge, the mayor and several councillors from the windows, killing them — the first Defenestration of Prague.1

  • The Pope declared a crusade against the Hussites, and Emperor Sigismund led armies from across Europe into Bohemia.2
  • Radical Hussites founded the town of Tábor, where they briefly tried to abolish private property and hold goods in common, and elected their own military leaders.2
  • Led by Jan Žižka, peasant armies used wagon forts — rings of armoured carts — and early handguns to defeat cavalry charges.2
  • The Hussites defeated five crusades between 1420 and 1431.2

Both sides committed atrocities. Crusaders massacred Hussites, including at Kutná Hora, where Hussite prisoners were thrown into mine shafts. Hussite armies burned monasteries and killed monks and Catholic townspeople, and Catholic Germans were driven out of Prague.2 Žižka also crushed the most radical sect, the Adamites, burning many of them.

04

What happened next?

  • Division: the moderate Hussites (Utraquists) made peace with the Catholic Church and defeated the radical Taborites at the Battle of Lipany in 1434.2
  • The Compacts: the Compacts of Basel (1436) allowed Bohemians to receive communion in both kinds — a unique concession.
  • Legacy: the Hussite church survived, and the Unity of the Brethren grew from Hussite roots. A century later, Martin Luther declared, "We are all Hussites".
05

What do others say?

At the time

The Church saw the Hussites as heretics. The Hussites saw themselves as God's warriors, restoring true Christianity.

Historians' debates

  • Religion, class or nation? Marxist historians saw it as a social revolution; nationalist historians as a Czech national movement; others emphasise religion.
  • Violence: historians acknowledge atrocities by both crusaders and Hussites, and the persecution of radical sects by other Hussites.
  • Tábor's communism: the experiment in common property was short-lived; historians debate how radical it really was.

The evidence

  • Chronicles, including that of Lawrence of Březová.
  • Hussite songs, manifestos and the Four Articles of Prague.
  • The Jena Codex and other illustrated manuscripts.

07

How is it remembered?

  • 6 July, the anniversary of Hus's execution, is a public holiday in the Czech Republic.
  • A huge statue of Jan Žižka stands on Vítkov Hill in Prague.
08

Why does it matter?

The Hussite Revolution was the first great challenge to the medieval Church, a century before Luther. Townspeople and peasants defeated the armies of Europe.

  • It linked religious reform with social and national demands.
  • Tábor was an early experiment in radical equality.
  • It became a central part of Czech national identity.

What other events are like this?

  • 1525The German Peasants' War and the Twelve Articles — Tens of thousands of peasants rose against their lords, armed with the new ideas of the Reformation and a printed manifesto of rights. It was Europe's largest popular uprising before the French Revolution.
  • 1381The Peasants' Revolt — Tens of thousands of villagers and townspeople marched on London to demand an end to serfdom. For a few days, the government of England was at their mercy.
  • 1378The Revolt of the Ciompi — The wool workers of Florence, excluded from the guilds that ran the city, seized the government — and for a few weeks a wool-carder sat at the head of the republic.
