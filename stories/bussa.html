Bussa's Rebellion

On Easter Sunday 1816, enslaved people across south-east Barbados rose up — the largest slave revolt in the island's history, and the first of three great risings that shook British slavery.

Where

01

What is the background to this event?

Barbados was England's first great sugar colony. From the mid-seventeenth century, its plantations were worked by enslaved Africans and their descendants, who by the early nineteenth century made up the great majority of the population. Barbados was often described by planters as a model colony where slavery was "mild" and the enslaved content — a claim that the revolt would shatter.

  • In 1807 Britain abolished the slave trade, but not slavery itself. Abolitionists in Britain then campaigned for the registration of all enslaved people, to stop illegal trading and as a step towards better treatment.1
  • In November 1815, the Barbados House of Assembly discussed and rejected the Imperial Registry Bill. News of the debates — and of the hostility of the planters — spread among the enslaved population.1
  • Historians believe that many enslaved people understood the proposals in London as preparation for emancipation, and concluded that the planters were blocking their freedom.1

03

What happened?

Bussa and his collaborators — among them Washington Franklin, Nanny Grigg and the driver Jackey — planned the revolt for the night of Easter Sunday, 14 April 1816, when the planters would be relaxing and the enslaved had time off for the holiday.1

That night, the revolt broke out in the parish of St Philip and quickly spread:12

  • The rebels set fire to cane fields and plantation buildings. A large part of the island's sugar crop was destroyed.
  • The revolt spread to more than seventy plantations across the parishes of St Philip, Christ Church, St John and St George — around a fifth of the island.
  • The rebels largely avoided killing white people; historians record that only one white civilian was killed.

The colonial militia and the British army's West India Regiment were called out. On Tuesday 16 April, Bussa, King Wiltshire, Dick Bailey and Johnny led around four hundred rebels into battle against the militia at Bailey's plantation. Bussa was killed, and the rebels, who had few firearms, were defeated by the soldiers' superior firepower.1

The repression was savage. According to the historian Hilary Beckles, around 120 enslaved people were killed in the fighting or executed on the spot, 144 were executed after trial, and 132 were deported, first to Honduras and then to Sierra Leone.2

04

What happened next?

  • A wave of revolts: Bussa's rebellion was followed by the Demerara rebellion in British Guiana (1823) and the Baptist War in Jamaica (1831–32). Each was crushed, but together they persuaded many in Britain that slavery could not last.
  • Registration: Barbados passed its own slave registry act in 1817.
  • Abolition: the Slavery Abolition Act was passed in 1833 and came into force on 1 August 1834, although formerly enslaved people were forced into a period of unpaid "apprenticeship" until 1838. Slave owners — not the enslaved — received compensation.
  • Independence: Barbados became independent in 1966, and a republic in 2021.
05

What do others say?

At the time

The Barbados Assembly blamed the revolt on the British abolitionists and on missionaries, whose ideas, they claimed, had misled the enslaved. Abolitionists in Britain argued that the revolt showed the cruelty and instability of slavery.

Historians' debates

  • What did the rebels want? Historians such as Hilary Beckles argue that the rebels aimed at freedom and the overthrow of the slave system on the island, not merely better treatment.
  • Who led? Bussa is remembered as the leader, but the evidence for his role comes largely from a few official records; historians emphasise that the revolt was the work of a wide network that included Nanny Grigg, Jackey and Franklin.
  • The influence of Haiti: testimony at the time suggests that news of the Haitian Revolution inspired the rebels; historians debate how important it was.

07

How is it remembered?

  • Karl Broodhagen's Emancipation Statue, unveiled in 1985 at Haggatt Hall, St Michael, is popularly known as the "Bussa Statue".
  • In 1998 the Parliament of Barbados named Bussa as one of the country's eleven National Heroes.
  • Emancipation Day (1 August) is a public holiday in Barbados, and the Crop Over festival has roots in the plantation era.
08

Why does it matter?

Bussa's rebellion was the largest revolt in the history of Barbados and the first of three great slave rebellions in the British Caribbean in the years before emancipation. It shattered the planters' claim that their slaves were content, and showed that enslaved people were following the debates about their future and were ready to fight for their freedom.

  • It is a reminder that emancipation was not simply granted by reformers in Britain, but was also won by the resistance of the enslaved.
  • The role of Nanny Grigg shows the importance of women in organising resistance.
  • Bussa has become a national hero and a symbol of freedom in Barbados.

Questions to think about

  1. Why do you think the rebels chose Easter Sunday to begin the revolt?
  2. How might news of debates in the British Parliament have reached enslaved people in Barbados?
  3. What does the role of Nanny Grigg tell us about women in the resistance to slavery?
  4. Was emancipation given by Britain, or won by the enslaved?

What other events are like this?

  • 1791The Haitian Revolution — The only revolution in history in which enslaved people overthrew their enslavers and founded an independent state.
  • 1865The Morant Bay Rebellion — Thirty years after slavery ended, poor Black Jamaicans marched on a courthouse demanding justice and land. The governor's response — hundreds killed, flogged and hanged — scandalised Britain.
  • 1835The Malê Revolt — During Ramadan, enslaved and freed African Muslims rose up in Salvador — the most significant urban revolt by enslaved people in the history of the Americas.
