The Lawyers' Movement

When Pakistan's military ruler suspended the chief justice, lawyers in black coats took to the streets. Two years of protests, boycotts and a 'Long March' brought the judges back — and helped end military rule.

When
9 March 2007 – 17 March 2009
Where
Islamabad, Lahore and Karachi, Pakistan
Facts checked
2026-11-17

See it on the map →

01

What is the background to this event?

General Pervez Musharraf, the army chief, had taken power in a coup in 1999 and was now president.

  • The chief justice, Iftikhar Muhammad Chaudhry, had taken on politically controversial cases, including the sale of the state steel mills.1
  • His court had also ordered the intelligence agencies to produce people who had disappeared — up to 400 people, including terror suspects and human rights activists, held without due process since 2001.1
  • When Chaudhry refused to resign, Musharraf suspended him on 9 March 2007 on unspecified charges of misconduct.1

In their own words

03

What happened?

The Supreme Court Bar Association called the chief justice's removal an "assault on the independence of judiciary", and lawyers began the 'Save the Judiciary Movement'.1

  • On 12 May 2007, Chaudhry arrived in Karachi to address lawyers. Rival demonstrations by his supporters and pro-government parties ended in clashes that killed more than 40 people.1
  • On 20 July, after four months of non-violent civil resistance, Chaudhry was reinstated, and the Supreme Court cleared him of all charges.1
  • On 3 November, Musharraf declared a state of emergency, dismissed the chief justice again and removed about sixty other judges. Leaders of the movement were placed under house arrest.1
  • On 5 November, police raided the Lahore High Court Bar Association, using batons and tear gas, and arrested more than 800 lawyers. Lawyers boycotted the courts and held protests and hunger strikes.1
  • Musharraf ended emergency rule in December 2007 under international pressure, but did not restore the judges. He resigned in August 2008.1
  • The new government of Asif Ali Zardari was reluctant to restore them. In March 2009, lawyers and opposition parties set out on a 'Long March' from Karachi to Islamabad, defying a ban on rallies. On 17 March 2009, Chaudhry was reinstated.1
04

What happened next?

  • Restoration: Chaudhry returned as chief justice in March 2009.1
  • Division: the movement later split between those who backed the restored judiciary and those closer to the government.1
05

What do others say?

At the time

The movement's supporters saw a fight for the independence of the judiciary; pro-government parties accused it of 'politicising' the issue.1

Debates since

  • Lawyers or politicians? Opposition parties joined the movement and shaped its later course.1
  • Violence: the movement was largely non-violent, but the clashes in Karachi on 12 May 2007 killed more than 40 people, and each side blamed the other.1
06

07

How is it remembered?

  • The lawyers' black coats and the Long March of 2009 became symbols of the struggle for the rule of law in Pakistan.1
08

Why does it matter?

The Lawyers' Movement was a rare case of a professional group leading a mass movement. It showed that non-violent civil resistance could push back against military rule in Pakistan.1

Open questions

  1. Why would lawyers lead a mass protest movement?
  2. Why did the government fear an independent chief justice?
  3. Why did the movement continue after Musharraf resigned?
  4. Was the movement about the rule of law, or about politics?
09

What other events are like this?

  • 1983The Women's March in Lahore — When Pakistan's military ruler proposed that a woman's testimony be worth half a man's, women lawyers and activists marched in defiance of martial law. They were tear-gassed and beaten — the first public protest against the dictatorship.
  • 1990Nepal's People's Movement of 1990 — Rival parties, from liberals to communists, joined forces against Nepal's absolute monarchy. After 49 days of strikes and protests, the king lifted the ban on political parties.
  • 2007The Bersih Rally — Tens of thousands of Malaysians, many dressed in yellow, marched through Kuala Lumpur to demand clean and fair elections. Police used water cannon and tear gas — and a year later, the ruling coalition suffered its worst result in decades.
