Workers & unions

The Cordobazo

Car workers, power workers and students rose against Argentina's military dictatorship and took control of much of the city for two days. The army retook it — but the dictatorship never recovered.

When
29 – 30 May 1969
Where
Córdoba, Argentina

See it on the map →

01

What is the background to this event?

In 1966, General Juan Carlos Onganía seized power in Argentina.1

  • His regime suspended the right to strike, froze wages, devalued the currency and raised the retirement age.1
  • It persecuted activists and trade unionists, and in the "Night of the Long Batons" in 1966, police violently expelled teachers and students from the universities.1
  • Córdoba had become a major industrial city, home to car plants such as IKA-Renault and FIAT, with a young, combative workforce.
  • In May 1969, police killed students during protests in Corrientes and Rosario.1
02

Who were the key people?

  • Agustín Tosco

    Leader of the Córdoba light and power workers' union, a key organiser, imprisoned after the uprising. He died in hiding in 1975.

  • Elpidio Torres

    Leader of the car workers' union SMATA in Córdoba.

  • Máximo Mena

    A car worker, the first person killed by police.

  • Students of the University of Córdoba

    Who joined the workers on the barricades.

03

What happened?

Córdoba's unions called a general strike for 29 May 1969, as the national union confederation called a national strike.1

  • Workers marched from the factories towards the city centre, joined by students.
  • Police shot dead a car worker, Máximo Mena, which triggered further demonstrations and rioting.1
  • Residents took control of most of the city and built barricades to defend themselves.1
  • Protesters burned administrative buildings and the offices of foreign firms, such as Citroën and Xerox, which symbolised the regime's economic policies — though they also helped firefighters stop the fires spreading.1

On the night of 29–30 May, Onganía sent in the army to crush the uprising. Union leaders, including Agustín Tosco, were arrested and sentenced by a military court.1 Official figures gave about a dozen dead; other estimates are higher.

04

What happened next?

  • Onganía falls: the dictatorship was weakened, and Onganía was removed by the military in 1970.
  • Clasismo: radical "class-struggle" unions took over the car workers' unions in Córdoba.1
  • Return and violence: Juan Perón returned to power in 1973. Political violence by left-wing guerrillas and right-wing death squads escalated. The historian James Brennan called the Cordobazo a step towards the violent climax of the 1976 coup, after which the dictatorship "disappeared" tens of thousands of people.1
05

What do others say?

At the time

The government and official media portrayed the uprising as a communist conspiracy.1 Participants saw it as a popular revolt against dictatorship.

Historians' debates

  • Spontaneous or organised? Historians debate how far the uprising was planned by unions and how far it was spontaneous.
  • Violence: some argue that the Cordobazo showed that people accepted violent means against the dictatorship; others see it as the start of a spiral of violence that ended in the horrors of the 1976–83 dictatorship.1

07

How is it remembered?

  • The Cordobazo is commemorated every 29 May by unions and students in Córdoba.
08

Why does it matter?

The Cordobazo was one of the great popular uprisings of 1960s Latin America. It brought together workers and students and showed that the dictatorship could be challenged.1

  • It shows the power of alliances between workers and students.
  • It also shows how a cycle of repression and rebellion can spiral into greater violence.
