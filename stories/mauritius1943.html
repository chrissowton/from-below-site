Anjalay Coopen and the Belle Vue Harel Strike

Sugar cane workers in British-ruled Mauritius struck for fairer pay. When police came to break up their gathering, they opened fire, killing four people — among them Anjalay Coopen, a pregnant labourer, and a 14-year-old boy.

When
13 – 27 September 1943
Where
Belle Vue Harel sugar estate, Pamplemousses, Mauritius

01

What is the background to this event?

Large sugar estates, owned by a Franco-Mauritian elite, dominated Mauritius and held great political power. Many of the labourers were Indo-Mauritians whose ancestors had come to the island under British colonial rule.21

  • In 1937, small cane growers had rioted over a cut in the price paid for their cane, and four people died.2
  • In December 1942, four workers at the Belle Vue Harel estate wrote to the Labour Department on behalf of all its workers, asking for fairer pay. They received no reply.1

03

What happened?

On 13 September 1943, the labourers of Belle Vue Harel went on strike.1

  • Their two representatives accepted a deal at the Conciliation Board without consulting the strikers. The workers rejected it and demanded a new board and a fairer agreement.1
  • The estate owners threatened to evict workers who did not accept the deal within five days, and arranged with the police to end the strike.1
  • On 27 September, the workers held a gathering on the estate. A policeman disguised as a labourer was discovered and assaulted.1
  • Armed police arrived and faced a crowd of 200 to 300 men, women and children carrying sticks and stones. Officers disagreed about whether the crowd was praying.1
  • When police tried to arrest the man accused of the assault, the crowd attacked them. Warning shots were fired, then 16 shots in all.1
  • Three labourers died, and a fourth died of his wounds on 6 October. Five more were wounded by bullets; two police officers were seriously injured.1

04

What happened next?

  • The funeral: more than 1,500 people attended the funeral organised by Basdeo Bissoondoyal.1
  • Back to work: one of the workers' representatives fasted for eight days in a temple, then persuaded the labourers to return to work on 12 October.1
  • The inquiry: the Moody Commission, reporting in 1944, was very critical of the police and of the Labour Department's failure to settle the dispute peacefully.1
05

What do others say?

At the time

The police said they had been attacked by an armed crowd; the workers' side stressed that men, women and children had gathered on the estate, some of them for a religious ceremony.1

Debates since

  • Violence: four labourers died and 5 more had bullet wounds; 2 police officers were seriously injured and 14 lightly injured, according to the inquiry.1
06

The evidence

  • The Moody Commission's report and the testimony of police officers and survivors.1
07

How is it remembered?

  • Between 1995 and 2007, monuments and statues of Anjalay Coopen were put up in Port Louis and the village of Cottage, and the Anjalay Stadium was named after her.1
  • The singer Siven Chinien remembered the dead in his song "L'année 1943".1
08

Why does it matter?

The Belle Vue Harel shootings became a symbol of the struggle of Mauritius's sugar workers, and Anjalay Coopen, a woman labourer, became a national figure.1
