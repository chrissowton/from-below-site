The Rescue of the Danish Jews

When the Nazis ordered the arrest of Denmark's Jews, ordinary Danes hid their neighbours and fishermen ferried them across the sea to Sweden. More than 7,000 people escaped.

When
October 1943
Where
Gilleleje and the Øresund coast, Denmark
Facts checked
2026-11-16

01

What is the background to this event?

Germany occupied Denmark in 1940.

  • About 8,000 Jews lived in Denmark.1
  • The Danish government refused to help the Germans move against them.1
  • On 28 September 1943, the German diplomat Georg Ferdinand Duckwitz leaked Hitler's plan to deport Denmark's Jews to a Danish politician, Hans Hedtoft.1

03

What happened?

On 29 September 1943, Jews at morning services were warned and urged to go into hiding.1

  • The Danish state church and almost all political parties publicly denounced the Nazi plan, and the bishops issued a pastoral letter read in churches.1
  • Danes hid Jewish families in homes, hospitals, woods and cottages.1
  • Fishing boats, rowing boats and even kayaks carried people across the Øresund to Sweden, usually in under an hour.1
  • Sweden announced on radio that it would take the refugees.1
  • About 7,500 of Denmark's 8,000 Jews, plus 686 non-Jewish spouses, reached Sweden.1
04

What happened next?

  • Survival: 464 Danish Jews were captured and sent to Theresienstadt; because Denmark kept pressing on their behalf, most survived. In all, 99% of Denmark's Jews survived the Holocaust.1
  • Resistance: the rescue strengthened the Danish resistance movement.
05

What do others say?

At the time

The German occupiers expected little resistance; Danish society largely refused to co-operate.1

Debates since

  • Why Denmark? Historians such as Bo Lidegaard stress Danish democracy and solidarity; others note special conditions, including Sweden's closeness and some Germans' reluctance.2
  • Money and betrayal: about half the cost was paid by Jewish families, and some fishermen charged high fares; at Gilleleje, about 80 Jews were caught after being betrayed.1

07

How is it remembered?

  • Yad Vashem has honoured the Danish resistance as Righteous Among the Nations, and boats used in the rescue are preserved in museums.
08

Why does it matter?

The rescue is one of the largest acts of collective resistance in Nazi-occupied Europe.1

  • It shows what ordinary people could do to protect their neighbours.

Open questions

  1. Why did so many Danes help their Jewish neighbours?
  2. How did geography help the rescue?
  3. Why did it matter that churches and parties spoke out publicly?
  4. Why did similar rescues not happen in most occupied countries?
09

What other events are like this?

  • 1943Stopping the Trains: Bulgaria 1943 — When the Bulgarian government secretly agreed to deport the country's Jews to Nazi death camps, townspeople, MPs, church leaders and ordinary citizens protested. Bulgaria's 48,000 Jews survived — but the 11,343 Jews in Bulgarian-occupied lands did not.
  • 1942The Luxembourg General Strike of 1942 — When Nazi Germany announced that young Luxembourgers would be conscripted into its army, a whole country stopped work. Twenty-one strikers were shot, and their families deported.
  • 1941The February Strike — After German police seized 425 Jewish men in Amsterdam, the city's tram drivers, dockers and factory workers walked out. It was the first mass public protest against the persecution of Jews in Nazi-occupied Europe.
