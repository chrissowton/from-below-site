The Trefechan Bridge Protest

Students sat down on a bridge in Aberystwyth to demand official status for the Welsh language — beginning decades of direct action that transformed Welsh public life.

When
2 February 1963
Where
Trefechan Bridge, Aberystwyth, Wales

See it on the map →

01

What is the background to this event?

Welsh is one of the oldest living languages in Europe. In 1900 around half the population of Wales could speak it, but by 1961 the figure had fallen to about a quarter. Welsh had no official status: courts, government forms, tax returns, road signs and most broadcasting were in English only.

On 13 February 1962 the writer and nationalist Saunders Lewis gave the annual BBC Wales radio lecture, titled Tynged yr Iaith ("The Fate of the Language"). He predicted that Welsh would cease to be a living language by the early twenty-first century unless drastic action were taken, and argued that this would require a campaign of civil disobedience to make it impossible for government and business to function without Welsh.

Inspired by the lecture, a group of young people founded Cymdeithas yr Iaith Gymraeg (the Welsh Language Society) at Pontarddulais in August 1962.

02

Who were the key people?

  • Saunders Lewis

    Writer, dramatist and co-founder of Plaid Cymru, whose 1962 lecture inspired the movement.

  • The founders of Cymdeithas yr Iaith

    Young people, many of them students, including Tedi Millward and John Davies, who founded the society in 1962.

  • The Trefechan protesters

    Around seventy members and supporters, mainly students from Aberystwyth and Bangor, who sat down on the bridge.

  • Gwynfor Evans

    President of Plaid Cymru, whose threat of a hunger strike in 1980 led to the creation of S4C.

  • Dafydd Iwan

    Singer and later chair of the society, imprisoned for his part in the road-sign campaign.

03

What happened?

The society's first public protest took place on Saturday 2 February 1963 in Aberystwyth. Members first pasted posters on the town's post office, hoping to be arrested and so force a court to deal with them in Welsh.1 When the police did not arrest them, they moved to Trefechan Bridge, at the southern entrance to the town.1

  • Around seventy members and supporters sat down on the bridge, blocking the road for about half an hour.1
  • The protest caused traffic delays and angered some motorists and onlookers.
  • The sit-in attracted press coverage across Wales and beyond, and announced a new style of direct action for the language.

The society's early campaigns demanded official status for Welsh in everyday public life: Welsh-language tax returns, electoral forms, birth certificates, post office signs and court proceedings.1 It organised in local "cells", the first established in Bangor in April 1963.1

04

What happened next?

  • The Welsh Language Act 1967 gave Welsh limited "equal validity" in Wales, particularly in the courts.
  • Road signs: in the late 1960s and 1970s the society campaigned for bilingual road signs, painting over and removing English-only signs; many members were fined or imprisoned. Bilingual signs became standard.
  • Broadcasting: after the government abandoned its promise of a Welsh-language television channel, Gwynfor Evans threatened a hunger strike in 1980. The government reversed its decision, and S4C began broadcasting in 1982.
  • Official status: the Welsh Language Act 1993 required public bodies to treat Welsh and English equally, and the Welsh Language (Wales) Measure 2011 gave Welsh official status in Wales.
  • Revival: the decline in the number of speakers slowed, Welsh-medium education expanded greatly, and the Welsh Government now aims for a million Welsh speakers by 2050.
05

What do others say?

At the time

Many people in Wales, including many Welsh speakers, disapproved of law-breaking and saw the protesters as troublemakers who would damage the language's cause. Some English-speaking residents felt that the campaign treated them as outsiders in their own country.

Historians' debates

  • Direct action or politics? Historians debate how much credit belongs to the society's direct action and how much to parliamentary campaigning, Plaid Cymru's electoral success and broader social change.
  • Was Saunders Lewis right? His lecture predicted the language's death by around 2000. Some argue the movement proved him wrong; others note that the proportion of speakers has continued to fall in some heartland areas.
  • Damage to property: the destruction of road signs and, later, of television transmitters divided opinion within the movement.

07

How is it remembered?

  • Anniversary events have been held on Trefechan Bridge, including for the 50th and 60th anniversaries.
  • The bridge remains a site of protest; in 2022 a rally there campaigned against second-home ownership in Welsh-speaking communities.
  • Cymdeithas yr Iaith continues to campaign today.
08

Why does it matter?

The Trefechan protest began a movement that changed the place of the Welsh language in public life. It shows how a minority language community, facing decline, used non-violent direct action to win legal recognition and public institutions.

  • It helped secure bilingual signs, Welsh-medium broadcasting and official status for Welsh.
  • The society's methods influenced other minority-language movements in Europe.
  • It raises wider questions about the relationship between language, identity and political rights.

What other events are like this?

  • 1952The Bengali Language Movement — Students in Dhaka defied a ban on protest to demand that Bengali be a national language of Pakistan. Police opened fire. The date is now International Mother Language Day.
  • 1972The Māori Language Petition — Young Māori activists delivered a petition of more than 30,000 signatures calling for te reo Māori to be taught in schools — the start of the language's revival.
