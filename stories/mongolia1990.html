Mongolia's Democratic Revolution

Young Mongolians gathered in the freezing main square of Ulaanbaatar to demand democracy. A hunger strike in March 1990 brought down the communist leadership — without a single death.

When
December 1989 – March 1990
Where
Sükhbaatar Square, Ulaanbaatar, Mongolia

01

What is the background to this event?

In 1924, Mongolia became the world's second communist state, the Mongolian People's Republic, closely allied with and dominated by the Soviet Union. It was ruled by the Mongolian People's Revolutionary Party (MPRP).1

  • In the late 1930s, under Khorloogiin Choibalsan, tens of thousands of people — including many Buddhist monks — were killed in purges, and hundreds of monasteries were destroyed.
  • Soviet troops were stationed in Mongolia, and Mongolian history and culture, including the memory of Chinggis Khan, were suppressed.
  • In the late 1980s, Gorbachev's reforms in the Soviet Union and the revolutions of 1989 in Eastern Europe inspired young Mongolians. From December 1988, the party newspaper began publishing letters criticising the leadership, and young dissidents formed underground groups.1

In their own words

03

What happened?

On 10 December 1989 — International Human Rights Day — the first open pro-democracy demonstration took place in Ulaanbaatar, where the creation of the Mongolian Democratic Union (MDU) was announced. Its demands included a multi-party system, free elections with universal suffrage, a market economy and respect for human rights.1

Through the bitterly cold winter, the movement grew:1

  • On 14 January 1990, around 5,000 people gathered in front of the Lenin Museum — the largest demonstration yet. Zorig called for the removal of the constitutional clause guaranteeing the party's monopoly on power.
  • Teachers and students in provincial towns such as Khovd and Erdenet held their own demonstrations.
  • New opposition parties were formed, and on 4 March 1990 the main opposition groups held a joint mass meeting near Sükhbaatar Square.

On 7 March 1990, ten members of the MDU began a hunger strike in Sükhbaatar Square, demanding the government's resignation. Thousands gathered to support them.1 The next day, the Politburo entered into negotiations with the opposition. Behind the scenes, some members of the leadership discussed a crackdown.1 But on 9 March, the entire Politburo, headed by Jambyn Batmönkh, announced its resignation.1 Throughout the revolution, no one was killed.

04

What happened next?

  • Multi-party elections: the constitution was amended to end the party's monopoly, and in July 1990 Mongolia held its first multi-party elections. The MPRP won a majority, but opposition parties entered parliament.
  • A new constitution: on 12 February 1992, a new democratic constitution was adopted; the country became simply "Mongolia".
  • Change of power: in 1996, the democratic opposition won a parliamentary election for the first time.
  • Hard times: the end of Soviet aid and the move to a market economy caused severe economic hardship in the early 1990s.
05

What do others say?

At the time

Some party hardliners saw the protests as a threat to stability and to the country's alliance with the Soviet Union. The demonstrators presented themselves as patriots seeking democracy and national revival.

Historians' debates

  • Why was it peaceful? Historians point to the restraint of the leadership, the Soviet Union's unwillingness to intervene, and the deliberate non-violence of the opposition — as when Zorig calmed angry crowds.
  • Revolution or reform? The old ruling party won the first free elections and remained powerful, leading some to see the change as a negotiated transition rather than a revolution.
  • Democracy and inequality: scholars such as Morris Rossabi have criticised the economic "shock therapy" that followed, which increased poverty and inequality.

07

How is it remembered?

  • A statue of Sanjaasürengiin Zorig stands in central Ulaanbaatar.
  • The anniversary of the first demonstration, 10 December 1989, is commemorated by Mongolia's democratic movement.
  • Sükhbaatar Square remains the focal point of Mongolian political life and protest.
08

Why does it matter?

Mongolia's revolution was one of the most peaceful of the revolutions of 1989–90, and one of the few in Asia. Young people, demonstrating in temperatures far below freezing, brought down a communist regime of almost seventy years without bloodshed.

  • Mongolia became one of the most durable democracies in Asia, sandwiched between Russia and China.
  • The revolution allowed Mongolians to recover their history, religion and culture, including the memory of Chinggis Khan.
  • It shows the power of peaceful mass protest, and of leaders who choose not to use force.

What other events are like this?

  • 1989The Baltic Way — Around two million people joined hands in a human chain 675 kilometres long, across Estonia, Latvia and Lithuania, to demand freedom from the Soviet Union.
  • 1980The Gdańsk Shipyard Strike and Solidarity — A strike over the sacking of a crane operator grew into the first independent trade union in the Soviet bloc, with ten million members.
  • 1989Tiananmen Square, 1989 — Students and workers occupied the heart of Beijing for seven weeks, calling for freedom of speech and an end to corruption, until the army cleared the streets with tanks and gunfire.
