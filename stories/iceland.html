Iceland's Women's Day Off

Around 90 per cent of Iceland's women stopped work — paid and unpaid — for a day, to show how much the country depended on them.

When
24 October 1975
Where
Reykjavík and across Iceland

See it on the map →

01

What is the background to this event?

In the early 1970s, Iceland was a small country of around 220,000 people with a strong tradition of social solidarity. Yet women's position was far from equal:

  • Women who worked outside the home earned less than sixty per cent of what men earned.
  • Women were concentrated in low-paid jobs in fish processing, cleaning, care and offices, and few held senior positions.
  • Only a handful of women had ever been elected to the Althing, the Icelandic parliament.
  • Housework and childcare — unpaid and largely invisible — fell almost entirely on women.

The United Nations declared 1975 International Women's Year. In Iceland, the radical feminist group known as the Redstockings (Rauðsokkur) proposed a women's strike, and a broad alliance of women's organisations, from the radical to the conservative, took up the idea.

02

Who were the key people?

  • The Redstockings

    A radical feminist movement, founded in 1970, which first proposed a women's general strike.

  • The organising committee

    Representatives of Iceland's major women's organisations and unions, who agreed to call the action a 'day off' (kvennafrí) rather than a strike, to broaden support and to protect participants from dismissal.

  • Aðalheiður Bjarnfreðsdóttir

    Who gave the final speech at the Reykjavík rally, representing Sókn, the trade union for the lowest-paid women in Iceland.

  • Vigdís Finnbogadóttir

    A theatre director who took part in the day off and was elected President of Iceland in 1980 — the first woman in the world elected head of state in a democratic national election.

  • The 90 per cent

    Women in towns, farms and fishing villages across the country who stopped working, at home and at work.

03

What happened?

On 24 October 1975, the women of Iceland took a "day off". They did not go to their paid jobs, and they did no housework or child-rearing for the whole day.1 The organisers stated that the aim was to "demonstrate the indispensable work of women for Iceland's economy and society" and to "protest wage discrepancy and unfair employment practices".1

The response was overwhelming:1

  • Around 90 per cent of Iceland's women took part.
  • About 25,000 people — in a country of 220,000 — gathered for a rally in central Reykjavík, with further meetings in towns across the country.
  • Schools, shops, nurseries, fish factories, banks and theatres were forced to close or operate with reduced staff, and many fathers had to take their children to work.

The day was marked by speeches, singing and a sense of celebration as well as protest. The final speaker at the Reykjavík rally was Aðalheiður Bjarnfreðsdóttir, who represented Sókn, the trade union for the country's lowest-paid women.1

04

What happened next?

  • A new law: in 1976 the Althing passed a law guaranteeing equal rights for women and men, and establishing an Equality Council.
  • A woman president: in 1980 Vigdís Finnbogadóttir was elected President of Iceland, the first woman in the world to be democratically elected as a head of state. She served for sixteen years.
  • Repeated actions: women walked out again in 1985, 2005, 2010, 2016, 2018 and 2023, each time leaving work at the minute of the working day after which, given the pay gap, they were in effect working for free — 2:08 p.m. in 2005, 2:38 p.m. in 2016.
  • Equal pay law: in 2018 Iceland became one of the first countries to require employers to prove that they pay men and women equally.
05

What do others say?

At the time

Some employers and commentators doubted that women would take part, or dismissed the day as a gimmick. Some conservative women's groups were initially uneasy about the language of a "strike", which is why the organisers chose "day off" instead.

Historians' debates

  • How much did it change? Some historians and activists stress the rapid legal and political gains; others point out that a significant gender pay gap persisted for decades, which is why women repeated the action in later years.
  • Strike or day off? The decision to avoid the word "strike" broadened participation, but some radicals felt it softened the political message.
  • Iceland's special conditions: some argue that Iceland's small, close-knit society made such a near-universal action possible in a way that would be harder elsewhere.
06

The evidence

  • Press coverage in Iceland and abroad, including photographs of the vast crowd in Lækjartorg square.
  • The records and speeches of the organising committee, held in the Women's History Archives of Iceland.
  • Oral histories and anniversary interviews with participants.
  • Many popular anecdotes — for example, that shops sold out of sausages because fathers had to feed their children — are widely repeated but hard to verify.
07

How is it remembered?

  • The day is commemorated on its anniversaries with new walk-outs, most recently in 2023, when Iceland's prime minister also joined.
  • The 50th anniversary in 2025 was marked with events across the country.
  • The story was told in the documentary The Day Iceland Stood Still (2024).
08

Why does it matter?

The Women's Day Off is one of the clearest demonstrations of how much societies depend on women's paid and unpaid work. By simply stopping, Icelandic women made visible what was usually taken for granted.

  • It contributed to rapid changes in Icelandic law and politics, and Iceland now regularly ranks at or near the top of international gender-equality indexes.
  • It inspired similar actions elsewhere, including Poland's "Black Monday" protest of 2016 and the International Women's Strike of 2017–18.
  • It shows how a broad coalition, choosing its language carefully, can bring almost an entire population into collective action.

Questions to think about

  1. Why do you think the organisers chose to call it a 'day off' rather than a 'strike'?
  2. What would happen if all women in your country stopped work for a day?
  3. Why does unpaid work, such as housework and caring, so often go unnoticed?
  4. Why do you think the gender pay gap has proved so persistent, even in Iceland?

What other events are like this?

  • 1893Women Win the Vote in New Zealand — A nationwide petition campaign made New Zealand the first self-governing country in which all women could vote in parliamentary elections.
  • 1888The Matchwomen's Strike — About 1,400 women and girls walked out of a match factory, won their demands in three weeks, and founded the largest union of women in Britain.
  • 1929The Women's War (Ogu Umunwanyi) — Tens of thousands of Igbo and Ibibio women challenged British colonial rule, using their own traditions of collective protest.
