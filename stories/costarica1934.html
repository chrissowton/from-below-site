The Great Banana Strike of 1934

Thousands of banana workers on the Caribbean coast walked off the plantations of the United Fruit Company. Led by a young Communist who later became one of Costa Rica's great novelists, they forced the company to the negotiating table.

When
August – September 1934
Where
Limón province, Costa Rica
Facts checked
2026-11-16

See it on the map →

01

What is the background to this event?

The United Fruit Company dominated Costa Rica's Caribbean coast, owning banana plantations, railways and ports.

  • Workers — Costa Ricans, Nicaraguans and Black West Indians — faced low pay, malaria, poor housing and payment in company vouchers.
  • Earlier banana strikes and protests had been repressed by the government on behalf of the company.1
  • The Communist Party of Costa Rica, founded in 1931, had begun organising plantation workers.
02

Who were the key people?

  • Carlos Luis Fallas ('Calufa')

    A former plantation worker and Communist organiser who led the strike.

  • Banana workers of the Atlantic coast

    Tens of thousands of whom joined the strike, according to estimates.

  • Manuel Mora

    Leader of the Communist Party and member of Congress.

  • The United Fruit Company

    The US company that owned most of the plantations.

03

What happened?

In August 1934, workers on the plantations around Limón went on strike. Carlos Luis Fallas, who had been sent to the coast as punishment for earlier union activity, became its leader.2

  • The strike involved some 15,000 banana workers.2
  • Workers demanded higher wages paid in cash, decent housing, medical care, and an end to the company's control of their lives.
  • The government brokered an agreement, but the company failed to keep it, and the strike resumed.
  • The authorities sent police, arrested leaders, including Fallas, and broke up the strike.
04

What happened next?

  • Partial gains: some demands were met in later years.
  • Mamita Yunai: in 1941 Fallas published Mamita Yunai, a novel about life on the plantations, praised by Pablo Neruda.2
  • Labour code: Costa Rica adopted major social reforms and a labour code in 1943.
05

Why does it matter?

The strike was one of the most important workers' struggles in Central America, and challenged the power of a foreign company over a whole region.

  • It contributed to the social reforms of the 1940s.
  • The strike leader Carlos Luis Fallas published Mamita Yunai, a novel of plantation life praised by Pablo Neruda.2
  • Costa Rica adopted major social reforms and a labour code in 1943.

What do others say?How it was seen at the time, and where historians disagree

At the time

The company and government blamed Communist agitators; the strikers said they were demanding basic decency.3

Debates since

  • Numbers: estimates of the number of strikers vary.2
  • Race and division: historians note tensions between Spanish-speaking workers and Black West Indian workers, which the company sometimes exploited.
  • Violence: the strike was largely peaceful, but met with arrests and police repression.
How do we know?The evidence, and a source from the time
  • Union and Communist Party newspapers, such as Trabajo.
  • Fallas's writings.

Primary source: Newspaper advertisement

What is it?
Newspaper advertisement
Made by, and when
Trabajo, Costa Rica, 1941

An advertisement for Mamita Yunai, the novel by Carlos Luis Fallas, leader of the 1934 banana strike. 'Mamita Yunai' was the workers' nickname for the United Fruit Company. Published in the Communist newspaper Trabajo, the novel drew on Fallas's own life on the plantations.

Reading it

'Mamita Yunai' — Mother United — was the workers' mocking nickname for the United Fruit Company, and the strike leader Fallas used it as the title of his novel. This advertisement ran in the Communist newspaper. A novel drawn from its author's own life on the plantations can be read as a historical source, as long as we remember it is also a story.
