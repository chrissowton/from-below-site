The Bengali Language Movement

Students in Dhaka defied a ban on protest to demand that Bengali be a national language of Pakistan. Police opened fire. The date is now International Mother Language Day.

When
21 February 1952
Where
Dhaka, East Bengal (now Bangladesh)

01

What is the background to this event?

When British India was partitioned in 1947, the new state of Pakistan was created in two parts, separated by more than 1,500 kilometres of Indian territory: West Pakistan and East Bengal (later East Pakistan). The majority of Pakistan's citizens lived in the east, and most of them spoke Bengali, a language with a long literary tradition.

The new state's leaders, however, promoted Urdu — the language of only a small minority, but associated with Muslim identity in north India — as the sole national language:

  • In November 1947 a national education summit in Karachi recommended Urdu and English as the only state languages.
  • On 8 December 1947 students at the University of Dhaka met to demand that Bengali be made an official language.
  • In March 1948 the Governor-General, Muhammad Ali Jinnah, declared in Dhaka that "Urdu, and only Urdu" would be the state language, and described those who disagreed as "enemies of Pakistan".
  • The government also explored writing Bengali in Arabic or Roman script.

For many Bengalis, this was not only a question of language but of jobs, education and political power, which were increasingly concentrated in West Pakistan.

03

What happened?

In January 1952 Prime Minister Khawaja Nazimuddin reaffirmed that Urdu alone would be the state language. An all-party action committee called for strikes, rallies and processions across East Bengal on 21 February.1 To prevent the demonstration, the provincial government of Chief Minister Nurul Amin imposed Section 144 of the Criminal Procedure Code in Dhaka, banning gatherings.1

On the morning of 21 February, students gathered on the University of Dhaka campus, which was surrounded by armed police:1

  • The students resolved to defy the ban, leaving the campus in small groups.
  • Police fired tear gas, and arrested students as they tried to leave.
  • Students gathered near Dhaka Medical College and the provincial assembly building. When a group tried to enter the building, police opened fire, killing several people, including Abdus Salam, Rafiquddin Ahmed, Abul Barkat and Abdul Jabbar.1

The killings provoked widespread anger. On 22 February a mass funeral prayer and procession took place in defiance of the ban, and further people were killed. Students built a makeshift martyrs' monument, the first Shaheed Minar, on the site; the police demolished it within days.12

04

What happened next?

  • Recognition: the movement continued, and in 1954 the provincial elections were won by the United Front, which supported Bengali. The 1956 constitution of Pakistan recognised both Bengali and Urdu as state languages.
  • From language to nation: the movement became a foundation of Bengali nationalism. Tensions over political and economic inequality grew through the 1960s, leading to the war of 1971 and the independence of Bangladesh.
  • International recognition: on 17 November 1999, UNESCO proclaimed 21 February as International Mother Language Day, to promote linguistic and cultural diversity worldwide.
05

What do others say?

At the time

Supporters of the Urdu-only policy argued that a single national language would unite a new and divided state, and that Urdu was closely linked to the Muslim identity on which Pakistan was founded. Some officials portrayed the movement as the work of communists or of Indian and Hindu influence; as late as 1967 the military ruler Ayub Khan claimed that East Bengal was "still under considerable Hindu culture and influence".

Historians' debates

  • A straight line to 1971? Bangladeshi national history often presents 1952 as the first step towards independence. Some historians caution that most participants in 1952 sought equal status within Pakistan, not separation.
  • Who led it? Historians debate the respective roles of students, communists, the Awami Muslim League and other parties; Badruddin Umar emphasised the role of the left.
  • The death toll remains uncertain, as does the exact sequence of the shootings.

The evidence

  • Rafiqul Islam's photographs of the marches, the funeral procession and the first Shaheed Minar.
  • Newspaper reports in Bengali and English, some censored.
  • Government records and the reports of the official inquiry.
  • Memoirs and oral histories of participants, many recorded decades later.

07

How is it remembered?

  • 21 February (Ekushey February) is a national holiday in Bangladesh. At midnight, people walk barefoot to lay flowers at the Shaheed Minar.
  • The Central Shaheed Minar in Dhaka, designed by Hamidur Rahman, was completed in its present form in 1963; it was damaged in 1971 and rebuilt.
  • The song "Amar Bhaier Rokte Rangano" ("My brother's blood spattered"), written by Abdul Gaffar Choudhury, is sung each year.
  • Replica Shaheed Minars have been built by Bangladeshi communities around the world, including in London's Altab Ali Park.
08

Why does it matter?

The Bengali Language Movement shows how the right to speak, learn and be governed in one's own language can become a question of dignity, equality and political power. A protest led by students against a ban on assembly became a turning point in the history of South Asia.

  • It is one of the most important examples of a movement for linguistic rights anywhere in the world.
  • It contributed directly to the emergence of Bangladesh as an independent state.
  • Through International Mother Language Day, it now supports efforts to protect endangered languages worldwide.
