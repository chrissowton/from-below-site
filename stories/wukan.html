The Wukan Uprising

When officials sold their farmland to developers, the villagers of Wukan drove the Communist Party officials out and held their own elections. For a while, they won.

When
September 2011 – 2016
Where
Wukan, Lufeng, Guangdong, China
Facts checked
2026-11-16

See it on the map →

01

What is the background to this event?

In China, rural land is collectively owned by villages, but local officials often sell it to developers, keeping much of the money. Land grabs were one of the main causes of the tens of thousands of "mass incidents" — protests — each year.

  • Wukan, a fishing and farming village of about 20,000 people in Guangdong province, had long been described as a model village.1
  • Over many years, local officials sold village land to developers without properly compensating the villagers.1
  • Villagers petitioned the national government in 2009 and 2010, without result.1

In their own words

03

What happened?

In September 2011, several hundred to several thousand villagers protested over the land sales, carrying signs saying "give us back our farmland". Some attacked a government building, a police station and an industrial park.1

  • Authorities allowed villagers to choose 13 representatives to negotiate.1
  • In December, security agents seized several of the representatives. One, Xue Jinbo, died in police custody in suspicious circumstances.1
  • The villagers drove the entire local government, party leadership and police out of the village.1
  • From 14 December, about a thousand police besieged the village, blocking food and supplies, while authorities censored information online.1

The provincial government, led by Wang Yang, intervened. Officials agreed to halt questionable land sales, investigate the villagers' claims and allow fresh elections.1 In 2012, villagers held open elections and chose Lin Zuluan as village leader.

04

What happened next?

  • Limited change: the elected village government struggled to recover the land, as much had already been sold.
  • 2016: in June 2016, as Lin Zuluan planned new protests, he was arrested and later sentenced to 37 months in prison for bribery, after a televised confession that supporters said was forced. Villagers protested again and were suppressed by riot police.1
  • Exile: several other village leaders fled abroad.
05

What do others say?

At the time

Local officials blamed outside forces and troublemakers. Villagers insisted they were loyal to the Party and only wanted their land back.

Debates since

  • A model? Some commentators hoped the "Wukan model" would spread; others argued it was a special case allowed by a reformist provincial leader.
  • Violence: the early protests involved attacks on buildings and police; the authorities responded with arrests, a siege and the death of a representative in custody.1
  • "Rightful resistance": scholars such as Kevin O'Brien and Lianjiang Li describe how Chinese villagers use the state's own laws and promises to make their claims.

07

How is it remembered?

  • Wukan was widely covered by international media; inside China, discussion of the protests has been censored.
08

Why does it matter?

Wukan briefly became a symbol of grassroots democracy in China — a village that expelled corrupt officials and elected its own leaders.

  • It shows the importance of land rights for rural people.
  • It shows the limits of local reform in a one-party state.

Open questions

  1. Why is land so important to China's rural people?
  2. Why do you think the provincial government agreed to negotiate?
  3. Why did the elected leaders struggle to recover the land?
  4. Can local democracy survive in a one-party state?

  • 1989Tiananmen Square, 1989 — Students and workers occupied the heart of Beijing for seven weeks, calling for freedom of speech and an end to corruption, until the army cleared the streets with tanks and gunfire.
  • 1879The Irish Land War and the First Boycott — Irish tenant farmers organised against evictions and high rents — and gave the world a new word, 'boycott'.
  • 2000The Cochabamba Water War — When Bolivia sold its city water system to a multinational consortium and bills soared, farmers, factory workers and residents rose up — and won.
