The German Coast Uprising

Enslaved people on the sugar plantations above New Orleans rose and marched on the city with drums and flags. It was the largest slave revolt in United States history, and it was crushed with mass executions.

When
8 – 10 January 1811
Where
LaPlace, Louisiana, United States
Facts checked
2026-11-15

See it on the map →

01

What is the background to this event?

From the 1780s, sugar plantations boomed on Louisiana's "German Coast", the Mississippi riverbank above New Orleans.1

  • Many of the enslaved people there had been born in Africa.1
  • Earlier resistance included the maroon (runaway) community of Jean Saint Malo, executed in 1784, and a slave conspiracy at Pointe Coupee in the 1790s.1
  • The Haitian Revolution had shown that enslaved people could win their freedom.

In their own words

03

What happened?

On 8 January 1811, between 64 and 125 enslaved people rose at the Andry plantation near present-day LaPlace.1

  • They wounded Manuel Andry and killed his son, then marched towards New Orleans with drums and flags.1
  • Others joined along the way; some accounts say 200 to 500 people took part.1
  • During their two-day march of about 20 miles, they burned five plantations and killed a second planter.1
  • On 10 January, militia and US troops attacked them, killing 40 to 45 rebels without loss.1
04

What happened next?

  • Executions: over the next two weeks, planters and officials tried and executed 44 more captives. Their heads were displayed on pikes.1
  • Deaths: in all, about 95 rebels were killed; the rebels had killed two members of planter families.1
05

Why does it matter?

The German Coast Uprising was the largest slave revolt in the history of the United States.1

  • It was largely forgotten for nearly two centuries.
  • The response was brutal: about 95 rebels were killed, and the heads of those executed were displayed on pikes.1
  • Since 1995, an annual commemoration has been held each January.1

What do others say?How it was seen at the time, and where historians disagree

At the time

Planters and officials called the rebels "brigands"; the rebels were fighting for their freedom.1

Debates since

  • Aims: Daniel Rasmussen argues the rebels planned to take New Orleans and end slavery; others are more cautious.3
  • Violence on both sides: the rebels killed two people and burned property; the militia and courts killed about 95 rebels and displayed their heads.1

Made by, and when
Louisiana, January 1811

Much of what we know about the revolt comes from slaveholders and officials, not from the enslaved rebels. Here, the planter Manuel Andry describes the first night of the uprising, when he was wounded and his son killed. He calls the rebels 'brigands' — a word that tells us how slaveholders wanted the revolt to be seen.

Reading it

Almost everything we know comes from slaveholders and officials, and this account is by the planter Manuel Andry, wounded on the first night and whose son was killed. He calls the rebels 'brigands' — a word chosen to make them criminals rather than people seeking freedom. The rebels' own account of what they did and why is missing.
