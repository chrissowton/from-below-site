The Gaeltacht Civil Rights Movement

Inspired by the civil rights marches in the North, Irish speakers in Connemara demanded jobs, services and a radio station in their own language — and set up a pirate one to prove the point.

When
1969 – 1972
Where
Connemara, County Galway, Ireland
Facts checked
2026-11-15

01

What is the background to this event?

Irish (Gaeilge) was the language of most people in Ireland until the nineteenth century. Colonial rule, the Great Famine of the 1840s and mass emigration devastated the Irish-speaking population, and English became the language of education, government and getting on in life.

  • When the Irish Free State was founded in 1922, Irish was declared the national language and taught in schools, and the remaining Irish-speaking districts — the Gaeltacht — were officially recognised.
  • But in the Gaeltacht itself, along the poor western coast, there were few jobs, emigration continued, and services were provided in English. People who spoke Irish every day often had to use English to deal with the state.
  • Many Irish speakers felt that the state honoured the language in words but neglected the communities that actually spoke it.1

In 1968–69, the civil rights movement in Northern Ireland, demanding equal rights for Catholics, inspired activists across Ireland.

03

What happened?

Gluaiseacht Chearta Sibhialta na Gaeltachta (the Gaeltacht Civil Rights Movement) was founded in Connemara in 1969 to highlight the decline of the Irish language and to campaign for the rights of Irish-speaking areas: for access to services in Irish, for broadcasting, for jobs and for local democracy.1 Among its founders were the writer Máirtín Ó Cadhain and the activists Seosamh Ó Cuaig and Seán Ó Cionnaith.1

The movement used a range of tactics:12

  • In the 1969 general election, Peadar Mac an Iomaire stood as a civil rights candidate in Galway West and won more than 6 per cent of the vote.
  • Some actions were confrontational: during the election campaign, activists placed nails under the wheels of the car carrying the Taoiseach (prime minister), Jack Lynch.
  • At Easter 1970, activists set up a pirate radio station, Saor Raidió Chonamara (Free Radio Connemara), broadcasting in Irish to show that a Gaeltacht radio service was possible.
  • The movement held marches, pickets and public meetings, and published its own newspaper.

The campaign quickly had results. In April 1972, the national broadcaster launched Raidió na Gaeltachta, an Irish-language radio station based in Casla, Connemara.1

04

What happened next?

  • Raidió na Gaeltachta: the station began broadcasting on 2 April 1972 and became a national service in Irish.
  • Údarás na Gaeltachta: in 1980, a development authority for the Gaeltacht, with an elected board, was established, partly in response to the movement's demands for local democracy.
  • Television: a later campaign, which included another pirate station in Connemara in 1987, led to the launch of the Irish-language television channel Teilifís na Gaeilge (now TG4) in 1996, also based in Connemara.
  • Language rights in law: the Official Languages Act 2003 gave people the right to deal with the state in Irish, and in 2007 Irish became an official language of the European Union.
05

What do others say?

At the time

Some politicians and newspapers dismissed the activists as troublemakers or as fronts for republican groups. Supporters argued that the state had betrayed the Gaeltacht while claiming to honour the language.

Historians' and commentators' debates

  • Republican links: the republican movement of the time (Sinn Féin and the IRA under Cathal Goulding) supported the movement as part of its social agitation; some activists welcomed this, while others saw the movement as independent of party politics.
  • Militant tactics: commentators debate whether confrontational tactics helped or hindered the campaign.
  • Did it save the language? Despite these achievements, the number of daily Irish speakers in the Gaeltacht has continued to decline, and debate continues about how best to support Gaeltacht communities.

07

How is it remembered?

  • Raidió na Gaeltachta celebrated its fiftieth anniversary in 2022, recalling the campaign that created it.
  • Máirtín Ó Cadhain is honoured as one of the greatest writers in Irish; a statue and murals commemorate him.
08

Why does it matter?

The Gaeltacht Civil Rights Movement reframed language as a question of rights and community survival, not just national symbolism. A small movement of Irish speakers in some of the poorest parts of the country won lasting institutions for their language.

  • It showed how language rights are linked to jobs, services and local democracy.
  • Raidió na Gaeltachta and TG4 became vital to the survival of Irish as a living language.
  • It was part of a wave of minority-language movements across Europe in the 1960s and 1970s.

Questions to think about

  1. Why do you think the activists described their campaign as one for 'civil rights'?
  2. How are language rights connected to jobs and local democracy?
  3. Why was a radio station so important to the movement?
  4. Can a state protect a language without supporting the communities that speak it?
