Bolívar, Haiti and the Promise of Freedom

Defeated and in exile, Simón Bolívar turned to Haiti, the first Black republic. Its president gave him guns and ships — on condition that he free the enslaved of South America.

When
1816 – 1821
Where
Carúpano, Venezuela, and Les Cayes, Haiti

01

What is the background to this event?

In 1810, the colonial elites of Caracas and other cities of Spanish America began to break away from Spain, which had been occupied by Napoleon. But the wars of independence were not simply a struggle between "patriots" and Spaniards:

  • Spanish American society was deeply divided by race and class. White Creole landowners — like Simón Bolívar himself, from one of the richest families in Venezuela — owned large estates worked by enslaved Africans. Pardos (free people of mixed descent) were the largest group in Venezuela, but faced legal discrimination.
  • Many poor and enslaved people distrusted the Creole patriots. In 1814, the royalist commander José Tomás Boves led an army of llaneros — the horsemen of the plains, many of them pardos and former slaves — that destroyed the Second Republic.1
  • Both sides fought with great brutality. In 1813, Bolívar issued his "Decree of War to the Death", ordering the death of all Spaniards in South America who did not actively support the patriots; royalist forces carried out massacres of their own.1

By 1815, the patriots had been defeated almost everywhere. Bolívar fled to Jamaica, where he wrote his famous "Jamaica Letter" setting out his vision of a free South America.1

02

Who were the key people?

  • Simón Bolívar

    A Venezuelan aristocrat who became the leader of independence in northern South America. He later ruled as dictator of Gran Colombia and died in 1830.

  • Alexandre Pétion

    President of the Republic of Haiti, who supported Bolívar with weapons, ships and money on condition that he abolish slavery. He asked for no public credit in return.

  • Manuel Piar

    A pardo general from Curaçao, one of the most successful patriot commanders, executed on Bolívar's orders in 1817 on charges of inciting a race war.

  • José Antonio Páez

    A llanero leader who brought the horsemen of the plains to the patriot side.

  • Manuela Sáenz

    A revolutionary from Quito who fought in the wars and saved Bolívar's life in 1828.

  • The soldiers

    Pardos, freed slaves, Indigenous people and llaneros who made up most of the patriot armies.

In their own words

03

What happened?

Haiti had won its independence in 1804, after the only successful revolution of enslaved people in history. In December 1815, Bolívar arrived in Haiti, and on 2 January 1816 he met President Alexandre Pétion.1 Bolívar pledged to free every slave in the areas he liberated, and in return Pétion gave him money, weapons and supplies.1 The patriot expedition sailed from Les Cayes on 31 March 1816.1

On landing in Venezuela, Bolívar declared the emancipation of slaves and annulled the Decree of War to the Death:13

  • At Carúpano on 2 June 1816, he proclaimed freedom for enslaved people who joined the patriot army; he repeated the decree at Ocumare in July.
  • When the first expedition failed, Pétion helped him again at the end of 1816.
  • From 1817, Bolívar built a new base in the Orinoco region. He won the support of the llaneros under José Antonio Páez — but also had the pardo general Manuel Piar tried and shot, accused of trying to incite a war of pardos against whites.

In 1819, Bolívar led his army across the Andes and defeated the Spanish at Boyacá. Venezuela, New Granada (Colombia) and Quito were joined in the Republic of Colombia (Gran Colombia). The patriots won the decisive battle of Carabobo in 1821, and went on to liberate Ecuador, Peru and Bolivia by 1825.1 Large numbers of pardos, freed slaves and Indigenous soldiers fought and died in these campaigns.

04

What happened next?

  • Slow abolition: the Congress of Cúcuta in 1821 passed a "free womb" law, freeing the children of enslaved mothers once they reached adulthood — but slavery itself was not abolished in Colombia until 1851, and in Venezuela and Peru until 1854.
  • Haiti forgotten: Haiti was not invited to the Congress of Panama in 1826, and the new republics did not recognise Haitian independence for many years.
  • Dictatorship and division: Bolívar became increasingly authoritarian, declaring himself dictator in 1828. Gran Colombia broke apart in 1830, the year of his death.
  • Inequality continued: independence ended Spanish rule but left power in the hands of the Creole elite; Indigenous tribute and other inequalities persisted.
05

Why does it matter?

The independence of Spanish South America is often told as the story of great generals. But it depended on the alliance between Bolívar and revolutionary Haiti, and on the ordinary soldiers — pardos, freed slaves, llaneros and Indigenous people — who fought in its armies, often in hope of freedom and equality.

  • It shows how the Haitian Revolution shaped the history of the whole Americas.
  • It reveals the gap between the promises of independence and the reality for enslaved and poor people.
  • Bolívar's legacy is still claimed by very different political movements, including Venezuela's "Bolivarian" government.

What do others say?How it was seen at the time, and where historians disagree

At the time

Many Creole patriots feared that freeing the enslaved and arming pardos would lead to a "second Haiti", and resisted abolition. Royalists presented themselves as protectors of the poor against the Creole elite. Bolívar himself wrote of his fear of "pardocracia" — rule by people of colour.

Historians' debates

  • Liberator or aristocrat? Some historians stress Bolívar's genuine commitment to abolition and republican government; others emphasise his authoritarianism and his fear of the masses.
  • Violence: both sides committed atrocities. Bolívar's "War to the Death" led to the execution of hundreds of Spanish prisoners in Caracas and La Guaira in 1814; Boves's royalist armies massacred civilians.
  • Piar's execution: historians debate whether Piar was a genuine threat or was killed because, as a pardo, he challenged Creole leadership.
  • Race and nation: historians such as Marixa Lasso show how the new republics proclaimed racial harmony while silencing pardo demands for equality.
How do we know?The evidence, and a source from the time
  • Bolívar's own writings, including the Jamaica Letter and his decrees and correspondence.
  • Haitian records and the correspondence between Bolívar and Pétion.
  • Military and administrative records of both sides, and the memoirs of officers, including foreign volunteers.

Reading it

An issue of the Correo del Orinoco, the newspaper founded under Bolívar two years after the expeditions from Haiti. A revolutionary army needed to win arguments as well as battles, and the paper carried news and the case for independence across the region. Later celebrations of Bolívar have often had little to say about Haiti's part.

How is it remembered?Memorials, anniversaries and arguments over memory
  • Bolívar is commemorated throughout Latin America; Bolivia and the Bolivarian Republic of Venezuela are named after him.
  • A bust of Pétion stands at the Quinta de Bolívar in Bogotá, and Venezuela and Haiti have both commemorated their alliance.
  • Manuela Sáenz, long remembered only as Bolívar's lover, has been recognised as a heroine of independence in her own right.
