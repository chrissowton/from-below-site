Nepal's Gen Z Uprising

When the government banned 26 social media platforms, young Nepalis angry at corruption and 'nepo kids' took to the streets. Police shot protesters dead, the parliament was burned, and the prime minister resigned within two days.

When
8 – 13 September 2025
Where
Kathmandu and towns across Nepal
Facts checked
2026-11-16

Ongoing situationLast reviewed 16 November 2026

Human rights groups have urged the government to publish the inquiry report in full and act on it. Nepal's new government, led by a party that has never governed before, faces high expectations from the young people whose protests brought it to power.24

Still unresolved:

  • Whether anyone is prosecuted for the killings of 8 September
  • Whether the inquiry report is published in full
  • Whether the new government can deliver on corruption and jobs

This is recent history. Accounts, casualty figures and outcomes may change as more evidence emerges.

01

What is the background to this event?

Nepal's politics had long been dominated by a few veteran party leaders. K. P. Sharma Oli was in his fourth term as prime minister.5

  • Many young people worked abroad; money sent home by migrants made up about a third of the economy.1
  • On social media, a trend highlighted the wealth of politicians' children, nicknamed "nepo kids", against the poverty of most Nepalis.1
  • On 4 September 2025, the government ordered the blocking of 26 social media platforms, including Facebook, YouTube and X, for failing to register under new rules. Critics saw it as an attempt to silence criticism.1

03

What happened?

On 8 September 2025, thousands of young people marched on the parliament building in Kathmandu.1

  • Police opened fire on the protesters, killing many on the first day. Protests spread to other cities. That evening the government lifted the social media ban, and a curfew was imposed in several cities.12
  • On 9 September, crowds set fire to the parliament, government offices, party headquarters and politicians' homes. Prime Minister Oli resigned the same day.16
  • On 12 September, Sushila Karki was appointed interim prime minister. Her name emerged from discussions among young people on a Discord server run by the youth group Hami Nepal, which also acted as a go-between with the army.1

An official inquiry later put the death toll at 76.3 According to the Nepali Army's figures, the dead included protesters, three police officers, people who died in the fires, and prisoners shot while trying to escape from jails.1 Human rights groups described most of the killings as unlawful.2

04

What happened next?

  • Inquiry: a commission led by a former Supreme Court justice reported in March 2026. It recommended action against those who ordered the shooting, those who fired and those who failed to stop it. Critics said it said little about the arson and violence of 9 September.36
  • Election: in the election of 5 March 2026, the three-year-old Rastriya Swatantra Party won 182 of 275 seats. Balendra Shah defeated Oli in his own constituency and became prime minister on 27 March 2026.45
05

What do others say?

At the time

Protesters saw a corrupt and out-of-touch political class. The government said it was enforcing rules on social media companies and protecting order.1

Debates since

  • Violence: the shooting of protesters on 8 September and the arson of 9 September are often discussed separately; some argue that the inquiry focused too much on one and too little on the other.36
  • Impunity: earlier inquiry reports into deaths during protests in Nepal were never published, and human rights groups warned that this report should not suffer the same fate.2

How is it remembered?

  • The burned parliament building became a symbol of the uprising.
08

Why does it matter?

Nepal's uprising was one of the fastest changes of government by protest in recent history. It shows both the power of online organising and the danger of protests that turn to destruction, and asks what young people want from democracy.

Questions to think about

  1. Why did a social media ban cause such anger?
  2. Why were politicians' children such a powerful symbol?
  3. Can choosing a leader in an online forum be democratic?
  4. How should we judge a movement in which protesters were shot, and buildings were burned?

What other events are like this?

  • 2006Nepal's People's Movement of 2006 — Defying curfews and shoot-on-sight orders, millions of Nepalis took to the streets for nineteen days and forced their king to give up absolute power — the beginning of the end of a 240-year-old monarchy.
  • 2024The July Uprising in Bangladesh — A student protest over government job quotas grew into a nationwide uprising. The crackdown killed as many as 1,400 people, and on 5 August 2024, Prime Minister Sheikh Hasina fled the country after fifteen years in power.
  • 2024Reject Finance Bill: Kenya's Gen Z Protests — Young Kenyans organised online against a bill that would have taxed bread and sanitary pads. They forced the president to withdraw it, but at least 60 people were killed in 2024, and dozens more when protests returned in 2025.
