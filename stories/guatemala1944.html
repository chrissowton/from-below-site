Guatemala's 'Ten Years of Spring'

Students, teachers and workers brought down a dictator. A decade of democracy and land reform followed — until a US-backed coup ended it.

When
June 1944 – June 1954
Where
Guatemala City, Guatemala
Facts checked

01

What is the background to this event?

From the late nineteenth century, Guatemala was ruled by dictators who supported coffee exports and gave huge concessions to the United Fruit Company, an American corporation, dispossessing many Indigenous communities of their land.1

  • Jorge Ubico, dictator from 1931, froze wages at very low levels, imposed harsh labour laws, and passed a law giving landowners immunity for any action to defend their property — described by historians as legalising murder.1
  • He gave the United Fruit Company 200,000 hectares of public land.1
  • The Second World War, fought in the name of democracy, inspired demands for change across Latin America.

In their own words

03

What happened?

In June 1944, a popular pro-democracy movement led by university students and labour organisations rose against Ubico.1

  • When Ubico suspended the constitution on 22 June, protesters — students, workers and many middle-class people — called a general strike and demanded its restoration.1
  • Ubico sent police to fire on protesters and declared martial law.1 On 25 June, soldiers fired on a peaceful march by women, killing the teacher María Chinchilla.
  • After a week of clashes and strikes, Ubico resigned at the end of June.1
  • His successor, General Federico Ponce, continued his policies, until he was overthrown on 20 October 1944 by a revolt of young officers and armed civilians — the "October Revolution".1

Elections in December 1944 were won in a landslide by Juan José Arévalo.1 A new constitution followed, along with a labour code, social security, a literacy campaign and free elections — though illiterate women were not given the vote, and communist parties were banned.1

04

What happened next?

  • Land reform: President Jacobo Árbenz, elected in 1950, introduced Decree 900 in 1952. Uncultivated land on large estates was expropriated, with compensation, and given to around 500,000 people, mostly Indigenous.1
  • The coup: the United Fruit Company lost some of its uncultivated land and lobbied the US government, which organised a coup in 1954, claiming Árbenz was a communist. Carlos Castillo Armas took power.1
  • Civil war: the land reform was reversed. A civil war from 1960 to 1996 followed, in which the army committed genocide against the Maya.1
05

What do others say?

At the time

Supporters saw the revolution as bringing democracy and justice to the poor. Landowners, the United Fruit Company and the US government portrayed the Árbenz government as communist.

Historians' debates

  • Communist threat? Historians such as Piero Gleijeses show that communists had influence in the Árbenz government, but that the reforms were capitalist land reform, not communism.
  • The role of the CIA and United Fruit: declassified documents confirm the CIA's central role in the coup.
  • Limits: some historians note that the revolution excluded illiterate women from voting and that many Indigenous people remained marginalised.
  • Violence: the 1944 revolution involved violence by the dictatorship and armed fighting in October; the land reform was accompanied by some land invasions and local violence, which opponents used to discredit it.

07

How is it remembered?

  • 20 October is a national holiday in Guatemala, Revolution Day.
  • 25 June is Teachers' Day in Guatemala, in memory of María Chinchilla.
  • In 2011, the Guatemalan government formally apologised to Árbenz's family for the coup.
08

Why does it matter?

The Guatemalan Revolution was one of the most hopeful moments in twentieth-century Latin America, brought about by ordinary students, teachers and workers. Its overthrow shaped the Cold War in the Americas.

  • It shows how popular movements can bring down dictators through strikes and protest.
  • It shows the power of foreign corporations and governments to reverse democratic change.
  • The 1954 coup radicalised a generation, including a young Argentine doctor in Guatemala, Ernesto "Che" Guevara.

What other events are like this?

  • 1954The Great Banana Strike — In the original 'banana republic', 40,000 workers walked out on the American fruit companies. After 69 days, they won higher pay and the right to a union.
  • 1820Atanasio Tzul and the Totonicapán Uprising — When Spanish officials kept collecting a tribute that had been abolished, K'iche' Maya communities refused to pay, drove out the mayor and set up their own government.
  • 1973The Thai Student Uprising of 14 October 1973 — When thirteen activists were arrested for handing out leaflets calling for a constitution, half a million people filled the streets of Bangkok and brought down a military dictatorship.
