The Sudanese Revolution

Protests over the price of bread grew into a nationwide revolution, led by professionals, young people and women. After thirty years, Omar al-Bashir was overthrown — but a massacre at the sit-in showed how hard the struggle would be.

When
December 2018 – August 2019
Where
Army headquarters sit-in, Khartoum, Sudan

See it on the map →

01

What is the background to this event?

Omar al-Bashir had ruled Sudan since a coup in 1989.

  • In 2018, the government removed wheat and electricity subsidies, and the price of bread and other basic goods soared.1
  • Women's and youth movements joined the early protests.1
02

Who were the key people?

  • The Sudanese Professionals Association and the Forces of Freedom and Change

    Coalitions that organised the protests.

  • Alaa Salah

    Student whose photo, leading a chant on top of a car, became a symbol of the revolution.

  • Omar al-Bashir

    President, ousted on 11 April 2019.

  • The Rapid Support Forces

    Paramilitary force responsible for the Khartoum massacre.

03

What happened?

From 19 December 2018, demonstrations broke out in several Sudanese cities over rising costs of living, and soon demanded Bashir's removal.1

  • In April 2019, protesters began a sit-in outside the army headquarters in Khartoum.1
  • A photo of Alaa Salah leading a chant went around the world.1
  • On 11 April, the army removed Bashir after thirty years in power.1
  • Protesters continued, demanding civilian rule from the Transitional Military Council.
  • On 3 June, the Rapid Support Forces and other security forces attacked the sit-in, killing 128 people and raping 70.1
  • The opposition answered with a general strike and civil disobedience.1

04

What happened next?

  • Transition: in August 2019, a military–civilian Sovereignty Council and a civilian prime minister, Abdalla Hamdok, took power; Sudan got its first female chief justice.1
  • Coup and war: the military seized power again in October 2021, and in April 2023 war broke out between the army and the Rapid Support Forces.
05

What do others say?

At the time

The military said it was protecting the nation; protesters demanded full civilian rule.1

Debates since

  • Unfinished revolution: many argue that the deal with the military left the generals too much power.1
  • Violence: the protesters were overwhelmingly non-violent; security forces killed many people, above all in the Khartoum massacre.1
06

The evidence

  • Photographs and videos taken by protesters.1
07

How is it remembered?

  • The sit-in's murals and songs became symbols of hope, even as Sudan fell into war.
08

Why does it matter?

The Sudanese revolution showed the power of mass non-violent protest — and the danger posed by armed forces that survive a revolution.

  • Women were at the forefront.

Questions to think about

  1. Why did a bread price rise become a revolution?
  2. Why were women so prominent in the protests?
  3. Why did the army remove Bashir, and what did it want?
  4. Why did the revolution's hopes collapse into war?
09

What other events are like this?

  • 1964Sudan's October Revolution — When police killed a student at a university debate about the war in the south, Sudanese professionals, workers and students launched a general strike that brought down a military dictatorship in days.
  • 1952Fatima Ahmed Ibrahim and the Sudanese Women's Union — A schoolgirl who led a strike for science lessons founded one of Africa's largest women's movements. In 1965 she became the first woman elected to Sudan's parliament.
  • 1919Egypt's 1919 Revolution — When Britain exiled the nationalist leader Saad Zaghlul, Egyptians of every class and religion rose up in strikes and demonstrations — and women marched in public for the first time.
