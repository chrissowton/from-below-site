The North-West Resistance

The Métis, threatened by surveyors who ignored their river farms, set up their own government under Louis Riel. Cree and Assiniboine fighters, starving after the loss of the buffalo, joined the resistance.

When
March – July 1885
Where
Batoche, Saskatchewan, Canada

See it on the map →

01

What is the background to this event?

After the Red River Resistance of 1869–70, many Métis moved west and founded settlements such as Batoche and Duck Lake on the South Saskatchewan River.1

  • From 1882, government surveyors divided the land into square townships, ignoring the long, narrow river lots of Métis farms.1
  • Cree and Assiniboine peoples faced hunger as the buffalo disappeared, and resented unfair treaties and the withholding of food by Indian agents.1
  • The Métis invited Louis Riel back from exile in the United States.1
02

Who were the key people?

  • Louis Riel

    Métis leader; hanged for treason in 1885.

  • Gabriel Dumont

    Métis military commander.

  • Big Bear and Poundmaker

    Cree chiefs; both imprisoned after the conflict.

  • Wandering Spirit

    Cree war chief who led the attack at Frog Lake; hanged.

In their own words

03

What happened?

On 18 March 1885, Riel declared a provisional government.1

  • The Métis won at Duck Lake and Fish Creek.1
  • On 2 April, a Cree party led by Wandering Spirit, against the wishes of Big Bear, attacked officials at Frog Lake, and several non-combatants were killed.1
  • The Canadian government sent 5,500 men west by the new railway.1
  • The rebel force — about 250 Métis and 250 First Nations fighters — was short of supplies, and government forces took Batoche in May.1
  • Cree and Assiniboine fighters won smaller battles but surrendered in June and July.1

04

What happened next?

  • Executions: Riel was convicted of treason and hanged, despite pleas for mercy from across Canada.1
  • Mass hanging: eight Indigenous men were hanged for killings outside the fighting — the largest mass hanging in Canadian history.1
  • Division: Riel's execution deepened the divide between French and English Canada.1
05

What do others say?

At the time

English Canada largely saw a treasonous rebellion; French Canada saw Riel's execution as an injustice.1

Debates since

  • Naming: many historians and organisations now use "resistance", since Indigenous nations governed the land before Canada claimed it.1
  • Violence on both sides: the killing of non-combatants at Frog Lake was condemned by Big Bear himself; the state's response included the mass hanging and the imprisonment of chiefs who had tried to keep the peace.12

07

How is it remembered?

  • Batoche is a National Historic Site, and Manitoba marks Louis Riel Day each February.
08

Why does it matter?

The resistance was a fight by the Métis and First Nations for land and survival in the face of settler colonialism.

  • Many scholars now call it a "resistance" rather than a "rebellion".1

Questions to think about

  1. Why did the land survey matter so much to the Métis?
  2. Why did Cree and Assiniboine people join the resistance?
  3. Why was Riel hanged when so many asked for mercy?
  4. Should it be called a rebellion or a resistance?
