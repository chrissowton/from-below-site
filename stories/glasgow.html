Housing & community

The Glasgow Rent Strikes

Working-class women in wartime Glasgow refused to pay rent rises and physically blocked evictions — forcing the government to introduce rent controls.

When
1915
Where
Govan, Partick and across Glasgow, Scotland

See it on the map →

01

What is the background to this event?

Glasgow before the First World War had some of the worst housing in Europe. Most working-class families lived in overcrowded tenements, many in one or two rooms, rented from private landlords through agents known as "factors".

The war made things much worse:

  • Thousands of workers moved to Glasgow to work in the shipyards and munitions factories on the Clyde, increasing the demand for housing.
  • Almost no new houses were being built.
  • Landlords raised rents sharply, and tenants who could not pay faced eviction — including the families of soldiers fighting at the front.

Women, who were usually responsible for paying the rent and running the household, were at the centre of the response. In February 1915 the Glasgow Women's Housing Association held its first meeting to resist rent increases.

02

Who were the key people?

  • Mary Barbour

    A Govan housewife and member of the Independent Labour Party who organised tenants' committees. The women who followed her became known as 'Mrs Barbour's Army'. She later became one of Glasgow's first women councillors.

  • Helen Crawfurd

    A suffragette and socialist who was one of the key organisers.

  • Agnes Dollan and Jessie Ferguson

    Leading figures in the Glasgow Women's Housing Association.

  • John Wheatley

    An Independent Labour Party councillor who supported the strikes and later, as a government minister, passed the 1924 Housing Act.

  • The shipyard and munitions workers

    Who stopped work and marched to the court in support of the tenants in November 1915.

03

What happened?

From spring 1915, tenants across Glasgow began refusing to pay rent increases, paying only the old amount.1 In Govan in March 1915, when a landlord tried to evict a woman over a rent debt of £1, hundreds of neighbours blocked the way into her home, preventing the eviction.1

The women developed effective tactics:

  • Committees in each street and tenement organised the refusal to pay.
  • When sheriff's officers arrived to carry out evictions, a lookout rang a bell or sounded a rattle, and women gathered to block the close (the entrance to the tenement). Some threw flour, water or worse at the officers.
  • Cards in windows declared "We are not removing". One famous banner read: "We are fighting against the Prussians of Partick", comparing landlords to the wartime enemy.

The movement spread across the city, and at its peak in November 1915 around 20,000 families were on rent strike.1 When a factor took a group of tenants — many of them munitions workers — to the Small Debt Court to have their rents deducted from their wages, thousands of shipyard and munitions workers stopped work and marched to the court in support. Faced with a huge demonstration outside, the authorities dropped the case.12

04

What happened next?

  • Rent control: on 25 November 1915 the government introduced the Increase of Rent and Mortgage Interest (War Restrictions) Act, which became law two days later. It froze rents across Britain at their pre-war level, unless landlords had made improvements.1
  • Council housing: the strikes helped convince governments that private landlords could not meet housing needs. The Housing Acts of 1919 and 1924 began large-scale council housing.
  • Political careers: Mary Barbour became one of Glasgow's first women councillors in 1920 and later a magistrate.
05

What do others say?

At the time

Landlords and factors argued that rising costs and taxes justified higher rents, and accused the strikers of undermining the war effort. Some critics portrayed the women as unpatriotic troublemakers.

Historians' debates

  • "Red Clydeside": historians debate whether the rent strikes were part of a revolutionary movement on the Clyde, or a more limited campaign for practical reform.
  • The role of women: older accounts focused on male socialist leaders; more recent research has stressed the leadership of ordinary women.
  • The decisive factor: some historians argue that the threat of industrial action by munitions workers — vital to the war — mattered more to the government than the tenants' own campaign.
06

The evidence

  • Photographs of women with placards and crowds blocking evictions.
  • Newspapers, including the socialist Forward and the Glasgow press.
  • Court records and government papers on the 1915 Act.
  • Memoirs, such as Helen Crawfurd's unpublished autobiography.
07

How is it remembered?

  • A statue of Mary Barbour leading the rent strikers was unveiled outside Govan subway station on International Women's Day, 8 March 2018, after a public campaign.
  • Blue plaques and community events commemorate the strikes.
  • Housing campaigners regularly invoke 1915 in debates about rents today.
08

Why does it matter?

The Glasgow rent strikes are one of the clearest examples in British history of working-class women organising in their own neighbourhoods to win a change in national law. They show that the home and the community, not just the workplace, can be a site of collective action.

  • They led directly to the first rent controls in Britain.
  • They demonstrated the power of combining community action with industrial solidarity.
  • Housing costs and tenants' rights remain urgent political questions today.

Questions to think about

  1. Why were women in particular at the centre of the rent strikes?
  2. How did the war help the strikers win?
  3. Should governments control rents? What are the arguments on each side?
  4. Are there movements for tenants' rights where you live?

What other events are like this?

  • 1879The Irish Land War and the First Boycott — Irish tenant farmers organised against evictions and high rents — and gave the world a new word, 'boycott'.
  • 1975Iceland's Women's Day Off — Around 90 per cent of Iceland's women stopped work — paid and unpaid — for a day, to show how much the country depended on them.
  • 1888The Matchwomen's Strike — About 1,400 women and girls walked out of a match factory, won their demands in three weeks, and founded the largest union of women in Britain.
