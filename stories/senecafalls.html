The vote & democracy

The Seneca Falls Convention

In a small chapel in upstate New York, some 300 people met to discuss 'the rights of woman'. Their Declaration of Sentiments demanded equality, and — after a heated debate — the vote.

When
19 – 20 July 1848
Where
Wesleyan Chapel, Seneca Falls, New York, United States

01

What is the background to this event?

In 1848, American women could not vote, and married women had few legal rights.

  • Quaker women in the area, along with Elizabeth Cady Stanton, planned a meeting while the speaker Lucretia Mott was visiting.1
  • Mott was famous for speaking in public at a time when most women were not allowed to.1

In their own words

03

What happened?

On 19 and 20 July 1848, the first women's rights convention met in the Wesleyan Chapel at Seneca Falls.1

  • Stanton and the Quaker women presented the Declaration of Sentiments and a list of resolutions.1
  • There was a heated debate on women's right to vote; many, including Mott, wanted it dropped.1
  • Frederick Douglass, the only African American present, argued for it, and the resolution was kept.1
  • 100 of the roughly 300 people present signed the declaration.1

04

What happened next?

  • More conventions: another convention met in Rochester two weeks later, and national conventions began in 1850.1
  • The vote: American women won the vote nationally in 1920 — though many Black women in the South were kept from voting for decades longer.
05

What do others say?

At the time

Local newspapers printed reports, some positive and others not; Mott and others saw it as one step in a longer struggle.1

Debates since

  • An origin story: historians such as Lisa Tetrault argue that Stanton and Susan B. Anthony later presented Seneca Falls as the beginning of the movement, downplaying others such as Lucy Stone.1
  • Violence: the convention was entirely peaceful.

Questions to ask of this source

  1. Why did the organisers want people to sign their names?
  2. Why might some signers later have removed their names?
  3. Who is not here — for example, Black women or working women?
07

How is it remembered?

  • The chapel is part of the Women's Rights National Historical Park.
08

Why does it matter?

Seneca Falls became a symbol of the start of the women's movement in the United States.

Questions to think about

  1. Why was demanding the vote so controversial even among the organisers?
  2. Why was Frederick Douglass's support important?
  3. Why did Seneca Falls later become the 'origin story' of the movement?
  4. Whose stories does that origin story leave out?
