The Tolpuddle Martyrs

Six farm labourers formed a society to resist wage cuts and were sentenced to transportation to Australia.

When
February – March 1834
Where
Tolpuddle, Dorset, England
Facts checked
2026-11-15

01

What is the background to this event?

In the early 1830s, agricultural labourers in the south of England were among the poorest workers in the country. The introduction of threshing machines had reduced winter work, and the "Swing Riots" of 1830–31, in which labourers had broken machines and burned ricks, had been suppressed with hangings and transportations.

In Tolpuddle, a small village in Dorset, wages had fallen to seven shillings a week and were due to fall again, to six — not enough to feed a family. The labourers asked for ten shillings.

The legal position of trade unions was ambiguous. The Combination Acts that had banned unions outright had been repealed in 1824–25, but the authorities remained deeply hostile to workers' organisations, and older laws remained on the statute book. In 1833 the labourers of Tolpuddle formed the Friendly Society of Agricultural Labourers, whose members swore an oath of loyalty and secrecy, as many friendly societies and lodges of the time did.

In their own words

03

What happened?

In February 1834 the six men were arrested. They were not charged with forming a union, which would not in itself have been illegal. Instead, the prosecution relied on the Unlawful Oaths Act 1797, a measure passed in the aftermath of the naval mutinies at Spithead and the Nore, which prohibited the administering of secret oaths.2

The case, reported as R v Lovelass and Others, was heard at Dorchester Assizes in March 1834 before Baron Williams.3 The evidence centred on the initiation ceremony: prosecution witnesses described being blindfolded, kneeling, and repeating an oath. One witness's account, recorded in the law report, conveys both the ritual and the witness's own uncertainty:

"We kissed a book when we were blinded. When we were on our knees, we repeated something that was said by somebody, but who said it I don't know."Evidence of a prosecution witness, R v Lovelass and Others (1834)3

All six were found guilty and in March 1834 sentenced to seven years' transportation to the Australian penal colonies.4 The severity of the sentence was widely understood as a warning to working people across the country.

The response was one of the first great campaigns of the British labour movement:

  • A petition calling for their release gathered about 800,000 signatures.5
  • On 21 April 1834 a huge procession of trade unionists marched from Copenhagen Fields in Islington through London to present a petition to the Home Office — one of the first successful mass marches in Britain.6
  • Radical MPs, including Thomas Wakley, raised the case repeatedly in Parliament.
04

What happened next?

In March 1836, under sustained public pressure, the Home Secretary Lord John Russell granted the men full pardons. Their return took time:

  • George Loveless reached England in June 1837.
  • James Loveless, the two Standfields and James Brine arrived at Plymouth in March 1838.
  • James Hammett, who had been sent to a different part of the colony, did not return until 1839.

Supporters raised funds to settle several of the men on farms in Essex. Most of them later emigrated to Canada, settling near London, Ontario, where George Loveless died in 1874. Since 1934 the Trades Union Congress has commemorated the martyrs, and the annual Tolpuddle Martyrs' Festival is held in the village every July.

05

What do others say?

At the time

The prosecution and the judge presented the case as a matter of upholding the law against secret oaths, which they associated with conspiracy and rebellion. Supporters of the government argued that the sentence was a necessary deterrent at a time of widespread rural unrest. The men's supporters saw it as a political prosecution aimed at stopping farm labourers from organising.

Historians' debates

  • Were they really 'martyrs' of trade unionism? Some historians point out that the Tolpuddle society was small and short-lived, and that the men's fame owes much to the later labour movement's need for founding heroes.
  • Was the conviction lawful? Legal historians have debated whether the 1797 Act was properly applied, given that many respectable societies, including Freemasons, also used oaths.
  • The role of religion: several of the men were Methodists, and historians have debated how far Methodism encouraged or restrained labour militancy.
06

The evidence

  • The law report of R v Lovelass and newspaper accounts of the trial at Dorchester.
  • George Loveless's own account, The Victims of Whiggery (1837), and the narrative of John Standfield — written to campaign as well as to record.
  • Petitions, Home Office correspondence and parliamentary debates about the case.
  • Convict records in Australia, which document where the men were sent and how they were treated.
07

How is it remembered?

  • The Trades Union Congress built a memorial shelter in the village in 1934, followed by six cottages for retired agricultural workers.
  • The Tolpuddle Martyrs Museum tells the story, and the annual festival each July attracts thousands of trade unionists.
  • The men are also commemorated in London, Ontario, where several of them settled, and in Australia.
  • The courtroom where they were tried, in Dorchester's Shire Hall, is now a museum.
08

Why does it matter?

The Tolpuddle Martyrs became the founding story of the British trade union movement. Their case demonstrated how the law could be used to suppress workers' organisation, but also how a mass campaign of ordinary people — signing petitions, marching, raising funds — could force a government to reverse an injustice.

  • It established the principle, now widely accepted, that working people have a right to combine to defend their wages and conditions.
  • It showed the importance of solidarity across trades and regions: the London march was organised largely by urban workers on behalf of rural labourers they had never met.
  • The memory of the case remains a reference point in debates about trade union law today.

What other events are like this?

  • 1888The Matchwomen's Strike — About 1,400 women and girls walked out of a match factory, won their demands in three weeks, and founded the largest union of women in Britain.
  • 1912The Bread and Roses Strike — More than twenty thousand migrant textile workers, speaking dozens of languages, shut down the mills of Lawrence and won.
  • 1966The Wave Hill Walk-Off — Gurindji stockmen and their families walked off a British-owned cattle station. What began as a strike over pay became the foundation of the Aboriginal land rights movement.
