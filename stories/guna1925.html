The Guna Revolution

When Panama's government banned Guna women's traditional dress and tried to impose its culture by force, the Guna of the San Blas islands rose up and declared independence. They won the autonomy they still have today.

When
February 1925
Where
Ustupu and Ailigandi, Guna Yala, Panama
Facts checked
2026-11-16

See it on the map →

01

What is the background to this event?

After Panama became independent in 1903, the government tried to bring the Guna of the San Blas islands under its control.1

  • It ran an assimilation programme, sent Guna children to be educated in Western ways, and stationed police on the islands.1
  • Laws banned Guna women from wearing their mola blouses, nose rings and beaded bindings, and forced them into Western clothes.1
  • The Guna saw this as a threat to their survival as a people.1
02

Who were the key people?

  • Nele Kantule

    Chief of the island of Ustupu and a leader of the revolution.

  • Simral Colman

    Chief of Ailigandi and a leader of the revolution.

  • Richard Marsh

    American explorer who advised the Guna leaders.

03

What happened?

On 12 February 1925, a conference began at Ailigandi and proclaimed the Republic of Tule.1

  • The Guna leaders co-ordinated attacks on the islands most controlled by Panamanian officials and police.1
  • The fighting left 27 dead.1
  • The United States mediated between the Guna and Panama.1
  • The Guna withdrew their declaration of independence, and Panama agreed not to threaten Guna traditions.1
04

What happened next?

  • Autonomy: the Guna won recognition of their territory, which became the self-governing region of Guna Yala.
  • Culture: molas and Guna customs survived, and molas are now a symbol of Panama.
05

Why does it matter?

The Guna Revolution is one of the most successful Indigenous revolts of the twentieth century.

  • It was fought to defend culture, dress and identity.
  • The Guna won recognition of their territory, which became the self-governing region of Guna Yala.
  • Their molas and customs survived, and molas are now a symbol of Panama.

What do others say?How it was seen at the time, and where historians disagree

At the time

Panama saw an uprising stirred by a foreigner; the Guna saw a defence of their way of life.1

Debates since

  • Outside influence: historians such as James Howe stress that the Guna planned the revolution themselves, though Marsh helped.2
  • Violence on both sides: Guna fighters killed Panamanian police and some Guna who had sided with them; Panamanian rule before the revolt was enforced with violence and forced assimilation.12

Reading it

Nele Kantule was photographed by a Swedish ethnographer who visited two years after the revolution and worked with Guna leaders to record their history. A Guna leader may have seen a foreign record as a way to strengthen the Guna's claims. An outsider's camera still shows only part of the picture — and who should control such images now is a fair question.
