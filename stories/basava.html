Basava and the Sharanas of Kalyana

A twelfth-century poet and minister taught that every worker's labour was sacred and that caste did not matter. When his followers celebrated a marriage between a Brahmin's daughter and a cobbler's son, the backlash was deadly.

When
c. 1150 – 1168
Where
Kalyana (Basavakalyan), Karnataka, India
Facts checked
2026-11-16

See it on the map →

01

What is the background to this event?

In twelfth-century India, society was divided by caste. Untouchable and low-caste people were excluded from temples, learning and many occupations.

  • Basava (1131–1196) was born into a Brahmin family in what is now northern Karnataka.1
  • He became a minister at the court of King Bijjala II of the Kalachuri dynasty at Kalyana.1
  • He was part of a Shiva-centred devotional (bhakti) movement whose followers became known as the Lingayats.1

03

What happened?

Basava wrote short poems in Kannada, the vachanas, that rejected caste, gender discrimination, superstition and ritual.1

  • He taught that anyone could reach salvation regardless of caste, and that all manual labour was equally worthy.1
  • He gave every devotee a personal linga, worn on the body, so that no temple or priest was needed.1
  • He founded the Anubhava Mantapa, an assembly where men and women of all backgrounds discussed spiritual and social questions.1
  • Basava insisted on non-violence.1
  • According to Lingayat tradition, when the movement celebrated a marriage between the daughter of a Brahmin, Madhuvarasa, and the son of a cobbler, Haralayya, the king had the fathers punished and killed.
  • Unrest followed. Many Sharanas fled Kalyana, and King Bijjala was assassinated.

04

What happened next?

  • Lingayats: the movement grew into the Lingayat community, today one of the largest in Karnataka.
  • The vachanas are classics of Kannada literature.
05

What do others say?

At the time

Orthodox Brahmins saw the Sharanas as a threat to social order; the Sharanas saw caste as a denial of true devotion.1

Debates since

  • The sources: the story of the marriage and the crisis comes from later hagiographies, and dates are uncertain.1
  • Violence: Basava preached non-violence; according to tradition, the violence came from the king's punishment and from revenge after it.
  • Founder? Modern scholars see Basava as reviving an existing tradition rather than founding it.1
06

The evidence

  • The vachanas of Basava and other Sharanas.2

07

How is it remembered?

  • A statue of Basava stands beside the Thames in London, unveiled in 2015.
08

Why does it matter?

Basava's movement was one of the earliest organised challenges to caste in India, eight centuries before Ambedkar.

  • It valued work, equality and the voices of women.

Questions to think about

  1. Why did Basava insist that all labour was sacred?
  2. Why was an inter-caste marriage so threatening?
  3. Why did a movement against caste later become a caste community itself?

What other events are like this?

  • 1450Kabir, the Weaver Poet — A weaver from Varanasi mocked the priests and mullahs alike and sang that God belonged to no caste or religion. Threatened by both Hindus and Muslims in his lifetime, he was claimed by both after his death.
  • 1927The Mahad Satyagraha — B. R. Ambedkar led thousands of Dalits to drink from a public water tank that caste Hindus had forbidden them to touch — a founding moment of the struggle against untouchability.
  • 1972The Dalit Panthers — Young Dalit poets in Bombay, inspired by the American Black Panthers, called India's 25th Independence Day a 'Black Independence Day' and demanded an end to caste oppression.
