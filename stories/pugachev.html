Pugachev's Rebellion

A Cossack deserter claiming to be the murdered Tsar Peter III proclaimed the end of serfdom. Cossacks, serfs, factory workers and Bashkirs rose in the largest peasant war in Russian history.

When
1773 – 1775
Where
The Volga and Ural regions, Russia
Facts checked
2026-11-16

01

What is the background to this event?

In eighteenth-century Russia, most peasants were serfs, bound to the land and to the nobles who owned them. Under Catherine the Great, who seized the throne in 1762 after the murder of her husband, Peter III, the power of nobles over their serfs grew even stronger.1

  • Serfs could be bought and sold, flogged and sent to the army at their owners' will. Many nobles returned to their estates after 1762 and imposed harsher rules on their peasants.1
  • Workers in the mines and ironworks of the Urals, many of them serfs assigned to factories, laboured in terrible conditions.
  • The Yaik Cossacks, who lived on the frontier, resented the loss of their traditional freedoms. Non-Russian peoples such as the Bashkirs, Tatars and Kalmyks resented the seizure of their land and forced conversion.
  • Old Believers, religious dissenters persecuted by the state church, were also hostile to the government.

Among the peasants, a legend spread that Peter III had not really died, and would return to free them.1

In their own words

03

What happened?

In 1773, Yemelyan Pugachev appeared among the Yaik Cossacks, claiming to be Peter III. He won the support of Cossacks who had rebelled against the government the year before, and launched an insurrection in September 1773.1

The rebellion spread rapidly:1

  • Pugachev recruited Cossacks, Russian peasants and factory workers, Tatars, Bashkirs and Chuvash. The Bashkir leader Salavat Yulaev joined him.
  • He set up his own court, army and administration, modelled on Catherine's, and besieged the city of Orenburg for six months.
  • At its height, the rebellion controlled most of the territory between the Volga and the Ural Mountains. In July 1774 the rebels captured and burned much of the city of Kazan.
  • In July 1774, Pugachev issued a manifesto granting the serfs their freedom and their land, freedom from taxes and recruitment, and calling on them to punish the nobles.

As the rebellion moved along the Volga, peasants rose against their landlords. Thousands of nobles, officials, officers and priests, and members of their families, were killed by the rebels. Government forces in turn carried out mass executions; gallows were floated down the Volga on rafts to terrify the population.3 In August 1774 Pugachev's army was defeated near Tsaritsyn. He was betrayed by his own Cossack followers, handed over to the authorities, and taken to Moscow in a cage. He was executed in Moscow on 10 January 1775.1

04

What happened next?

  • Erasing the memory: Catherine ordered that the rebellion be "consigned to eternal oblivion". The Yaik river was renamed the Ural, and the Yaik Cossacks became the Ural Cossacks.
  • Tighter control: in 1775 Catherine reorganised local government, increasing the number of provinces and strengthening the power of the nobility in the countryside.
  • Serfdom continued: far from weakening serfdom, the rebellion was followed by its extension to new territories. Serfdom in Russia was only abolished in 1861.
05

What do others say?

At the time

Catherine and the nobility saw the rebellion as a barbaric revolt by an impostor and a criminal. For many peasants and Cossacks, Pugachev was the true tsar who had come to free them.

Historians' debates

  • Revolution or rebellion? Soviet historians presented Pugachev's revolt as a "peasant war" and a forerunner of the Russian Revolution; Western historians have often stressed its traditional, monarchist character — the rebels sought a good tsar, not a republic.
  • The violence: the rebels' killing of landowners and their families, including women and children, and the government's mass executions both reached extreme levels. Alexander Pushkin, who studied the rebellion, wrote of "the Russian revolt, senseless and merciless".
  • Non-Russian peoples: historians increasingly emphasise the role of the Bashkirs and other non-Russian peoples, for whom the rebellion was also a struggle against colonisation.

07

How is it remembered?

  • Pushkin's novel The Captain's Daughter (1836) is set during the rebellion.
  • Vasily Perov's painting Pugachev's Judgement (1879) shows the rebel leader sitting in judgement on nobles.
  • In Bashkortostan, Salavat Yulaev is commemorated with statues, a town named after him and a famous ice hockey team.
08

Why does it matter?

Pugachev's Rebellion was the largest peasant revolt in the history of the Russian Empire. It showed the depth of the anger of serfs, workers and colonised peoples against a system that treated them as property, and their hope for freedom and land.

  • It united very different groups — Russians and non-Russians, Orthodox and Muslim — against the nobility and the state.
  • Its memory haunted the Russian ruling class for a century.
  • Salavat Yulaev is honoured today as the national hero of Bashkortostan.

What other events are like this?

  • 1381The Peasants' Revolt — Tens of thousands of villagers and townspeople marched on London to demand an end to serfdom. For a few days, the government of England was at their mercy.
  • 1780The Rebellion of Túpac Amaru II — An Indigenous leader and his wife, Micaela Bastidas, led the largest rebellion in Spain's American empire, demanding an end to forced labour and abuse.
  • 184 CEThe Yellow Turban Rebellion — Inspired by a Daoist healer who promised a new age of 'Great Peace', hundreds of thousands of peasants rose against the Han dynasty — a revolt that helped to bring the empire down.
