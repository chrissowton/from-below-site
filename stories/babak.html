Babak Khorramdin and the Khurramites

From a mountain fortress in Azerbaijan, a former cowherd led a religious and social movement against the Abbasid caliphs for more than twenty years.

When
c. 816 – 837
Where
Badhdh (Babak Fort), near Kaleybar, Iran
Facts checked
2026-11-16

See it on the map →

01

What is the background to this event?

Iran had been conquered by the Arabs in the seventh century, and was ruled by the Abbasid caliphs from Baghdad.1

  • The Khurramites ("those of the joyous religion") followed a faith that is considered an offshoot of the teachings of Mazdak.1
  • Babak, born in the Ardabil area in about 795, worked as a cowherd and as a servant before joining the movement.1

In their own words

03

What happened?

From about 816, Babak led a rebellion from his fortress of Badhdh in Azerbaijan.1

  • The revolt spread to western and central Iran and lasted more than twenty years.1
  • It called for a return to the glories of Iran's past before the Arab conquest.1
  • In 835–836, the caliph sent his general al-Afshīn, who rebuilt fortresses and protected his supply lines.1
  • Babak's fortress fell in 837. He fled, but was handed over by an Armenian prince.1
  • In 838, he was executed: his hands and feet were cut off and he was gibbeted.1

04

What happened next?

  • The end of the Khurramites: the movement declined after his death, though it survived in places.
  • Memory: he was little known outside academia until the twentieth century.1
05

What do others say?

At the time

Muslim chroniclers were hostile, and spread stories of his low birth that historians now treat as fabrications.1

Debates since

  • Social revolution? Because the Khurramites followed Mazdakite ideas, some saw Babak as a proto-socialist; the sources on his beliefs are mostly hostile.12
  • Violence on both sides: chroniclers claimed his forces killed huge numbers of people, figures historians treat with caution; the caliph's armies killed many of his followers, and his execution was deliberately brutal.1
  • Nation: Soviet Azerbaijan proclaimed him a national hero, and Iranian nationalists also claim him.1
06

The evidence

  • The histories of al-Ṭabarī and other Arabic writers.
  • The ruins of Babak Fort.

07

How is it remembered?

  • Babak Fort near Kaleybar attracts visitors who come to remember him.
08

Why does it matter?

Babak's revolt was one of the longest challenges to the Abbasid caliphate, combining resistance to foreign rule with radical religious ideas.

  • His story has been claimed by many later movements.

Questions to think about

  1. Why did the Khurramites rebel against the caliphate?
  2. Why could Babak hold out for so long?
  3. How do hostile sources shape what we know about him?
  4. Why have both Iranians and Azerbaijanis claimed him as a national hero?
