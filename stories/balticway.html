The Baltic Way

Around two million people joined hands in a human chain 675 kilometres long, across Estonia, Latvia and Lithuania, to demand freedom from the Soviet Union.

When
23 August 1989
Where
Tallinn – Riga – Vilnius

See it on the map →

01

What is the background to this event?

Estonia, Latvia and Lithuania won their independence after the First World War. But on 23 August 1939, Nazi Germany and the Soviet Union signed the Molotov–Ribbentrop Pact. A secret protocol divided Eastern Europe into "spheres of influence", placing the Baltic states in the Soviet sphere.1

  • In June 1940, the Soviet Union occupied the three countries and annexed them. After German occupation during the war, Soviet rule returned in 1944.
  • Tens of thousands of people were deported to Siberia, and partisans known as the "Forest Brothers" fought on into the 1950s. Russian-speaking settlers were moved in, and Russian was promoted at the expense of the national languages.
  • The Soviet Union insisted that the three states had joined it voluntarily, and for decades denied that the secret protocols existed.1

From 1987, under Mikhail Gorbachev's policies of glasnost (openness) and perestroika (restructuring), protests became possible. Mass movements — the Popular Front of Estonia (Rahvarinne), the Popular Front of Latvia, and Sąjūdis in Lithuania — were founded in 1988. Huge song festivals and rallies became known as the "Singing Revolution".1

02

Who were the key people?

  • The Popular Fronts and Sąjūdis

    The three pro-independence movements that planned and organised the Baltic Way.

  • Vytautas Landsbergis

    A musicologist and chairman of Sąjūdis, who led Lithuania's declaration of independence in 1990.

  • Edgar Savisaar and Dainis Īvāns

    Leaders of the Estonian and Latvian Popular Fronts.

  • The two million

    Around a quarter of the population of the three countries, of all ages, who joined the chain.

03

What happened?

The Baltic independence movements planned a huge demonstration for 23 August 1989, the fiftieth anniversary of the Molotov–Ribbentrop Pact, to draw the world's attention to the illegality of the Soviet occupation.1 A week before, on 18 August, a Soviet commission had for the first time admitted that the secret protocols were genuine; on 22 August, a commission of the Lithuanian Supreme Soviet declared the 1940 occupation illegal.1

On the evening of 23 August 1989, around two million people joined hands to form a human chain stretching 675 kilometres, from Tallinn through Riga to Vilnius, across the three republics:1

  • The demonstrators linked hands for fifteen minutes at 7 p.m. local time. Special radio broadcasts and aeroplanes helped coordinate the chain so that there were no gaps.
  • People held candles and the pre-war national flags, decorated with black ribbons in memory of the victims of Soviet terror — the deportees, the Forest Brothers and political prisoners.
  • In Vilnius, about 5,000 people gathered in Cathedral Square with candles, singing national songs.
  • Solidarity demonstrations were held in Western cities, including outside the Soviet embassy in Bonn.

The Popular Fronts issued a joint declaration condemning the Molotov–Ribbentrop Pact as a crime and urging that it be declared "null and void from the moment of signing".1 On 26 August, the Central Committee of the Soviet Communist Party responded with a harshly worded statement warning against "nationalist, extremist groups" with "anti-socialist and anti-Soviet" aims — but took no action against the demonstrators.1

04

What happened next?

  • Lithuania first: on 11 March 1990, seven months after the Baltic Way, Lithuania became the first Soviet republic to declare independence. Estonia and Latvia began a transition to independence.
  • Violence: in January 1991, Soviet troops attacked the television tower in Vilnius, killing fourteen civilians, and attacked barricades in Riga. Unarmed crowds defended their parliaments.
  • Independence: after the failed coup in Moscow in August 1991, the independence of all three states was recognised internationally, and in September 1991 by the Soviet Union itself.
  • Europe: Estonia, Latvia and Lithuania joined the European Union and NATO in 2004.
05

What do others say?

At the time

Soviet official media denounced the demonstration as nationalist hysteria and claimed that the Baltic states had joined the Soviet Union voluntarily. Some Russian-speaking residents of the Baltic states feared what independence would mean for them.

Historians' debates

  • How many took part? Organisers estimated around two million participants; Soviet sources gave lower figures for each republic. Aerial footage shows an almost continuous chain.
  • People power or Soviet weakness? Historians debate how far Baltic independence was won by the popular movements, and how far it resulted from the collapse of Soviet power and the decisions of leaders in Moscow.
  • Citizenship: after independence, Estonia and Latvia restricted automatic citizenship to the descendants of pre-1940 citizens, leaving many Russian-speakers stateless — a policy that remains controversial.
06

The evidence

  • Film and photographs taken from the ground and from aeroplanes and helicopters.
  • The documents of the Popular Fronts and Sąjūdis, inscribed on UNESCO's Memory of the World Register in 2009.
  • Soviet press reports and Communist Party statements.
  • Personal memories collected by museums and archives in the three countries.
07

How is it remembered?

  • 23 August is marked across the European Union as the European Day of Remembrance for Victims of Stalinism and Nazism (Black Ribbon Day).
  • Anniversaries of the Baltic Way are marked with human chains, concerts and exhibitions.
  • Human chains inspired by the Baltic Way have been organised elsewhere, including in Catalonia in 2013 and Hong Kong in 2019.
08

Why does it matter?

The Baltic Way was one of the most remarkable non-violent protests in history. By joining hands across three countries, ordinary people turned a historical injustice — a secret deal between two dictators — into a powerful moral demand for freedom that the world could not ignore.

  • It showed the strength of peaceful mass action against an authoritarian superpower.
  • It linked the three nations' struggles together, and helped bring about the restoration of their independence.
  • It became a model for later human-chain protests around the world.

Questions to think about

  1. Why do you think the organisers chose a human chain as their form of protest?
  2. Why was the fiftieth anniversary of the Molotov–Ribbentrop Pact so important?
  3. Why did the Soviet Union not use force against the demonstrators in 1989?
  4. How much can peaceful protest achieve against a powerful state?
09

What other events are like this?

  • 1956The Hungarian Revolution of 1956 — Students, workers and soldiers rose against Hungary's Soviet-backed dictatorship and pulled down Stalin's statue. For twelve days they won — until Soviet tanks returned.
  • 1980The Gdańsk Shipyard Strike and Solidarity — A strike over the sacking of a crane operator grew into the first independent trade union in the Soviet bloc, with ten million members.
  • 1986The People Power Revolution — More than two million unarmed Filipinos filled a Manila highway to protect rebel soldiers and end Ferdinand Marcos's twenty-year rule.
