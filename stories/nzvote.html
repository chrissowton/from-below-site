The vote & democracy

Women Win the Vote in New Zealand

A nationwide petition campaign made New Zealand the first self-governing country in which all women could vote in parliamentary elections.

When
19 September 1893
Where
Wellington, Aotearoa New Zealand
Facts checked
2026-11-15

01

What is the background to this event?

In the 1880s, women in New Zealand were running households, farms and businesses, paying taxes and raising families, yet were excluded from voting for the Parliament that made the laws governing their lives. Many first became politically active through the Women's Christian Temperance Union (WCTU), founded in New Zealand in 1885, which campaigned against the harm caused by alcohol, including domestic violence and poverty.

The temperance campaigners soon concluded that they would make little progress without the vote. Their arguments were set out in a series of pamphlets, including Kate Sheppard's Is It Right? (1892), which asked:

"Is it right that while the gambler, the drunkard, and even the wife-beater has a vote, earnest, educated and refined women are denied it?"Kate Sheppard, Is It Right? (1892)

The campaign also drew in trade unionists such as Harriet Morison, who organised women in the Dunedin clothing trade, and Māori women, who were simultaneously seeking a voice in Te Kotahitanga, the Māori Parliament.

In their own words

03

What happened?

Between 1891 and 1893 the campaigners organised a series of petitions to Parliament, collected door to door and at public meetings throughout the country:2

  • 1891: more than 9,000 signatures.
  • 1892: almost 20,000 signatures.
  • 1893: nearly 32,000 signatures — close to a quarter of the adult European female population of the colony. The sheets were glued together into a roll about 270 metres long.

The 1893 petition was presented to Parliament and a new electoral bill passed the House of Representatives with a large majority. Its fate in the appointed upper house, the Legislative Council, was uncertain. The Premier, Richard Seddon, opposed the measure and tried to secure its defeat by persuading a councillor to change his vote. The manoeuvre backfired: two other councillors, angered by it, switched sides, and the bill passed by 20 votes to 18 on 8 September 1893.2

Eighteen legislative councillors then petitioned the new Governor, Lord Glasgow, to withhold his assent. On 19 September 1893 he signed the Electoral Act 1893, giving all women aged 21 or over, Māori and Pākehā alike, the right to vote in parliamentary elections.2 Kate Sheppard wrote:

"The news is being flashed far and wide, and before our earth has revolved on her axis every civilized community within the reach of the electric wires will have received the tidings that civic freedom has been granted to the women of New Zealand."Kate Sheppard, statement of September 18933

Women voted for the first time just over ten weeks later, in the general election of 28 November 1893; the Māori electorates voted on 20 December.2 The same year, Elizabeth Yates became mayor of Onehunga, the first woman to hold such an office anywhere in the British Empire.

04

What happened next?

Winning the vote was a beginning rather than an end:

  • Women were not permitted to stand for Parliament until 1919, and the first woman MP, Elizabeth McCombs, was not elected until 1933. The first Māori woman MP, Iriaka Rātana, was elected in 1949.
  • In 1896 Kate Sheppard became founding president of the National Council of Women of New Zealand, which campaigned on issues including equal pay, divorce law reform and the rights of children.
  • New Zealand's example was widely cited by suffrage campaigners in Australia, Britain and the United States in the decades that followed.
05

What do others say?

At the time

Opponents argued that women belonged in the home, that they were not interested in politics, and that their votes would simply double those of their husbands. The liquor industry campaigned hard against suffrage because it feared women would vote for prohibition. Some politicians, including the Premier Richard Seddon, opposed the measure while publicly professing support.

Historians' debates

  • Why New Zealand first? Historians have pointed to the colony's small size, its Liberal government, the strength of the temperance movement, and the relative absence of entrenched institutions such as an elected upper house.
  • Gift or victory? Older accounts sometimes presented the vote as granted by enlightened male politicians; Patricia Grimshaw and others have emphasised the scale of women's own campaigning.
  • Race and empire: some historians have questioned the celebratory story by pointing out that Māori women's political aspirations, expressed through Te Kotahitanga, had different aims from those of the mainly Pākehā suffrage movement.

The evidence

  • The 1893 petition itself, with the names and addresses of nearly 32,000 women — now searchable online, and inscribed on the UNESCO Memory of the World register.
  • Parliamentary debates recorded in Hansard.
  • Pamphlets such as Kate Sheppard's Is It Right? and the WCTU's journal, The White Ribbon.
  • Records of Te Kotahitanga, including Meri Te Tai Mangakāhia's 1893 address.

07

How is it remembered?

  • Kate Sheppard appears on the New Zealand ten-dollar note.
  • The Kate Sheppard National Memorial in Christchurch was unveiled in 1993 for the centenary.
  • 19 September is marked each year as Suffrage Day, and many people wear white camellias, the symbol of the movement.
  • The petition is on permanent display in the He Tohu exhibition at the National Library.
08

Why does it matter?

New Zealand's victory demonstrates that democratic rights can be won not only through dramatic confrontations but through years of patient, determined organisation: drafting pamphlets, holding meetings, and collecting signatures one household at a time. The petition itself, now held by Archives New Zealand and inscribed on the UNESCO Memory of the World register, is a remarkable record of thousands of ordinary women putting their names to a political demand.

  • It made New Zealand the first self-governing country in which all adult women could vote in national elections.
  • It included Māori women from the outset, unlike many later franchise reforms elsewhere.
  • It shows how a social movement (temperance) can grow into a broader campaign for political equality.
