The Spanish Revolution and Civil War

When generals rose against Spain's elected government, workers' militias stopped them in the great cities — and in much of the Republic, workers took over the farms and factories. Three years of war followed, ending in Franco's dictatorship.

When
July 1936 – April 1939
Where
Barcelona, Madrid, Aragon and across Spain

01

What is the background to this event?

In February 1936, the left-wing Popular Front narrowly won Spain's general election.1

  • Spain was deeply divided. In 1935, the reversal of land reform had led to evictions, sackings and violence against farmworkers in the south.1
  • Between February and July 1936, some 300 to 400 people died in political violence, and hundreds of churches were damaged or destroyed.1
  • The anarcho-syndicalist union, the CNT, had about 1.5 million members, and the socialist union, the UGT, almost as many.2
  • Officers began plotting a coup soon after the election. In July, Falangists killed a police officer in Madrid; in revenge, members of a loyal police corps killed the monarchist politician José Calvo Sotelo.13

In their own words

03

What happened?

The military rising began in Spanish Morocco on 17 July 1936 and spread to garrisons on the mainland the next day.6

  • In most big cities, it failed. In Barcelona, anarchist militias and loyal police built barricades and defeated the rebel troops on 19–20 July.3 In Madrid, workers' militias and loyal security forces stormed the Montaña barracks.7
  • The unions called a general strike and armed themselves. Across much of the Republican zone, local committees took power, and farms, factories, transport and even barber shops and restaurants were taken over by those who worked in them. In Catalonia, as much as 75% of the economy came under workers' control.2
  • Militias of volunteers, men and women, left for the front. The first left Barcelona for Aragon on 24 July.2
  • Behind both sides' lines, there was terrible violence. In the Republican zone, revolutionaries killed people they saw as enemies, including 6,832 Roman Catholic priests.8 In the rebel zone, the Nationalists carried out mass executions of trade unionists, left-wing supporters and others.9
  • Fascist Italy and Nazi Germany sent troops and aircraft to the Nationalists; the Soviet Union and Mexico supported the Republic. Britain and France chose non-intervention.1
  • Within the Republic, the revolution was contested. In May 1937, anarchists and the anti-Stalinist POUM fought the government and the Communists in the streets of Barcelona.10 In August 1937, the government sent troops to dissolve the anarchist council of Aragon.11
  • The Nationalists slowly conquered the Republican zone. Barcelona fell in January 1939, and nearly 500,000 people fled to France.12 Franco declared victory on 1 April 1939.1
04

What happened next?

  • Dictatorship: Franco ruled Spain until his death in November 1975. The anarchist movement, among others, was outlawed.113
  • Repression after the war: some 50,000 more people were executed after the war ended.9
  • Exile: of the refugees who reached France, about 300,000 returned to Spain within months; 160,000–180,000 stayed in France, and about 30,000 went on to other countries, especially Mexico.12
  • The cost: demographers estimate that the war caused about 346,000 excess deaths between 1936 and 1939, and 540,000 including the post-war years to 1942.1
05

What do others say?

At the time

Around the world, the war was seen in very different ways: as a class struggle, a religious struggle, a fight between democracy and dictatorship, revolution and counter-revolution, or communism and fascism.1

Debates since

  • War or revolution? Communists and moderate republicans argued that the revolution should wait until the war was won; anarchists and the POUM argued that the revolution was what people were fighting for.102
  • Violence on both sides: estimates of killings in the Republican zone range from 38,000 to about 111,000; estimates of executions in the Nationalist zone during the war range from under 50,000 to 200,000 (for example, Hugh Thomas 75,000, Julián Casanova 100,000, Paul Preston at least 130,000). Historians including Preston, Graham, Beevor and Thomas argue that the Nationalist killings were organised policy, while those in the Republican zone grew out of the collapse of authority.891
  • The cost of the war: estimates of combat deaths range from 100,000 to 700,000, depending on what is counted.1

Made by, and when
Madrid, May 1936

The first issue of Mujeres Libres ('Free Women'), published two months before the war began by three anarchist women who argued that women's liberation and social revolution must go together. By 1938 the organisation had about 30,000 members, running classes, childcare centres and maternity care in the Republican zone.

07

How is it remembered?

  • After Franco's death, Spain's transition to democracy was based on a "pact of forgetting": the 1977 Amnesty Law meant no prosecutions, and mass graves were not opened.14
  • A Historical Memory Law in 2007 condemned the Franco regime's repression. In 2019, Franco's remains were removed from the Valley of the Fallen, renamed the Valley of Cuelgamuros in 2022.1415
  • The Democratic Memory Law of 2022 made the state responsible for finding and identifying people buried in unmarked graves. The far-right party Vox has pledged to repeal it, and the conservative People's Party has criticised it.14
08

Why does it matter?

For many on the left around the world, Spain was where ordinary people both fought fascism and tried to build a new society from below. It remains one of the most argued-over events of the 20th century.12

Open questions

  1. Why did the rising fail in the big cities but succeed elsewhere?
  2. What did workers' control of farms and factories mean in practice?
  3. Should the Republic have put winning the war before the revolution, or were the two inseparable?
  4. Why did Britain and France choose non-intervention, and what difference did it make?

What other events are like this?

  • 1919The Makhnovshchina: Anarchist Ukraine — In the chaos of revolution and civil war, peasants in southern Ukraine tried to build a society without landlords, bosses or a state. They fought Germans, Whites and Reds — and were finally crushed by the Bolsheviks.
  • 1931The Barcelona Rent Strike of 1931 — With unemployment soaring after the 1929 Exposition, 100,000 working-class families in Barcelona stopped paying rent. When bailiffs carried their furniture into the street, neighbours carried it back in.
  • 1936The Battle of Cable Street — When the British Union of Fascists tried to march through the Jewish East End, tens of thousands of local people blocked the way.
