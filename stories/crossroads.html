Crossroads: The Squatters Who Defied Apartheid

Under apartheid, Black families were not allowed to live together in Cape Town. At Crossroads, thousands built homes anyway — and women led the fight to stop the bulldozers.

When
1975 – 1986
Where
Crossroads, Cape Town, South Africa
Facts checked
2026-11-16

See it on the map →

01

What is the background to this event?

Under apartheid, the government tried to control where Black South Africans could live. The Western Cape was declared a "Coloured labour preference area", and Black Africans were only allowed there as migrant workers.

  • Black men were housed in single-sex hostels, and their wives and children were supposed to stay in the impoverished "homelands", such as the Transkei and the Ciskei.
  • Many families defied these "influx control" laws, building shacks on the edge of Cape Town so that they could live together.
  • In the 1970s, farm workers who had been told to leave a nearby farm were told to move to "the crossroads". By 1977, around 18,000 people lived there.1
02

Who were the key people?

  • The Crossroads Women's Committee

    Women residents who organised resistance to the demolitions and won support inside and outside the settlement.

  • The Men's Committee

    A residents' committee that also opposed the removals.

  • The Black Sash

    An organisation of mostly white women who opposed apartheid and supported the residents with legal advice and publicity.

  • Johnson Ngxobongwana

    Head of the residents' committee, who later allied with the police against the United Democratic Front.

  • P. W. Botha and Piet Koornhof

    The prime minister and the minister responsible for Black affairs, who planned and then postponed the demolition.

03

What happened?

The authorities considered Crossroads temporary, and orders to evict the residents and dismantle the settlement were issued in 1975.1 Residents refused to leave.

  • The Women's Committee and the Men's Committee organised the resistance. The Women's Committee was particularly successful at organising and winning support within the community and beyond.1
  • The Black Sash and other supporters gave legal help and publicised the families' plight.1
  • In 1978, Prime Minister P. W. Botha led a campaign to demolish Crossroads. Amid national and international opposition, Botha and his minister Piet Koornhof agreed to "indefinitely delay" the demolitions.1
  • Crossroads was declared an "emergency camp", which obliged the city council to provide basic services.1

The "Save Crossroads" campaign became an international symbol of resistance to apartheid's laws on where people could live.

04

What happened next?

  • Division and violence: the victory was followed by bitter conflict within the community. Opponents accused the committee leader, Johnson Ngxobongwana, of favouritism and of rewarding his followers.1
  • The witdoeke: from 1983, violence spread into neighbouring settlements. Older Crossroads residents, resentful of the rising influence of the anti-apartheid United Democratic Front, formed vigilante groups known as the witdoeke (white cloths) and allied with the police.1
  • 1986: with the support of the security forces, the witdoeke attacked neighbouring settlements and burned shacks, leaving around 60,000 people homeless.1 Many moved to the new township of Khayelitsha.
  • Influx control abolished: the pass laws were abolished in 1986.
05

What do others say?

At the time

The government argued that "influx control" was needed to manage the growth of the cities and protect jobs for Coloured workers. Residents and their supporters argued that the laws destroyed families and denied Black people the right to live in their own country.

Historians' debates

  • Victory or defeat? Josette Cole showed how the government tried to "reform" apartheid by co-opting some leaders while repressing others, so that the 1978 victory was followed by division.
  • Violence among residents: historians document how the witdoeke attacked rival residents with the support of the police and army. They also note that violence by some UDF-aligned youth contributed to the conflict, while emphasising the role of the security forces in arming and directing it.
  • Women's leadership: scholars such as Anne-Maria Makhulu highlight the central role of women in the squatter movement.
06

The evidence

  • Black Sash records, photographs and reports.
  • Newspaper reports and documentary films from the 1970s and 1980s.
  • The hearings of the Truth and Reconciliation Commission on the 1986 violence.
07

How is it remembered?

  • Crossroads is today one of Cape Town's largest townships.
  • The 1986 attacks were examined by the Truth and Reconciliation Commission in the 1990s.
08

Why does it matter?

Crossroads is a story about the right to a home and to family life. Ordinary people, many of them women, forced the apartheid state to back down.

  • It shows how housing was at the centre of the struggle against apartheid.
  • It shows how a state can exploit divisions within communities, turning residents against each other.
  • Struggles over informal settlements and evictions continue in South Africa today.
