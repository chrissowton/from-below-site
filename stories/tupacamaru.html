The Rebellion of Túpac Amaru II

An Indigenous leader and his wife, Micaela Bastidas, led the largest rebellion in Spain's American empire, demanding an end to forced labour and abuse.

When
4 November 1780 – 1781
Where
Tinta and Cusco, Peru
Facts checked
2026-11-15

01

What is the background to this event?

In the late eighteenth century, the Indigenous peoples of the Andes lived under a colonial system that extracted labour and wealth from them in several ways:

  • The mita, a system of forced labour that required Indigenous communities to send men to work in the silver mines of Potosí and the mercury mines of Huancavelica, where many died.
  • The reparto de mercancías, under which local Spanish officials (corregidores) forced Indigenous people to buy goods — often useless or overpriced — on credit, then pursued them for the debts.
  • Tribute payments and, after the Bourbon reforms of the 1770s, increased sales taxes and customs duties that also hit mestizo and creole traders.

José Gabriel Condorcanqui was a kuraka (hereditary Indigenous leader) of several Quechua communities near Tinta, south of Cusco, and a prosperous muleteer who owned some 350 mules. Educated by the Jesuits, he claimed descent from Túpac Amaru I, the last Inca ruler, executed by the Spanish in 1572. For years he petitioned colonial authorities in Tinta, Cusco and Lima to end the abuses of the mita and the corregidores, without success.

In their own words

03

What happened?

On 4 November 1780, after a dinner held by a local priest, Condorcanqui and his allies seized the corregidor Antonio de Arriaga, whom he accused of repeated abuses. Arriaga was forced to write letters summoning Spaniards and kurakas, and on 10 November he was publicly hanged in Tungasuca.1 Claiming to act on the orders of the Spanish Crown, Condorcanqui proclaimed the end of the reparto and the mita.

The rebellion spread rapidly:12

  • On 16 November 1780 he issued a decree abolishing slavery for Black people — the first such decree in Spanish America.
  • On 18 November his forces defeated a royalist army at Sangarará.
  • Micaela Bastidas commanded forces and organised the movement's logistics, and urged a rapid attack on Cusco before its defences could be strengthened.

The rebels' siege of Cusco at the end of 1780 failed, and Spanish forces, reinforced from Lima, gradually gained the upper hand. Betrayed by two of his officers, Túpac Amaru was captured in April 1781 together with Micaela Bastidas and members of his family.1 To the royal official José Antonio de Areche, who interrogated him, he is reported to have said:

"You as oppressor, I as liberator, deserve to die."Túpac Amaru II to José Antonio de Areche, 1781, as recorded in later accounts1

On 18 May 1781, in the main square of Cusco, he was forced to watch the execution of his wife, his eldest son Hipólito and other relatives and captains. An attempt to tear his body apart with four horses failed, and he was then beheaded.1 Of the 73 leaders of the rebellion who were executed, 32 were women.1

04

What happened next?

  • The rebellion continued: in the south, Túpac Katari and Bartolina Sisa besieged La Paz for months in 1781, and Túpac Amaru's relatives fought on until 1782. The total death toll of the rebellions is uncertain; it is estimated at around 100,000 Indigenous people and 10,000–40,000 others.
  • Repression: the Spanish authorities banned Inca dress, the use of Inca titles and the display of Inca imagery, and abolished the hereditary office of kuraka in much of the region.
  • Reforms: the system of corregidores and the forced reparto were abolished in the 1780s and replaced by a new system of intendants, though the mita continued.
  • Independence: Peru declared independence in 1821. The rebellion's memory made many creole elites wary of mass Indigenous mobilisation.
05

What do others say?

At the time

The colonial authorities portrayed the rebellion as a savage race war against Spaniards and creoles and used extreme public punishments as a warning. Many creoles who had initially shared grievances against Bourbon taxes turned against the rebellion as its Indigenous character and violence became clear.

Historians' debates

  • Reformer or revolutionary? Túpac Amaru initially claimed to act in the name of the Spanish king against corrupt officials. Historians disagree about whether he aimed to reform the colonial system or to overthrow it and restore an Inca state.
  • Precursor of independence? Peruvian nationalist historians, and the military government of Juan Velasco (1968–75), presented him as a forerunner of national independence. Others argue this reads later nationalism back into an eighteenth-century movement.
  • Violence: rebel forces killed many Spaniards and creoles, and the royalist repression was extremely brutal. Historians such as Charles Walker have explored both.
06

The evidence

  • The trial records of Túpac Amaru, Micaela Bastidas and others, including their testimony under interrogation — some of it given under torture.
  • Proclamations, edicts and letters issued by the rebel leaders, including letters by Micaela Bastidas.
  • Reports and correspondence of colonial officials.
  • Andean oral tradition, including the belief that the leader's body would one day be reunited.

07

How is it remembered?

  • The Velasco government made Túpac Amaru II a national symbol, using his image on posters and naming its agrarian reform after him.
  • His name was adopted by guerrilla movements, including the Tupamaros in Uruguay and the MRTA in Peru — a use that many find troubling.
  • The rapper Tupac Shakur was named after him.
  • Monuments stand in Cusco, Lima and many other towns, and Micaela Bastidas has been honoured on stamps and banknotes.
08

Why does it matter?

The rebellion of Túpac Amaru II was the largest uprising against Spanish rule in the Americas before the wars of independence. It brought tens of thousands of Indigenous people into a political movement against forced labour and colonial abuse, and it put forward demands — including the abolition of slavery — that went beyond those of many later independence leaders.

  • It is a central reference point for Indigenous movements in Peru, Bolivia and beyond.
  • The leadership of women such as Micaela Bastidas, Tomasa Tito Condemayta and Bartolina Sisa makes it an important case in the history of women's resistance.
  • It shows how colonial rule could be challenged from within its own legal and political language.

Questions to think about

  2. Why do you think the Spanish authorities chose such a brutal public execution?
