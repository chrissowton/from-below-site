The Battle of Surabaya

Weeks after Indonesia declared independence, the young people of Surabaya tore the blue from a Dutch flag and then fought British and Indian troops street by street — a defeat that became a national legend.

When
September – November 1945
Where
Surabaya, East Java, Indonesia
Facts checked
2026-11-16

01

What is the background to this event?

For more than three centuries the Dutch had ruled the islands they called the Netherlands East Indies. In 1942 Japan invaded and occupied the colony. When Japan surrendered in August 1945, the nationalist leaders Sukarno and Mohammad Hatta declared the independence of the Republic of Indonesia on 17 August 1945.1

  • As news of the declaration spread across the archipelago, most ordinary Indonesians came to regard themselves as citizens of the new Republic.1
  • Young people — the pemuda — formed militias, seized weapons from the defeated Japanese and took control of towns and cities. By the time British forces arrived in Surabaya, the pemuda's hold on the city was described as "a strong unified fortress".1
  • The Dutch were determined to return. Allied (mostly British and British Indian) troops were sent to disarm the Japanese and rescue European internees — but many Indonesians believed they were preparing the way for Dutch rule.
  • In September and October 1945 a period of violence known as the bersiap saw attacks on Europeans, Eurasians and others suspected of supporting the Dutch.1
02

Who were the key people?

  • Bung Tomo (Sutomo)

    A young journalist whose fiery broadcasts on Radio Pemberontakan (Rebel Radio) rallied the people of Surabaya to resist.

  • The pemuda

    Students, workers, former soldiers of Japanese-trained units and young people from the kampungs, who formed the backbone of the resistance.

  • Governor Suryo

    The Republic's governor of East Java, who negotiated with the British.

  • Brigadier A. W. S. Mallaby

    The commander of the British Indian brigade in Surabaya, killed on 30 October 1945.

  • The British Indian soldiers

    Troops of the 23rd and 5th Indian Divisions, many of whom were themselves colonial subjects — some later deserted to the Indonesian side.

In their own words

03

What happened?

On 19 September 1945, a group of Dutch internees, supported by the Japanese, raised the Dutch flag outside the Hotel Yamato (formerly the Oranje Hotel) in Surabaya. Indonesian youths stormed the hotel, climbed to the roof and tore off the blue stripe, turning the red, white and blue Dutch flag into the red and white flag of Indonesia.1

On 25 October, about 6,000 British Indian troops landed in Surabaya. Their commander, Brigadier Mallaby, agreed with the Republic's governor that the British would not demand that Indonesians hand over their weapons. But leaflets dropped by other British commanders ordered exactly that, and the Indonesians felt betrayed.1 On 28 October, Indonesian fighters attacked the British positions, cutting off their water supply. Around 20,000 Republican troops and crowds of 70,000–140,000 people overwhelmed the British forces, who were saved only when Sukarno himself flew in to negotiate a ceasefire.1

On 30 October, while touring the city to enforce the ceasefire, Mallaby was killed in confused circumstances.1 The British sent reinforcements and issued an ultimatum on 9 November: all Indonesians must surrender their weapons. The Indonesians refused, and Bung Tomo urged the people to resist on the radio.1 On 10 November, the British launched a full-scale attack:13

  • Some 24,000 troops of the 5th Indian Division, supported by tanks, aircraft and warships, attacked the city.
  • Indonesian fighters, many armed only with rifles, knives and bamboo spears, resisted street by street under the slogan Merdeka atau mati — "Freedom or death".
  • It took the British around three weeks to take the city. Estimates of Indonesian deaths range from about 6,000 to 16,000, and as many as 200,000 people fled the city.3
04

What happened next?

  • A military defeat, a political victory: the Republic lost the city and many of its best fighters and weapons. But the battle convinced the British, and the world, that the Republic had real popular support. Britain soon pressed the Dutch to negotiate.
  • Four more years of war: the Dutch launched two major military offensives, which they called "police actions", in 1947 and 1948, alongside negotiations.
  • Independence: under international pressure, especially from the United States and the United Nations, the Netherlands recognised Indonesian sovereignty in December 1949.
  • Apology: in 2020 King Willem-Alexander apologised for "excessive violence" by Dutch forces, and in 2022, after a major research programme, the Dutch prime minister apologised for the systematic and extreme violence used in the war.
05

Why does it matter?

The Battle of Surabaya was the largest single battle of the Indonesian Revolution. Young people and ordinary townspeople, poorly armed and largely untrained, took on a modern army to defend a freedom that was only weeks old.

  • It showed the world that Indonesian independence was a mass popular movement, not the creation of a few collaborators.
  • It is one of the great founding stories of the Indonesian nation, remembered every year on Heroes' Day.
  • It is part of the wave of decolonisation that followed the Second World War across Asia and Africa.

What do others say?How it was seen at the time, and where historians disagree

At the time

British and Dutch officials described the Indonesian fighters as extremists and "terrorists" incited by Japanese-trained agitators, and the killing of Mallaby as murder under a flag of truce. Indonesians saw the British attack as an attempt to restore Dutch colonial rule by force.

Historians' debates

  • Who killed Mallaby? Accounts differ on whether he was shot by a young Indonesian or killed by an explosion during the confusion; the circumstances remain disputed.
  • The bersiap: historians have increasingly examined the violence against Dutch, Eurasian, Chinese and other civilians in the early months of the revolution, which was long neglected in Indonesian accounts and long emphasised in Dutch ones.
  • From below? Benedict Anderson and William Frederick stressed the role of the pemuda and the urban poor, who often acted ahead of the Republic's more cautious leaders.
How do we know?The evidence, and a source from the time
  • British military reports and war diaries, and the memoirs of British and Indian officers.
  • Indonesian newspapers, radio broadcasts and the memoirs of participants, including Bung Tomo.
  • Photographs taken by Indonesian photographers such as the Mendur brothers, founders of the IPPHOS agency.
  • The Dutch research programme 'Independence, Decolonization, Violence and War in Indonesia, 1945–1950' (2017–2022).

Reading it

Young Indonesians tear the blue stripe from a Dutch flag, leaving the red and white of Indonesia. It needed no words: one tear turned the colonial flag into the national one. In Indonesia the moment is still re-enacted and remembered.

How is it remembered?Memorials, anniversaries and arguments over memory
  • 10 November is Heroes' Day (Hari Pahlawan) in Indonesia.
  • The Heroes Monument (Tugu Pahlawan) and a museum in Surabaya commemorate the battle; Surabaya is known as the "City of Heroes".
  • The Hotel Majapahit (formerly the Yamato) still stands, and the flag incident is re-enacted each year.
