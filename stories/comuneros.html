The Revolt of the Comuneros

The cities of Castile rose against a foreign-raised young king and his heavy taxes. They set up their own councils, and the movement turned against the great nobles before it was crushed.

When
1520 – 1521
Where
Toledo, Valladolid and Villalar, Castile, Spain

See it on the map →

01

What is the background to this event?

In 1516, the sixteen-year-old Charles of Habsburg became king of Castile and Aragon. He had been raised in the Netherlands and knew little Castilian.1

  • He arrived with Flemish nobles and clerics who took high office.1
  • In 1519 he was elected Holy Roman Emperor, and in 1520 he left Spain, leaving the Dutch cardinal Adrian of Utrecht in charge.1
  • The towns resented new taxes to pay for his ambitions.
02

Who were the key people?

  • Juan López de Padilla

    A leader of the comuneros of Toledo; executed.

  • Juan Bravo and Francisco Maldonado

    Leaders from Segovia and Salamanca; executed.

  • María Pacheco

    Padilla's wife, who led the defence of Toledo.

  • Charles I (Emperor Charles V)

    King of Castile.

03

What happened?

In 1520, riots broke out in the cities, and local councils, the comunidades, took power.1

  • At their height, the rebels controlled the heart of Castile, including Valladolid, Tordesillas and Toledo.1
  • They tried to make the king's confined mother, Queen Joanna, their ruler.1
  • The movement took on a radical, anti-feudal side, supporting peasant risings against the nobles.1
  • On 23 April 1521, the royal army defeated the comuneros at Villalar.1
  • The next day, Padilla, Bravo and Maldonado were beheaded.1
  • Toledo held out under María Pacheco until October 1521.1

04

What happened next?

  • Royal power: the defeat strengthened the monarchy and the great nobles.
  • María Pacheco fled to Portugal and died in exile.
05

What do others say?

At the time

Royalists saw a treasonous rebellion; the comuneros claimed to defend the kingdom against foreign misrule.1

Debates since

  • First modern revolution? Some scholars see democratic ideals and opposition to social injustice; others see a typical revolt against high taxes and foreign control.12
  • Violence on both sides: crowds killed royal officials; royal troops burned the town of Medina del Campo, and the rebel leaders were executed.2
06

The evidence

  • The records of the rebel council, the Santa Junta.
  • Royal correspondence and chronicles.2
07

How is it remembered?

  • Liberals adopted the comuneros as heroes from the nineteenth century.1
  • 23 April, the anniversary of Villalar, is the official day of the region of Castile and León.
08

Why does it matter?

The comuneros are seen by many as early defenders of self-government against absolute monarchy.

  • Their revolt combined civic, national and social demands.

Questions to think about

  1. Why did the cities of Castile rebel against Charles?
  2. What were the comunidades, and how did they govern?
  3. Why did the nobles turn against the comuneros?
  4. Was this a modern revolution or a traditional tax revolt?
09

What other events are like this?

  • 1378The Revolt of the Ciompi — The wool workers of Florence, excluded from the guilds that ran the city, seized the government — and for a few weeks a wool-carder sat at the head of the republic.
  • 1282The Sicilian Vespers — At the hour of evening prayer on Easter Monday, a quarrel outside a Palermo church set off a rising against the island's French rulers. Within weeks, French rule in Sicily was over.
  • 1525The German Peasants' War and the Twelve Articles — Tens of thousands of peasants rose against their lords, armed with the new ideas of the Reformation and a printed manifesto of rights. It was Europe's largest popular uprising before the French Revolution.
