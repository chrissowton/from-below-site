Migrant Workers and the Qatar World Cup

When Qatar won the 2022 World Cup, hundreds of thousands of migrant workers came to build it. Their deaths, unpaid wages and protests — and campaigns by unions and rights groups worldwide — forced labour reforms, though how much changed is still disputed.

01

What is the background to this event?

Foreign workers make up around 91% of Qatar's population and around 95% of its workforce. Most come from South Asia, the Philippines and Egypt.1

  • Under the kafala (sponsorship) system, employers controlled whether workers could change jobs or leave the country.1
  • Employers often took workers' passports, and many workers paid large recruitment fees to get jobs.12
  • In 2010, Qatar was chosen to host the 2022 FIFA World Cup, which required a huge building programme.1

In their own words

03

What happened?

After 2010, investigations by journalists and rights groups drew attention to workers' conditions.1

  • Workers reported unpaid wages, confiscated passports and dangerous work in extreme heat.14
  • In 2021, The Guardian estimated, from embassy data, that 6,500 migrant workers had died since 2010, though not all deaths could be linked to World Cup work.4
  • Qatar's own figures recorded 15,021 deaths of non-Qataris between 2010 and 2019.4
  • In 2020, Qatar allowed workers to change jobs without their employer's permission, and introduced a minimum wage of 1,000 riyals (about US$275) a month from March 2021.4
  • In August 2022, more than 60 workers who protested about months of unpaid wages were arrested and deported.4
04

What happened next?

  • Praise and criticism: the ILO called Qatar the first country in the region to introduce a non-discriminatory minimum wage; the campaign group Migrant Rights said it was too low.4
  • Compensation: in 2022, an Amnesty poll of over 17,000 football fans in 15 countries found 73% supported FIFA compensating workers.4
  • After the tournament: in their reports for 2025, Amnesty International and Human Rights Watch said workers still faced wage theft, unexplained deaths and exploitation, and that the reforms had had limited impact.1
05

Why does it matter?

The World Cup made the conditions of millions of Gulf migrant workers visible to the world. It showed how global attention, unions and campaigners can press governments to reform — and how hard it is for workers with little power to organise to make sure reforms are enforced.

  • The ILO called Qatar the first country in the region to introduce a non-discriminatory minimum wage; campaigners said it was too low.4
  • In 2022 an Amnesty poll of over 17,000 football fans in 15 countries found 73% supported FIFA compensating workers.4
  • Campaigns for compensation for the families of workers who died continue.4

What do others say?How it was seen at the time, and where historians disagree

At the time

Qatari officials pointed to their reforms and accused critics of ignoring them; rights groups described conditions as amounting to forced labour.4

Debates since

  • How many deaths? Estimates vary widely, and the larger figures count deaths from all causes, not only deaths at work. In 2021 Amnesty criticised Qatar for failing to investigate workers' deaths, which makes a firm figure impossible.4
  • Was kafala abolished? Qatar has said the system was effectively ended; rights groups say important parts remain.1
  • The ILO's role: some former ILO staff accused it of being too uncritical of Qatar; the ILO said cooperation had won real improvements.1
How do we know?The evidence, and a source from the time

Reading it

Migrant workers at Doha's central bus station in the early years of the building boom. The men in the picture came mostly from South Asia, and under the kafala system their right to change jobs or leave depended on their employer. Who had the power to change that — Qatar, FIFA, or the workers' home countries — is the question behind the photograph.
