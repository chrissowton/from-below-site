The First Secession of the Plebs

Crushed by debt and denied any say in government, the common people of Rome — who made up most of its army — simply walked out of the city. It was perhaps the world's first general strike.

When
c. 494 BCE
Where
The Sacred Mount (Mons Sacer), near Rome
Facts checked
2026-11-16

See it on the map →

01

What is the background to this event?

The last king of Rome was expelled around 509 BCE, and Rome became a republic.1

  • Power lay with the patricians, an aristocratic class. Only patricians could be consuls or members of the Senate.1
  • The plebeians — the common people — were the majority of the population and most of the soldiers in Rome's army.1
  • Many plebeians were deeply in debt. Debtors could be imprisoned, beaten and even enslaved by their creditors.1
  • According to the historian Livy, an old soldier appeared in the Forum, pale and thin, showing his battle scars and the marks of beatings, and told how he had fallen into debt while fighting for Rome.1
02

Who were the key people?

  • The plebeians

    Farmers, craftsmen and soldiers who made up most of Rome's population and army.

  • Menenius Agrippa

    A senator said to have persuaded the plebs to return with a fable about the body and its parts.

  • Lucius Sicinius Vellutus

    Named by later sources as a leader of the secession and one of the first tribunes.

In their own words

03

What happened?

The plebeians demanded reform of the debt laws, but the Senate refused.1 The dispute grew into a wider struggle over plebeian rights.1

  • Around 494 BCE, the plebeians — including soldiers — left Rome and gathered on the Sacred Mount, a hill outside the city.1
  • Without them, Rome had no army and no workforce.
  • According to Livy, the senator Menenius Agrippa persuaded them to return by telling a fable: the parts of the body rebelled against the stomach, which they thought did nothing — but the whole body began to waste away.

A reconciliation was negotiated. The plebeians won political representation through the creation of the tribunes of the plebs — officials elected by the plebeians who could protect them against abuses by magistrates.1

04

What happened next?

  • The Conflict of the Orders: the plebeians seceded several more times over the next two centuries, winning written laws (the Twelve Tables), the right to hold the consulship, and in 287 BCE the rule that decisions of the plebeian assembly bound all Romans.
  • The tribunes: the office of tribune became a powerful part of the Roman constitution — and was later used by reformers such as the Gracchi brothers.
05

What do others say?

In ancient times

Roman writers presented the story as a lesson in concord between the classes. For later radicals, it was an example of the common people standing up to the elite.

Historians' debates

  • Did it happen? Our sources, such as Livy, wrote centuries later. Historians such as T. J. Cornell accept that there was a real secession and that the tribunes were created early, but many details — such as the fable — are probably legend.
  • Who were the plebeians? Historians debate whether the patrician–plebeian divide was one of wealth, birth or both.
06

The evidence

  • Livy's History of Rome and Dionysius of Halicarnassus's Roman Antiquities.

07

How is it remembered?

  • The fable of the belly appears in Shakespeare's Coriolanus.
  • The secession has been cited by labour movements as an ancient general strike.
08

Why does it matter?

The first secession of the plebs is one of the earliest recorded examples of collective withdrawal of labour and service to win political rights.

  • It shows the power of people who do the work and the fighting when they act together.
  • The fable of the belly has been used ever since — sometimes to argue for social harmony, sometimes to justify inequality.

Questions to think about

09

What other events are like this?

  • 73 BCESpartacus and the Slave Revolt — Seventy gladiators broke out of a training school armed with kitchen knives. Within two years, an army of some 120,000 enslaved people had defeated Rome's legions again and again.
  • 1157 BCEThe Tomb-Builders' Strike at Deir el-Medina — When the pharaoh's grain rations failed to arrive, the craftsmen who built the royal tombs laid down their tools and marched to the temples — the earliest strike in recorded history.
  • 1647The Putney Debates — Ordinary soldiers and their elected representatives argued with Cromwell and the army's generals about who should have the right to vote.
