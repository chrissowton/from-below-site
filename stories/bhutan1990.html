The Lhotshampa Protests and Expulsion

When Bhutan reclassified many of its Nepali-speaking citizens as illegal immigrants, they protested for citizenship and rights. More than 100,000 people were forced out, and many spent nearly two decades in refugee camps.

When
1988 – 1993
Where
Southern Bhutan and refugee camps in eastern Nepal
Facts checked
2026-11-16

See it on the map →

01

What is the background to this event?

People of Nepali origin had settled in southern Bhutan since the nineteenth century. Called the Lhotshampa ("southern borderlanders"), they were mainly Hindu and spoke Nepali.1

  • In the 1980s, the government introduced a "One Nation, One People" policy, making the national dress and customs of the Buddhist majority compulsory.1
  • A census of 1988 reclassified many people of Nepali heritage as illegal immigrants.1

03

What happened?

Lhotshampa leaders responded to the census and the new policies with protests demanding citizenship.1

  • In 1990, large protests took place across southern Bhutan, demanding democracy and respect for minority rights.1
  • Some protesters damaged government buildings, and the Bhutan People's Party began a campaign of violence against the government.1
  • The government responded with arrests and, according to refugees and human rights groups, torture and the forced eviction of families.
  • From the late 1980s, more than 100,000 Lhotshampa were forced out of Bhutan.1
  • Many crossed India to eastern Nepal, where they lived in refugee camps.1

04

What happened next?

  • Camps: in 2010, more than 85,000 refugees still lived in the camps in Nepal.1
  • Resettlement: from 2007, most were resettled in the United States, Canada, Australia, the United Kingdom and elsewhere.1
  • No return: talks between Bhutan and Nepal on repatriation failed.
05

What do others say?

At the time

Bhutan's government said it was protecting its culture from illegal immigration and responding to violence; refugees described ethnic cleansing.1

Debates since

  • Violence: some protesters and militants attacked government property and officials; the state's response included mass expulsion.12
  • Leaving "voluntarily": refugees say they were forced to sign forms saying they left willingly.1

08

Why does it matter?

The expulsion of the Lhotshampa was one of the largest refugee crises in South Asia, and is little known outside the region.

  • It shows how citizenship laws can be used to exclude a minority.

Questions to think about
