The Trưng Sisters' Rebellion

Two sisters led the peoples of the Red River Delta in a revolt against Chinese rule, drove out the Han governor and ruled as queens for three years.

When
40 CE – 43
Where
Mê Linh, Red River Delta, Vietnam
Facts checked
2026-11-16

See it on the map →

01

What is the background to this event?

In 111 BCE the Han dynasty of China conquered the kingdom of Nanyue, which included the Red River Delta in what is now northern Vietnam. The region became the Han commandery of Jiaozhi.1

  • At first the Han ruled largely through the local Lạc lords, who kept much of their traditional power.
  • In the early first century AD, Han governors began to impose Chinese laws, customs and taxes more directly, and Chinese settlers and officials arrived in greater numbers.
  • The Lạc peoples had their own traditions, in which women could hold property and authority — traditions that came into conflict with Han Confucian norms.

The governor of Jiaozhi at the time, Su Ding, is remembered for his cruelty and greed.1

In their own words

03

What happened?

The Trưng sisters were daughters of a wealthy Lạc lord of Mê Linh, near present-day Hanoi.1 According to Vietnamese tradition, the governor Su Ding had Trưng Trắc's husband, Thi Sách, executed; the Chinese Book of the Later Han says that Su Ding tried to restrain "her" (or "them") with the law, and that she became furious and rebelled.1

In 40 CE, Trưng Trắc and Trưng Nhị led the Lạc peoples in revolt against the Han:1

  • The rising began in the Red River Delta and quickly spread to other peoples across a wide area stretching from Hepu (in present-day Guangxi, China) to Rinan (in central Vietnam).
  • Han settlements were overrun, and Su Ding fled.
  • The rebels captured some sixty-five strongholds, and Trưng Trắc was proclaimed queen, with her capital at Mê Linh.

The Book of the Later Han, the official history of the dynasty they fought, records the rising briefly:

"…in all she took sixty-five strongholds and established herself as queen."Book of the Later Han, as translated in Wikipedia1

In 42 CE, the Han emperor Guangwu sent the veteran general Ma Yuan with an army and a fleet to crush the rebellion.1 After a year of fighting, the sisters were defeated in 43. Chinese sources record that Ma Yuan captured and beheaded them and sent their heads to the Han capital at Luoyang. Vietnamese tradition holds that the sisters drowned themselves in the Hát River rather than surrender.1

04

What happened next?

  • Han rule tightened: Ma Yuan reorganised the administration, strengthened Han law and weakened the power of the Lạc lords. Legend says he erected bronze pillars to mark the Han frontier.
  • Centuries of Chinese rule: northern Vietnam remained under Chinese control, with interruptions, for almost a thousand years, until independence in 938.
  • Later risings: other rebels, including the woman leader Bà Triệu in 248 CE, followed the sisters' example.
05

What do others say?

The sources

The only contemporary or near-contemporary written sources are Chinese histories written by the sisters' enemies, which give only a few lines. The rich Vietnamese tradition about the sisters was written down much later, from the thirteenth century onwards, and includes legend as well as history.

Historians' debates

  • Why did they rebel? Vietnamese tradition stresses revenge for Thi Sách's death; Chinese sources suggest that Trưng Trắc reacted against the governor's legal restrictions on her. Some historians argue that the deeper cause was Han interference with Lạc society, including its customs of inheritance and the position of women.
  • Was Thi Sách killed? Some historians doubt the story of his execution, which does not appear in the earliest Chinese sources.
  • National heroes? Historians caution that the idea of a "Vietnamese nation" fighting "China" projects modern categories onto the first century; others argue that the rising does show an early sense of shared identity among the peoples of the Red River Delta.
06

The evidence

  • Brief passages in the Book of the Later Han and in the biography of Ma Yuan.
  • Later Vietnamese chronicles, especially the Đại Việt sử ký toàn thư (1479).
  • Temples, local festivals and oral traditions across northern Vietnam.
  • Archaeological evidence of Lạc (Đông Sơn) society, including bronze drums.

07

How is it remembered?

  • The sisters are honoured at temples in Hanoi and at Mê Linh, and many streets, schools and a district of Hanoi bear their name.
  • Their festival is celebrated on the sixth day of the second lunar month.
  • They are a frequent subject of traditional Đông Hồ woodblock prints, which show them riding war elephants.
08

Why does it matter?

The Trưng sisters are among the most important national heroes of Vietnam. Their rising is remembered as the first great revolt against foreign rule, and as proof that women could lead armies and states.

  • It is one of the earliest recorded independence movements in Asia led by women.
  • For later Vietnamese nationalists, resisting French rule and later wars, the sisters were a symbol of national resistance.
  • Their story is also part of the history of women's leadership, and of debates about how women's roles changed under Chinese influence.
