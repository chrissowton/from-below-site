Nunavut: 'Our Land'

After decades of relocation, residential schools and the slaughter of their sled dogs, the Inuit negotiated the largest Indigenous land claim in Canadian history — and a territory of their own.

When
1973 – 1999
Where
Iqaluit and the Eastern Arctic, Canada
Facts checked
2026-11-16

See it on the map →

01

What is the background to this event?

The Inuit have lived in the Arctic for a thousand years. In the twentieth century, the Canadian state transformed their lives.

  • From the 1950s, many Inuit families were moved into permanent settlements, sometimes far from their hunting grounds. In the "High Arctic relocation" of 1953–55, families were moved from northern Quebec to Ellesmere and Cornwallis Islands.
  • Inuit children were sent to residential schools, where many lost their language and suffered abuse.
  • In the 1950s and 1960s, many Inuit sled dogs were killed by police and officials, destroying families' ability to hunt and travel.
  • In 1971, Inuit leaders founded the Inuit Tapirisat of Canada to defend their rights.

03

What happened?

In 1973, the Inuit Tapirisat of Canada began research into Inuit land use and occupancy, mapping where Inuit had hunted, travelled and lived.1

  • In 1976, it proposed the creation of a Nunavut territory.1
  • From 1982, the Tunngavik Federation of Nunavut negotiated with the federal government.1
  • In a 1992 vote, residents of the Northwest Territories approved dividing the territory to create Nunavut.1
  • On 25 May 1993, the Nunavut Land Claims Agreement was signed in Iqaluit. It is the largest Aboriginal land claim settlement in Canadian history.1

Under the agreement, the Inuit gained title to about 350,000 square kilometres of land, including mineral rights to part of it; the right to harvest wildlife throughout the territory; equal representation on wildlife and environmental boards; a share of resource royalties; and $1.9 billion over 15 years.1

On 1 April 1999, Nunavut — "Our Land" in Inuktitut — became a territory of Canada, with its capital at Iqaluit.

04

What happened next?

  • Self-government: because about 85 per cent of Nunavut's population is Inuit, the territorial government is effectively Inuit-led, and Inuktitut is an official language.
  • Apology: in 2010, the Canadian government apologised for the High Arctic relocation, and in 2019 for the killing of sled dogs in the Qikiqtani region.
  • Challenges: Nunavut faces high costs of living, housing shortages, and high rates of suicide; Inuit organisations have taken the government to court over its implementation of the agreement.
05

What do others say?

At the time

Some critics argued that a territory based on one ethnic group was undemocratic. Supporters pointed out that Nunavut's government is public and open to all residents.

Debates since

  • Surrender of rights: some Inuit criticised the agreement for requiring them to give up Aboriginal title to the rest of their lands in exchange for defined rights.
  • Implementation: Inuit organisations argue that Canada has failed to meet its obligations, for example on Inuit employment in government.
  • Resources: debate continues about mining and its effects on wildlife and communities.

How is it remembered?

  • Nunavut Day is celebrated on 9 July, the anniversary of the passing of the Nunavut Act in 1993; the territory itself was created on 1 April 1999.
08

Why does it matter?

The creation of Nunavut redrew the map of Canada. It showed how Indigenous peoples could use research, negotiation and law to win back control over their land.

  • It is one of the world's largest examples of Indigenous self-government.
  • It shows the importance of Indigenous knowledge — the land-use maps — in winning rights.

Open questions

  1. Why do you think the Inuit mapped their use of the land?
  2. What did the Inuit gain, and what did they give up, in the agreement?
  3. Can a territory with a mainly Indigenous population be both Indigenous-led and democratic?
  4. How can governments make up for past relocations?
