Nepal's People's Movement of 2006

Defying curfews and shoot-on-sight orders, millions of Nepalis took to the streets for nineteen days and forced their king to give up absolute power — the beginning of the end of a 240-year-old monarchy.

When
6 – 24 April 2006
Where
Kathmandu and across Nepal

01

What is the background to this event?

Nepal was ruled for centuries by the Shah dynasty of kings. A first People's Movement (Jana Andolan) in 1990 forced King Birendra to accept a constitutional monarchy and multi-party democracy. But the following years were troubled:

  • In February 1996 the Communist Party of Nepal (Maoist) launched a "People's War" to overthrow the monarchy. The civil war that followed killed more than 17,000 people. According to figures compiled for 1996–2005, more than 4,000 were killed by the Maoists and more than 8,000 by government forces.3
  • Both sides committed serious abuses. Government forces carried out extrajudicial killings, torture and enforced disappearances; more than 3,000 complaints of disappearance were later filed. The Maoists killed civilians they considered enemies, abducted people, extorted money and recruited children as fighters.3
  • In 2001 King Birendra and much of the royal family were killed in a massacre at the palace, apparently carried out by Crown Prince Dipendra. Birendra's brother Gyanendra became king.
  • On 1 February 2005 King Gyanendra dismissed the government, declared a state of emergency and took absolute power. Politicians were arrested, telephone and internet lines were cut, and the press was censored.

In November 2005, the main parliamentary parties, which had formed a Seven Party Alliance, agreed a twelve-point understanding with the Maoists to work together against the king's direct rule.

03

What happened?

On 6 April 2006, the Seven Party Alliance called a general strike and mass protests against the king's rule, with the support of the Maoists. The protests continued for nineteen days and grew into one of the largest movements in Nepal's history:12

  • The government imposed curfews in Kathmandu and other towns and issued shoot-on-sight orders against people who broke them. Mobile phone networks were cut.
  • Hundreds of thousands of people defied the curfews to march on the Kathmandu Ring Road and in towns across the country. Civil servants, lawyers, journalists and teachers joined the strike.
  • Security forces fired on demonstrators. Nineteen demonstrators were killed, hundreds were arrested and many more were injured.3

On 21 April, King Gyanendra offered to hand executive power to a prime minister chosen by the parties. The alliance rejected the offer as inadequate, and the protests continued. On the night of 24 April, in a nationally televised address, the king announced that he was reinstating the House of Representatives, which he had dissolved in 2002, and called on the Seven Party Alliance to lead the nation. The alliance named Girija Prasad Koirala as prime minister.1

04

What happened next?

  • The king stripped of power: on 18 May 2006, the restored parliament passed a proclamation removing the king's control of the army, ending his immunity and making him subject to taxation.
  • Peace: the Maoists declared a ceasefire, and in November 2006 the government and the Maoists signed a Comprehensive Peace Agreement, ending the civil war.
  • A republic: elections to a Constituent Assembly were held in 2008, and the Maoists emerged as the largest party. On 28 May 2008, the assembly abolished the monarchy and declared Nepal a federal democratic republic.
  • A new constitution: after years of debate, a new constitution was adopted in 2015.
05

What do others say?

At the time

The royal government claimed that the protests had been infiltrated by Maoist "terrorists" and that the curfews were needed to protect lives. The protesters argued that the king had no right to rule without a parliament and that democracy was the only path to peace.

Commentators' debates

  • Who led? Some commentators emphasise the role of the political parties; others the Maoists' ability to mobilise; others still the spontaneous participation of ordinary citizens, which often went further than the leaders.
  • The Maoists and the civil war: the 2006 movement was largely peaceful, but it depended on an alliance with an armed movement responsible for many killings, abductions and the use of child soldiers. Supporters argue that the alliance brought the Maoists into peaceful politics and ended the war; critics argue that it rewarded political violence.
  • Justice for victims: neither side's abuses have been fully addressed. Truth and reconciliation commissions created after 2015 made little progress, very few people have been convicted, and victims' groups — including families of the disappeared and of those killed by Maoists — continue to campaign against impunity.
  • What changed? Many Nepalis have since expressed frustration with political instability and corruption, and some have called for the return of the monarchy.

07

How is it remembered?

  • The movement is known in Nepal as the Loktantra Andolan (Democracy Movement) or the second Jana Andolan.
  • Those killed are honoured as martyrs of democracy.
  • Republic Day, 28 May, marks the abolition of the monarchy that followed.
08

Why does it matter?

The People's Movement of 2006 was a remarkable example of mass, largely non-violent protest bringing an end to absolute rule. It opened the way for the end of a decade-long civil war and the abolition of one of the world's last absolute monarchies.

  • It united parliamentary parties, former armed rebels and civil society in a common movement for democracy.
  • It led to the recognition of the rights of Nepal's many ethnic and caste groups, including Dalits and the Madhesis of the plains, which became central to later political debates.
  • It shows how a peace process can grow out of a popular movement.

What other events are like this?

  • 1986The People Power Revolution — More than two million unarmed Filipinos filled a Manila highway to protect rebel soldiers and end Ferdinand Marcos's twenty-year rule.
  • 2010The Tunisian Revolution — A street vendor's desperate protest in a small town sparked a month of uprising that toppled a dictator of 23 years and began the Arab Spring.
  • 1990Mongolia's Democratic Revolution — Young Mongolians gathered in the freezing main square of Ulaanbaatar to demand democracy. A hunger strike in March 1990 brought down the communist leadership — without a single death.
