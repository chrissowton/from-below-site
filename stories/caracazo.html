Human rights

The Caracazo

When a new government raised fuel and bus fares overnight, the poor of Caracas rose in anger. The army was sent into the hillside barrios, and hundreds — perhaps thousands — were killed.

When
27 February – March 1989
Where
Guarenas and Caracas, Venezuela

See it on the map →

01

What is the background to this event?

Venezuela had grown rich on oil, but when oil prices fell in the 1980s, the country faced debt and economic crisis.1

  • In 1988, Carlos Andrés Pérez was elected president, promising a return to prosperity and attacking the International Monetary Fund.1
  • Once in office, he announced IMF-backed austerity measures, including higher petrol prices and transport fares.1
02

Who were the key people?

  • Residents of the barrios

    Poor neighbourhoods around Caracas, where most of the protests and deaths took place.

  • President Carlos Andrés Pérez

    Whose austerity measures sparked the protests.

  • COFAVIC

    Committee of relatives of victims, which campaigned for justice.

03

What happened?

On 27 February 1989, protests began in Guarenas, near Caracas, over the rise in bus fares, and spread to Caracas and other towns.1

  • The protests turned into riots and looting.1
  • The government declared a curfew and sent in the army.1
  • Troops fired into poor neighbourhoods, killing unarmed residents, including in their homes.1
  • Officially, 276 people died; after hundreds of unmarked graves were found, many estimates put the toll above 2,000.1

04

What happened next?

  • Justice: in 1999, the Inter-American Court of Human Rights found that Venezuela had committed human rights violations, including extrajudicial killings.1
  • Politics: more riots followed in 1990; there were two coup attempts in 1992, one led by Hugo Chávez, who later called the Caracazo the beginning of his revolution.1
05

What do others say?

At the time

The government spoke of restoring order against looters; residents described soldiers shooting into homes.1

Debates since

  • Its legacy: Chávez and his supporters claimed the Caracazo as the start of their movement; critics say it has been used for political ends.1
  • Violence: rioters looted shops; security forces caused most of the deaths, including two soldiers and one police officer on their own side.1
06

The evidence

  • Inter-American Court of Human Rights judgment (1999).1
  • Exhumations of unmarked graves and testimony collected by COFAVIC.
07

How is it remembered?

  • Victims' families, organised in COFAVIC, have campaigned for justice since 1989.
08

Why does it matter?

The Caracazo was one of the first great revolts against IMF austerity, and changed Venezuelan politics.

  • It is also one of the worst massacres by a democratic government in Latin America.

Questions to think about

  1. Why did a rise in bus fares cause such anger?
  2. Why did the army kill so many people?
  3. Why is the true death toll still unknown?
  4. How did the Caracazo shape Venezuela's later history?
09

What other events are like this?

  • 1969The Cordobazo — Car workers, power workers and students rose against Argentina's military dictatorship and took control of much of the city for two days. The army retook it — but the dictatorship never recovered.
  • 2000The Cochabamba Water War — When Bolivia sold its city water system to a multinational consortium and bills soared, farmers, factory workers and residents rose up — and won.
  • 1905Bloody Sunday, St Petersburg — Tens of thousands of workers and their families, led by a priest and carrying icons, marched to ask the tsar for help. Soldiers opened fire, and the revolution of 1905 began.
