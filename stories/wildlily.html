The vote & democracy

The Wild Lily Student Movement

When Taiwan's president was chosen by an unelected assembly, students sat in at the memorial to Chiang Kai-shek with white lilies. Within six years, Taiwan held its first direct presidential election.

When
16 – 22 March 1990
Where
Liberty Square, Taipei, Taiwan
Facts checked

01

What is the background to this event?

Taiwan had been ruled by the Kuomintang (KMT) as a one-party state under martial law until 1987.1

  • The president was chosen by a National Assembly whose members had largely been elected in mainland China in 1947.1
  • In 1990, President Lee Teng-hui was re-elected by just 641 members of that assembly, with only one candidate.1

03

What happened?

On 16 March 1990, students began a sit-in at the Chiang Kai-shek Memorial square in Taipei.1

  • The number of demonstrators grew to 22,000.1
  • They demanded direct elections for president and vice-president and new elections for the whole National Assembly.1
  • They wore white Formosa lilies — a native flower symbolising resilience — as a symbol of democracy.1
  • On 21 March, President Lee met fifty students and promised full democracy.1

04

What happened next?

  • Democracy: the old National Assembly was replaced, and in 1996 Lee became Taiwan's first directly elected president, with over 95% turnout.1
  • Sunflowers: the movement inspired later student protests, including the Sunflower Movement of 2014.
05

What do others say?

At the time

Conservatives in the KMT resisted reform; the students had wide public sympathy.1

Debates since

  • Who made democracy? Historians debate the roles of students, opposition parties and reformers within the KMT such as Lee.2
  • Violence: the protest was entirely peaceful, and the government did not use force.1

07

How is it remembered?

  • The square was renamed Liberty Square, and democracy supporters gather there every 21 March.1
08

Why does it matter?

The Wild Lily movement was a turning point in Taiwan's peaceful transition to democracy.1

  • It took place less than a year after the crushing of the Tiananmen protests in Beijing.

09

What other events are like this?

  • 1989Tiananmen Square, 1989 — Students and workers occupied the heart of Beijing for seven weeks, calling for freedom of speech and an end to corruption, until the army cleared the streets with tanks and gunfire.
  • 1947The February 28 Incident — A dispute over contraband cigarettes sparked an island-wide uprising against a corrupt new government. The crackdown that followed killed tens of thousands and was not spoken of publicly for decades.
  • 1980The Gwangju Uprising — Students and citizens in Gwangju rose against a military coup. Paratroopers killed hundreds, but the uprising became the foundation of South Korean democracy.
