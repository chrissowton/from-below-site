Mahalla and the April 6 Strike

Women textile workers walked out of Egypt's largest factory, starting a wave of strikes. Their call for a general strike on 6 April 2008 spread through Facebook — and helped create the movement that toppled Mubarak three years later.

01

What is the background to this event?

Egypt under President Hosni Mubarak was ruled by emergency laws, and strikes were illegal.1

  • El-Mahalla El-Kubra, in the Nile Delta, is home to Misr Spinning and Weaving, then Egypt's largest factory, with more than 20,000 workers.
  • Economic reforms and privatisation in the 2000s brought rising prices and stagnant wages.
  • In December 2006, when promised bonuses were not paid, women workers walked out and called on the men to join them. The strike won its demands and sparked a wave of strikes across Egypt.

In their own words

03

What happened?

In early 2008, Mahalla workers called a strike for 6 April over low wages and rising food prices.1

  • Activists picked up the call and spread it through Facebook, blogs, text messages and word of mouth, calling for a nationwide general strike and civil disobedience.1
  • A Facebook group, the April 6 Youth Movement, attracted more than 64,000 members.1
  • The call urged people to stay at home, and demanded decent wages, education, health care, an end to torture and corruption, and "freedom and dignity".1

On 6 April, hundreds of plainclothes security men occupied the Mahalla factories before work began, forcing workers to work.1 But protests broke out in the town. Crowds tore down a poster of Mubarak. Police used tear gas and live ammunition, and two people were killed, including a 15-year-old boy.1 Hundreds were arrested.

04

What happened next?

  • Concessions: the government granted bonuses to public-sector workers, but repression continued.
  • Independent unions: workers began forming independent unions outside the state-controlled federation.
  • 2011: the April 6 Youth Movement helped organise the protests of 25 January 2011, which, together with strikes by workers, brought down Mubarak.
  • After: under President Abdel Fattah el-Sisi, the April 6 movement was banned in 2014, and its founders were imprisoned.
05

What do others say?

At the time

The government portrayed the protests as riots by criminals. Workers and activists saw them as a struggle for dignity and a living wage.

Debates since

  • Facebook or factory? Some commentators emphasise social media; historians such as Joel Beinin stress the decisive role of workers' strikes.
  • Success or failure? Some called the 6 April strike a failure because the factory was occupied by security forces; others see it as a turning point.1
  • Violence: protesters threw stones and burned property; police fired tear gas and live ammunition, killing two people.1

08

Why does it matter?

The Mahalla strikes show that the Egyptian revolution of 2011 had deep roots in workers' struggles, not only in social media. Women workers played a key role in starting the strike wave.

  • It shows how labour struggles and youth activism can come together.
  • It shows the possibilities and limits of online organising.
