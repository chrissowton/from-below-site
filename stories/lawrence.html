The Bread and Roses Strike

More than twenty thousand migrant textile workers, speaking dozens of languages, shut down the mills of Lawrence and won.

01

What is the background to this event?

Lawrence, Massachusetts, was one of the great textile centres of the United States, dominated by the American Woolen Company and other large mills. Its workforce was overwhelmingly made up of recent immigrants — Italians, Poles, Lithuanians, Syrians, Franco-Canadians, Portuguese, Irish and many others — living in crowded tenements close to the mills.

Living conditions were dire:

  • Many families survived on bread, molasses and beans. One worker later told a congressional inquiry: "When we eat meat it seems like a holiday, especially for the children."
  • Child mortality in Lawrence was among the highest of any American city, and a large proportion of mill workers died young.
  • Children under fourteen worked in the mills alongside adults.

On 1 January 1912 a new Massachusetts law reduced the maximum working week for women and children from 56 to 54 hours. The mill owners reduced pay to match, cutting the earnings of families already living at the margin. The Industrial Workers of the World (IWW), a radical union that sought to organise all workers regardless of skill, sex or nationality, had a small presence in the city.

03

What happened?

On 11 January 1912, Polish women weavers at the Everett Mill discovered that their pay had been cut and walked out. The following day workers at the Washington Mill of the American Woolen Company found the same, and left with the cry "short pay, all out." Within days more than 20,000 workers had joined the strike.1

The strike was organised with great care across language barriers:

  • A strike committee with representatives from each nationality ran the dispute; workers from more than 51 different nationalities took part.1
  • Strike meetings were translated into some 25 languages.1
  • The committee's demands were a 15 per cent increase in wages for a 54-hour week, double pay for overtime, and no discrimination against strikers.1

The authorities responded with force. A local court sentenced 24 workers to a year in jail for throwing ice; the judge stated, "The only way we can teach them is to deal out the severest sentences."1 Governor Eugene Foss ordered out the state militia. After the death of Anna LoPizzo, the organisers Ettor and Giovannitti were arrested and charged as accessories, although they had been elsewhere at the time. Haywood responded with the slogan "Open the jail gates or we will close the mill gates."1

To relieve hungry families, strikers began sending their children to stay with sympathisers in other cities — a tactic used in European strikes. On 10 February, 119 children were sent to New York.1 On 24 February, when parents tried to send another group to Philadelphia, police at the Lawrence railway station attacked mothers and children with clubs. Elizabeth Gurley Flynn later described the work of keeping the strike going:

"I remember one night in Lawrence none of us slept. The strike spirit was in danger of waning for lack of action."Elizabeth Gurley Flynn, 'The Truth About the Paterson Strike', speech of 19142

The violence at the station provoked national outrage and a congressional investigation in March, at which young workers including Carmela Teoli testified. On 12 March the American Woolen Company agreed to the strikers' main demands, and other employers soon followed, raising wages for textile workers across New England.13

04

What happened next?

The victory had wide consequences:

  • Tens of thousands of other textile workers across New England received pay increases as employers sought to avoid similar strikes.
  • The IWW and supporters mounted a national campaign for Ettor and Giovannitti, including a one-day political strike in Lawrence. Both men were acquitted in November 1912.
  • Eugene Debs called it "the most decisive and far-reaching" victory ever won by organised labour, though many gains were eroded in later years as the IWW's influence in Lawrence declined.

The phrase "Bread and Roses", taken from a 1911 poem by James Oppenheim, became attached to the strike in later memory and has since been adopted by labour and feminist movements around the world.

05

What do others say?

At the time

Mill owners and much of the local press blamed the strike on outside agitators from the IWW and on "foreign" workers. The discovery of dynamite in Lawrence early in the strike was used to portray the strikers as dangerous; it later emerged that it had been planted, and a local businessman connected to the mill owners was charged. Many in the established labour movement, including the American Federation of Labor, were also hostile to the IWW.

Historians' debates

  • Who led the strike? Some accounts focus on IWW organisers such as Ettor, Haywood and Flynn; social historians such as Ardis Cameron have emphasised the leadership of women and of neighbourhood networks.
  • "Bread and Roses": there is little evidence that the slogan was actually used during the strike. It appears to have become associated with it later, through Oppenheim's poem and labour tradition.
  • Long-term impact: the IWW's influence in Lawrence declined rapidly, and some gains were lost within a few years. Historians debate whether the strike should be seen as a lasting victory.
06

The evidence

  • The congressional hearings of March 1912, which recorded testimony from strikers, including children, and from officials.
  • Press coverage, often sharply divided between pro-company and sympathetic papers.
  • Photographs, including those of militia with bayonets and of the children's evacuation.
  • Later speeches and memoirs by IWW organisers, which were partly intended to promote the union.

How is it remembered?

  • Lawrence holds an annual Bread and Roses Heritage Festival each Labor Day.
  • The song "Bread and Roses", set to music by Mimi Fariña in 1974, has been recorded by many artists.
  • The strike featured in the 2014 film Pride, in which the song is sung, linking it to later movements.
08

Why does it matter?

Employers have often relied on divisions of language, religion and nationality to keep workers apart. Lawrence showed that those divisions could be overcome through deliberate, democratic organisation. It is also a powerful example of migrant workers not as a "problem" for society but as people fighting for dignity and a decent life.

  • It showed that unskilled, mostly migrant and largely female workers could win a major industrial dispute.
  • The evacuation of the children demonstrated how a strike could be sustained through community solidarity beyond the workplace.
  • The demand for "roses" as well as bread — for time, beauty and dignity, not only wages — continues to resonate.

What other events are like this?

  • 1888The Matchwomen's Strike — About 1,400 women and girls walked out of a match factory, won their demands in three weeks, and founded the largest union of women in Britain.
  • 1834The Tolpuddle Martyrs — Six farm labourers formed a society to resist wage cuts and were sentenced to transportation to Australia.
  • 1966The Wave Hill Walk-Off — Gurindji stockmen and their families walked off a British-owned cattle station. What began as a strike over pay became the foundation of the Aboriginal land rights movement.
