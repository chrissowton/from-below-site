LGBTQ+ liberation

Striking Down Section 377

A colonial-era law made gay sex a crime in India for 158 years. After a long campaign by activists, lawyers and ordinary queer Indians, the Supreme Court struck it down in 2018.

When
2001 – 2018
Where
New Delhi, India

01

What is the background to this event?

Section 377 of the Indian Penal Code, introduced by the British in 1860, criminalised "carnal intercourse against the order of nature". It was used against gay men, transgender people and others.1

  • The same law was exported to other British colonies, and similar laws remain in several countries today.1
  • The law enabled police harassment, blackmail and violence, even where prosecutions were rare.

In their own words

03

What happened?

In 2001, the Naz Foundation challenged Section 377 in the Delhi High Court.2

  • In 2009, the Delhi High Court ruled that the law was unconstitutional as applied to consenting adults.2
  • In 2013, in Koushal v. Naz, the Supreme Court overturned that decision and restored the law.12
  • In 2016, five LGBT people — Navtej Singh Johar, Sunil Mehra, Ritu Dalmia, Aman Nath and Ayesha Kapur, later joined by Keshav Suri — filed a new petition.2
  • Some church groups led the opposition to decriminalisation in court.2
  • On 6 September 2018, a five-judge bench of the Supreme Court ruled unanimously that the law was unconstitutional in so far as it criminalised consensual sex between adults of the same sex.2

04

What happened next?

  • Celebration: crowds celebrated across India after the judgment.
  • Unfinished business: India does not yet recognise same-sex marriage; in 2023 the Supreme Court declined to do so, leaving it to parliament.
  • Transgender rights: the Supreme Court had recognised transgender people as a "third gender" in 2014.
05

What do others say?

At the time

Supporters of the law argued that it protected morality and religion; opponents argued it violated equality, privacy and dignity.2

Debates since

  • Courts or parliament? some argue rights should be won through elected politicians, others that courts must protect minorities.
  • Colonial legacy: activists stress that the law was imposed by the British, not rooted in Indian tradition.1

07

How is it remembered?

  • 6 September is celebrated by many LGBTQ+ Indians.
  • Pride marches are held in many Indian cities.
08

Why does it matter?

The ruling ended a colonial-era law and affirmed the dignity of LGBTQ+ people in the world's largest democracy.

  • It was won through a long campaign, including a major setback in 2013.
  • It influenced debates in other former British colonies.
