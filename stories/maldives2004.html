Black Friday: The Maldives' Largest Protest

A small vigil for a detained reformist grew overnight into the largest protest in the country's history, calling for the president to go after 26 years. After 22 hours, security forces broke it up with batons and tear gas.

When
12 – 13 August 2004
Where
Republic Square, Malé, Maldives
Facts checked

01

What is the background to this event?

Maumoon Abdul Gayoom had been president of the Maldives since 1978. Reform activists were being detained — without fair trial, according to the opposition.1

  • Writers of an internet magazine, Sandhaanu, were among those imprisoned.1

In their own words

03

What happened?

On the evening of 12 August 2004, a few reform activists gathered near Republic Square in Malé to ask why a fellow activist had been detained.1

  • He was released, but the crowd grew and demanded the release of other reformists held without fair trial.1
  • Their demands included the release of the Sandhaanu writers and the resignation of the president and two of his brothers who were ministers.1
  • Early on 13 August, the detained reformists were freed and came to the square, where they called for Gayoom to resign. The crowd kept growing.1
  • That morning, two police officers were stabbed, a fire broke out in a government building and objects were thrown at police headquarters. The authorities blamed the protesters; reformists said pro-government agitators were responsible.1
  • In the afternoon, after a final warning, the National Security Service charged the crowd with batons and used tear gas. Many people were beaten and arrested.1
  • The president declared a state of emergency. Internet and text messaging services were shut down.1
04

What happened next?

  • Pardons: after nearly two months, the state of emergency was lifted under international pressure. Following the tsunami of December 2004, Gayoom pardoned all those arrested.1
  • The anniversary: in August 2005, Mohamed Nasheed sat down in Republic Square to mark the anniversary. He was removed by riot police, and three nights of unrest followed.1
  • Democracy: in 2008, the Maldives held its first multi-party presidential election. Nasheed defeated Gayoom.4
05

What do others say?

At the time

State television reported only a small crowd that had caused disorder, and later called the gathering "illegal in every possible sense"; reformists described a peaceful protest attacked by security forces.1

Debates since

  • Who started the violence? The authorities blamed the protesters for the stabbings and the fire; reformists said they were the work of pro-government provocateurs. Our main source is itself sympathetic to the reformists.1
  • Violence: many people were injured; our sources report no deaths.1
06

The evidence

  • Photographs and video that reached foreign media despite the shutdown, reports by Amnesty International, and broadcasts by state television.1
07

How is it remembered?

  • The day is remembered as Black Friday, and its anniversary became an occasion for protest.1
08

Why does it matter?

Black Friday was the moment when demands for reform in the Maldives became a mass movement. Four years later, the country held its first multi-party presidential election.14
