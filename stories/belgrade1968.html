Belgrade 1968: 'Down with the Red Bourgeoisie'

In socialist Yugoslavia, students occupied their university and accused the party elite of betraying socialism. Tito went on television and told the nation: 'The students are right.'

When
2 – 9 June 1968
Where
Belgrade, Yugoslavia (Serbia)
Facts checked
2026-11-15

See it on the map →

01

What is the background to this event?

After the Second World War, Josip Broz Tito's Yugoslavia broke with Stalin and developed its own form of socialism, with "workers' self-management" of factories.

  • By the 1960s, economic reforms introduced market mechanisms. Unemployment rose, and hundreds of thousands of Yugoslavs left to work in Western Europe.1
  • Many young people felt that a privileged party elite — a "red bourgeoisie" — enjoyed wealth and power while workers' self-management was hollow.
  • Philosophers of the Praxis school criticised the system from a humanist Marxist perspective.
  • Student protests were sweeping the world in 1968, from Paris to Prague.

In their own words

03

What happened?

On the night of 2 June 1968, a clash between students and police at a concert sparked a seven-day student strike at Belgrade University.1 It was the first mass protest in Yugoslavia since the Second World War.1

  • The police beat students and banned public gatherings.1
  • Ignoring the ban, students gathered at the Faculty of Philosophy — renamed the "Red University Karl Marx" — held debates on social justice, and distributed the banned magazine Student.1
  • They protested against unemployment, the economic reforms that forced workers to seek jobs abroad, and the privileges of the elite.1
  • Protests spread to Sarajevo, Zagreb and Ljubljana. In Ljubljana, more than 5,000 people gathered and were violently dispersed by police; hundreds were injured.1
  • Leading cultural figures supported the protests.1

On 9 June, Tito gave a televised speech conceding that "the students are right" and promising to meet some of their demands. The protests ended.1

04

What happened next?

  • Retaliation: in the following years, the regime imprisoned student leaders such as Vladimir Mijanović, Milan Nikolić, Pavluško Imširović and Lazar Stojanović, and dismissed critical professors.1
  • Praxis: in 1975, eight Praxis philosophers were expelled from Belgrade University.
  • The Belgrade Six: in 1984, six dissidents, including former 1968 activists, were tried for "hostile propaganda".
  • Collapse: after Tito's death in 1980, economic crisis and nationalism led to the break-up of Yugoslavia and the wars of the 1990s.
05

Why does it matter?

The Belgrade student protest was a socialist critique of a socialist state. Students used the regime's own ideals to challenge its privileges and failures.

  • It shows how protest can take different forms in different political systems.
  • It raises questions about how leaders co-opt protest movements by agreeing with them — and then punishing their leaders.
  • In the following years the regime imprisoned student leaders and dismissed critical professors.1

What do others say?How it was seen at the time, and where historians disagree

At the time

Party officials at first blamed foreign influences and anarchists; Tito then embraced the students' criticisms. The students saw themselves as defenders of true socialism.

Historians' debates

  • Was Tito sincere? Historians see his speech as a clever move that defused the protest; later repression suggests he had no intention of fundamental change.
  • The left and nationalism: some note that the left-wing protest of 1968 was followed by nationalist movements, such as the Croatian Spring of 1971.

Reading it

One of Yugoslavia's best-known press photographers captured the students in Belgrade. Their mood looks confident rather than angry. Whether a Yugoslav newspaper could have published such an image in 1968 is itself a question about how far the regime tolerated dissent.

How is it remembered?Memorials, anniversaries and arguments over memory
  • The protests are remembered by students in Serbia, and have been recalled in later student-led protests in Serbia.
