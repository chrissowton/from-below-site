The Tunisian Revolution

A street vendor's desperate protest in a small town sparked a month of uprising that toppled a dictator of 23 years and began the Arab Spring.

When
17 December 2010 – 14 January 2011
Where
Sidi Bouzid and Tunis, Tunisia
Facts checked
2026-11-16

01

What is the background to this event?

Zine El Abidine Ben Ali had ruled Tunisia since 1987. His government was praised abroad for economic growth, stability and women's rights, but at home it was authoritarian:

  • Elections were not free, the press was censored, opponents were imprisoned and tortured, and the internet was tightly controlled.
  • The president's family, especially that of his wife, Leila Trabelsi, was widely seen as corrupt. In late 2010, leaked US diplomatic cables published by WikiLeaks described the family's corruption in detail.1
  • Unemployment was high, especially among young graduates, and the interior regions, such as Sidi Bouzid, Kasserine and Gafsa, were much poorer than the coast. In 2008, protests in the Gafsa mining basin had been violently repressed.
02

Who were the key people?

  • Mohamed Bouazizi

    A 26-year-old street vendor in Sidi Bouzid whose self-immolation sparked the uprising. He died on 4 January 2011.

  • Zine El Abidine Ben Ali

    President of Tunisia from 1987 to 2011. He fled to Saudi Arabia, where he died in 2019.

  • The UGTT

    The Tunisian General Labour Union, whose local and regional branches helped to organise and spread the protests.

  • The young unemployed and the bloggers

    Young people, many of them graduates, and online activists who spread images of the protests despite censorship.

  • The lawyers

    Members of the Tunisian bar, who demonstrated in their robes and were among the first professional groups to join the protests.

In their own words

03

What happened?

On the morning of 17 December 2010, a municipal official confiscated the fruit and vegetables of Mohamed Bouazizi, a street vendor in the town of Sidi Bouzid, on the grounds that he had no licence. Bouazizi tried to pay the 10-dinar fine — a day's wages — and a scuffle followed. Humiliated, he went to the provincial headquarters to complain and to get his goods back, but was ignored. Within an hour of the confrontation, he returned, doused himself with a flammable liquid and set himself on fire.1

His act, and the heavy-handed police response to peaceful marchers, set off protests in Sidi Bouzid the next day. Despite censorship, images spread on social media and through the Qatari television channel Al Jazeera.1 Over the following weeks the protests spread across the country:1

  • On 24 December, a protester, Mohamed Ammari, was shot dead by police in Bouziane. Police claimed to have fired in "self-defence".
  • President Ben Ali visited Bouazizi in hospital on 28 December, in an attempt to calm the unrest. Bouazizi died on 4 January 2011.
  • In early January, protests in Thala, Kasserine and other towns of the interior were met with live fire, and many were killed.
  • Regional branches of the UGTT trade union called general strikes, and lawyers, teachers and students joined the protests.

Ben Ali promised 300,000 new jobs, then promised not to stand for re-election, but the protests continued. On 14 January 2011, huge crowds gathered outside the Ministry of the Interior in Tunis, chanting "Dégage!" ("Get out!"). That evening, Ben Ali fled to Saudi Arabia, ending 23 years in power.1

In total, 338 people were killed and 2,174 injured during the revolution.1

04

What happened next?

  • Continued protest: protests continued until members of Ben Ali's ruling party (the RCD) were removed from the interim government; the party was dissolved in March 2011.
  • Free elections: in October 2011, Tunisia held its first free elections, for a Constituent Assembly. The Islamist Ennahda party won the most seats and formed a coalition with two secular parties.
  • Crisis and dialogue: after the assassination of two left-wing politicians in 2013, the country was brought back from the brink by a National Dialogue, led by four civil-society organisations — the UGTT, the employers' federation, the Human Rights League and the Order of Lawyers. A new democratic constitution was adopted in January 2014.
  • Nobel Prize: the National Dialogue Quartet was awarded the 2015 Nobel Peace Prize.
  • Reversal: in July 2021 President Kais Saied suspended parliament and began to rule by decree. A new constitution in 2022 concentrated power in the presidency, and many critics and opposition figures have since been imprisoned.
05

What do others say?

At the time

The Ben Ali government blamed the unrest on "terrorists" and foreign media. Western governments, especially France, which had long supported Ben Ali, were slow to criticise him; France's foreign minister even offered French police expertise in crowd control days before he fell.

Historians' and commentators' debates

  • The Bouazizi story: early reports that Bouazizi was a university graduate and had been slapped by a policewoman were widely used to mobilise protesters. The official involved, Fedia Hamdi, denied slapping him, and the case against her was later dropped; many details of the original story were inaccurate.
  • A Facebook revolution? Some commentators credited social media and WikiLeaks; others argue that the decisive factors were the trade unions, the economic grievances of the interior and the army's refusal to fire on protesters.
  • Why did it end the way it did? Analysts debate whether the 2021 power grab reflected a failure of the democratic transition, the country's economic problems, or popular frustration with political parties.

The evidence

  • The report of the national commission of inquiry into abuses during the revolution, and later the Truth and Dignity Commission (2014–2018).
  • The leaked diplomatic cables published by WikiLeaks.

07

How is it remembered?

  • 17 December is marked in Tunisia as Revolution Day. A monument to Bouazizi stands in Sidi Bouzid, and squares are named after him in Tunis and in Paris.
  • The anniversary of 14 January has become a day of protest against the reversal of democratic gains.
  • Bouazizi was posthumously awarded the European Parliament's Sakharov Prize in 2011, together with other Arab Spring activists.
08

Why does it matter?

The Tunisian Revolution was the first of the uprisings that became known as the Arab Spring. Within weeks, protests had spread to Egypt, Libya, Yemen, Bahrain, Syria and beyond. For a decade, Tunisia was the only country of the Arab Spring to make a lasting transition to democracy.

  • It showed that a long-entrenched dictatorship could be overthrown by a largely unarmed popular movement in a matter of weeks.
  • It highlighted the anger of young people facing unemployment, corruption and humiliation by the state.
  • Its later history raises difficult questions about how democratic gains can be defended.

Questions to think about

  1. Why do you think one man's act of protest had such enormous consequences?
  2. How important were social media and WikiLeaks compared with trade unions and street protests?
  3. Why did Tunisia's democratic transition last longer than those elsewhere in the Arab Spring?
  4. How can democratic gains be lost? What can be done to defend them?

What other events are like this?

  • 1919Egypt's 1919 Revolution — When Britain exiled the nationalist leader Saad Zaghlul, Egyptians of every class and religion rose up in strikes and demonstrations — and women marched in public for the first time.
  • 1986The People Power Revolution — More than two million unarmed Filipinos filled a Manila highway to protect rebel soldiers and end Ferdinand Marcos's twenty-year rule.
  • 1980The Gdańsk Shipyard Strike and Solidarity — A strike over the sacking of a crane operator grew into the first independent trade union in the Soviet bloc, with ten million members.
