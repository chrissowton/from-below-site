The Basotho Gun War

When the Cape Colony ordered the Basotho to hand in their guns, they refused and fought. It was a rare African victory over a colonial power — and the reason Lesotho was never swallowed by South Africa.

When
September 1880 – April 1881
Where
Mafeteng, Lesotho
Facts checked
2026-11-16

See it on the map →

01

What is the background to this event?

King Moshoeshoe I had united the Basotho nation in the early nineteenth century.1

  • In 1868, Basutoland became British territory, and it was later placed under the Cape Colony.1
  • The Cape pursued rapid "westernisation" of Basotho society.1
  • In 1879, the Cape Parliament extended its Peace Preservation Act to Basutoland, ordering the Basotho to give up their guns.1
  • Guns were hugely important in Basotho society; many men had bought them with wages earned in the diamond mines.

03

What happened?

On 13 September 1880, the rebellion led by Lerotholi and Masopha broke out.1

  • Basotho fighters besieged the Cape forces at Mafeteng.1
  • Basotho cavalry harried Cape columns with rifle fire; at Qalabane in October 1880, the Cape lost 32 killed and the Basotho 40.1
  • The Cape forces, outnumbered and stretched by other revolts, could not win a decisive victory.1
  • The war became costly and unpopular in the Cape, and on 29 April 1881 a settlement was announced.1
  • The Basotho kept their guns.1
04

What happened next?

  • Disannexation: unable to control Basutoland, the Cape Parliament gave it up in 1883, and it became a British High Commission Territory.1
  • Independence: as a result, Basutoland was not included in the Union of South Africa, and became independent Lesotho in 1966.1
05

What do others say?

At the time

The Cape saw a rebellion against the law; the Basotho argued that annexation and disarmament broke earlier agreements, and that they had always been loyal.1

Debates since

  • A divided nation: some Basotho chiefs sided with the Cape, and the war was partly a struggle among chiefs.2
  • Violence: both sides lost men in battle; villages and crops were burned during the fighting.12

07

How is it remembered?

  • The war is remembered in Lesotho as a defence of the nation's independence.
08

Why does it matter?

The Gun War was a rare military victory by an African nation against a colonial power.1

  • It helps explain why Lesotho exists as an independent country today.
