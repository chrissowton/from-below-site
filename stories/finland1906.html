The vote & democracy

The First Women in Parliament

A general strike against Russian rule forced the Tsar to grant Finland a new parliament — elected by all adults, women included. In 1907, nineteen women took their seats, among them a former maidservant.

When
1905 – 1907
Where
Helsinki and across Finland

See it on the map →

01

What is the background to this event?

From 1809 Finland was a self-governing Grand Duchy within the Russian Empire. Its old Diet of four estates represented only a small part of the population.1

  • Taxpaying women had won the vote in local elections in the countryside in 1863 and in towns in 1872.1
  • The Finnish Women's Association was founded in 1884, and the Union of Women's Associations (Naisasialiitto Unioni), devoted to the vote, in 1892.1
  • Around 1900, the Russian government tried to reduce Finland's autonomy.
  • Working-class women organised in the labour movement, and demanded the vote for all, not just the propertied.
02

Who were the key people?

  • Miina Sillanpää

    A former maidservant and labour organiser, elected in 1907 and later Finland's first woman minister.

  • Lucina Hagman

    A teacher and founder of the Union of Women's Associations, elected in 1907.

  • Hilja Pärssinen

    A Social Democrat teacher and poet, also elected.

  • Strikers across Finland

    Workers, students and civil servants who joined the Great Strike of 1905.

03

What happened?

In late October 1905, during the revolution in Russia, a general strike spread to Finland. Railways, factories, offices and shops stopped; it became known as the Great Strike.2

  • Strikers demanded the restoration of Finland's rights and a parliament elected by universal suffrage.2
  • Women's organisations and labour women held mass meetings demanding that women be included.
  • The Tsar gave way. In 1906 the old Diet voted to replace itself with a single-chamber parliament elected by all adults over 24.12
  • Finland became the first country in Europe to give women the vote, and the first in the world to give women the full right both to vote and to stand for parliament.1
  • In the first election, in March 1907, nineteen women were elected out of 200 members.1

04

What happened next?

  • The new MPs proposed laws on married women's property rights, maternity protection and the conditions of servants.
  • Independence: Finland declared independence in 1917. A civil war followed in 1918.
  • Miina Sillanpää became the first woman government minister in Finland in 1926.
05

What do others say?

At the time

Some conservatives opposed votes for women, and for propertyless men. Some middle-class women's groups at first favoured a more limited franchise.3

Debates since

  • Who won it? Historians debate the weight of the women's movement, the labour movement and the Russian crisis.3
  • Non-violence: the Great Strike was largely peaceful, though tensions grew between workers' and middle-class militias.2

07

How is it remembered?

  • The photograph of the nineteen women of 1907 is one of Finland's best-known historical images.
08

Why does it matter?

Finland's reform showed that women's suffrage could come in one step, together with votes for working-class men, as part of a wider struggle for democracy.

  • It linked the women's movement with the labour movement and national resistance.

09

What other events are like this?

  • 1893Women Win the Vote in New Zealand — A nationwide petition campaign made New Zealand the first self-governing country in which all women could vote in parliamentary elections.
  • 1991The Swiss Women's Strike — 'If women want, everything stands still.' Ten years after equality was written into the constitution with little effect, half a million Swiss women stopped work, at home and in the workplace.
  • 1975Iceland's Women's Day Off — Around 90 per cent of Iceland's women stopped work — paid and unpaid — for a day, to show how much the country depended on them.
