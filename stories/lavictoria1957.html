La Victoria: The Land Seizure That Built a Neighbourhood

One night in 1957, some 1,200 families from a riverside shanty town occupied an empty farm on the edge of Santiago. They laid out their own streets, built their own homes and named the streets after their heroes.

Where

01

What is the background to this event?

In the 1950s, many poor families in Santiago lived in shanty towns, including along the Zanjón de la Aguada, a channel known as the 'belt of misery'.1

03

What happened?

On 30 October 1957, about 1,200 families from the Zanjón de la Aguada took over the land of the La Feria farm. It is described as the first organised land seizure in Chile and Latin America.1

  • The residents divided the land into plots themselves, planned the public spaces and built them.1
  • They set up watch committees to keep order and even published their own newspaper, La Voz de La Victoria.1
  • Cardinal Caro interceded with President Carlos Ibáñez del Campo to stop the new settlement from being evicted.1
  • The residents named their streets after people and events from the struggles of working people — including the Chicago Martyrs of 1886 and Ramona Parra, a young communist killed at a protest in 1946.1
04

What happened next?

  • A model: the seizure became a model for poor residents elsewhere in Santiago organising to demand housing, and influenced social policy and the 1958 presidential election.1
  • Under the dictatorship: La Victoria became a centre of opposition to Pinochet's regime, with protests and clashes with police.1
  • A priest killed: in 1984, the French priest André Jarlan was killed by a police bullet that came through the wall of his room as he read the Bible.1
05

What do others say?

At the time

The authorities saw an illegal occupation; the families saw the only way to get a home.1

Debates since

  • Law and need: land seizures raise the question of whether the right to housing can justify taking land.

07

How is it remembered?

  • Víctor Jara's album La población (1972) tells of the pobladores' struggles, and the band Illapu recorded a song named after La Victoria.1
08

Why does it matter?

La Victoria shows people without homes not waiting for the state but building a neighbourhood themselves — and organising it as a community, from its streets to its newspaper.1

Open questions

  1. Why might families risk eviction to occupy land?
  2. What does it tell us that the residents planned their own streets and spaces?
  3. Why did they name the streets after working-class heroes?
  4. How do land seizures like this compare with squatting movements elsewhere?

What other events are like this?

  • 1946The Squatters of 1946 — Returning from war to find no homes, tens of thousands of families moved into empty army camps across Britain. In London, Communist activists led squatters into empty luxury flats.
  • 2014Brazil's Homeless Workers' Movement (MTST) — In a country with millions of families in inadequate housing and millions of empty homes, the MTST occupies unused land and buildings to demand housing — sometimes in tent cities of thousands of families.
  • 2005Abahlali baseMjondolo — the Shack Dwellers' Movement — Residents of a Durban shack settlement blocked a highway to demand the land and homes they had been promised. Their movement grew to tens of thousands — and has faced arrests, attacks and the killing of its leaders.
