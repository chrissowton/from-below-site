The Casablanca Uprising of 1965

Secondary school students marched against a rule that would end the education of most of them. The poor of the shanty towns joined them, and the army fired on the crowds from tanks and a helicopter.

When
22 – 24 March 1965
Where
Casablanca, Morocco

See it on the map →

01

What is the background to this event?

King Hassan II came to the throne in 1961, and a new constitution kept power in the monarchy's hands.1

  • The opposition party UNFP, led by Mehdi Ben Barka, and the student union UNEM criticised the king.1
  • Eleven UNFP leaders were sentenced to death, and Ben Barka fled into exile.1
  • In 1965, the education minister issued an order barring pupils over 17 from the upper years of secondary school — excluding about 60% of them.1
02

Who were the key people?

  • Secondary school students of Casablanca

    Who began the protest.

  • Parents, workers and the unemployed

    Who joined them on the second day.

  • General Mohamed Oufkir

    Interior minister who led the repression.

  • Mehdi Ben Barka

    Exiled opposition leader; disappeared in Paris later in 1965.

03

What happened?

On 22 March 1965, about 15,000 students gathered at the Lycée Mohammed V in Casablanca to march for the right to education.1

  • Police broke up the march and opened fire.1
  • The next day, students were joined by parents, workers, the unemployed and people from the shanty towns.1
  • Protesters attacked shops, burned buses and cars, and threw stones.1
  • The army and police were deployed with tanks, and General Oufkir fired on the crowd from a helicopter.1
  • The king blamed teachers and parents, and said in a speech that intellectuals were the greatest danger to the state.1

04

What happened next?

  • Death toll: the authorities reported about a dozen deaths; the foreign press and the UNFP counted more than 1,000.1
  • State of exception: in June 1965, the king suspended parliament and ruled directly.
  • Ben Barka: in October 1965, Ben Barka was abducted in Paris and never seen again.
  • The "Years of Lead": decades of repression followed.
05

What do others say?

At the time

The king blamed teachers, parents and intellectuals; the opposition blamed the regime's repression and inequality.1

Debates since

  • Truth and reconciliation: in 2004, Morocco's Equity and Reconciliation Commission began investigating abuses of this period.
  • Violence on both sides: rioters burned vehicles and attacked shops; security forces killed between a dozen and more than 1,000 people.1
06

The evidence

  • Witness accounts and foreign press reports.1
07

How is it remembered?

  • 23 March gave its name to a Moroccan left-wing movement of the 1970s.
08

Why does it matter?

The Casablanca uprising began with young people demanding education, and became a turning point towards harsh repression in Morocco.
