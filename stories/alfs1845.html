The Alleged Lunatics' Friend Society

Former asylum patients who had been locked up against their will joined together to defend others. They are now seen as pioneers of the psychiatric survivors' movement — people with experience of the system speaking for themselves.

When

Where

01

What is the background to this event?

In 19th-century Britain there was wide concern about people being wrongly shut up in private madhouses and about the mistreatment of patients.1

  • Doctors were establishing themselves as judges of sanity, but their diagnoses were subjective and often treated eccentric or immoral behaviour as madness. Private madhouses made profits, which added to public suspicion.1
  • In 1838, Richard Paternoster was released after 41 days in a London madhouse, where he had been detained after a dispute with his father over money. He announced his release in a letter to The Times.1
  • The letter was read by John Perceval, son of a former prime minister, who had spent three years in two private asylums and had been brutally treated in one of them.1

03

What happened?

Paternoster and Perceval were joined by other former patients and supporters. They wrote to the press, lobbied MPs, published pamphlets and took legal action against those who had confined them.1

  • On 7 July 1845, they formally founded the Alleged Lunatics' Friend Society, to protect people from unjust confinement and cruel treatment and to reform the lunacy laws.1
  • Four days later, Thomas Duncombe described cases of wrongful confinement in the House of Commons. The Society's supporters won some safeguards for patients in the Lunacy Act of 1845.1
  • The Society never had more than about sixty members and relied on its own money. The Times mocked it, suggesting its members were acting out of "self-preservation".1
  • Over some twenty years it took up the cases of at least seventy patients, including a German tutor held in Bethlem Hospital for fourteen years.1
  • With patients' help, it persuaded the Home Secretary to let inspectors into Bethlem, which had been exempt from inspection. Their critical report in 1852 led to reforms.1
04

What happened next?

  • An inquiry at last: after many petitions, Parliament held an inquiry into the lunacy laws in 1859. Perceval and a former patient the Society had freed gave evidence, but the committee's recommendations were not put into place.1
  • The end: the Society's activities seem to have ended in the 1860s. In 1862, Perceval wrote that it was so little supported that it was "rather a myth than a reality".1
  • Successors: Louisa Lowe's Lunacy Law Reform Association later took up similar aims.1
05

Why does it matter?

The Society insisted that people who had been patients were worth listening to about how patients were treated. That idea — "nothing about us without us" — is at the heart of the disability rights movement today.1

  • Later campaigners, including Louisa Lowe's Lunacy Law Reform Association, took up similar aims.1
  • After years of petitions, Parliament held an inquiry into the lunacy laws in 1859, where former patients gave evidence.1
  • It is recognised today as a pioneer of advocacy and of the psychiatric survivors' movement.1

What do others say?How it was seen at the time, and where historians disagree

At the time

The Times argued that abuses should be checked by the law, not by "a body of private individuals"; Perceval replied that the law gave patients too little protection.1

Debates since

  • Why so little support? Nicholas Hervey, who has written the fullest history of the Society, points to its links with radical politics, its opposition to central control by the Lunacy Commission, and its readiness to expose upper-class families.1

Reading it

The Society's first statement speaks of the rights of 'the British subject' — the language of liberty and the law, not of medicine. That was a deliberate choice by people who had been locked up: they were claiming rights, not asking for better treatment. Coming from former patients, it was easy for the press to dismiss.
