Benin's National Conference

Strikes by unpaid teachers, students and civil servants forced Benin's military ruler to call a national conference. The delegates declared themselves sovereign, took power peacefully — and inspired democratic movements across Africa.

When
19 – 28 February 1990
Where
Cotonou, Benin
Facts checked
2026-11-15

See it on the map →

01

What is the background to this event?

Mathieu Kérékou seized power in a military coup in 1972. In 1974 he declared Benin a Marxist–Leninist state, ruled by a single party.1

  • By the late 1980s, the economy had collapsed. Banks failed, and the state could not pay its employees.1
  • In 1989, Kérékou accepted an IMF austerity programme that cut public spending.1
  • Students, teachers and civil servants, many unpaid for months, went on strike.
  • In December 1989, the government abandoned Marxism–Leninism and agreed to a national conference.

03

What happened?

The National Conference of the Active Forces of the Nation met in Cotonou from 19 to 28 February 1990. Delegates represented unions, students, religious groups, parties, exiles and the regime.2

  • Archbishop Isidore de Souza chaired the conference.2
  • The conference was called to address the economic crisis, but declared itself sovereign and took decisions binding on the government.12
  • It stripped Kérékou of most of his powers, appointed Nicéphore Soglo as prime minister, and created a High Council of the Republic to run a transition.12
  • Kérékou accepted its decisions, and publicly asked for forgiveness.
  • A new democratic constitution was approved by referendum in December 1990.

04

What happened next?

  • 1991 election: Soglo defeated Kérékou in the presidential election. Kérékou accepted defeat — one of the first times in Africa that a ruler lost power peacefully at the ballot box.1
  • Return: Kérékou won the 1996 election and served two more terms.1
  • A model: national conferences followed in Gabon, Congo, Mali, Niger, Togo and Zaire.3
  • Since: Benin was long seen as one of Africa's most stable democracies, though critics have warned of democratic backsliding since 2016.
05

What do others say?

At the time

Supporters called the conference a "civilian coup"; some regime loyalists feared it was illegal.

Debates since

  • From below or above? Historians debate the relative importance of strikes, church mediation, France's pressure and the end of the Cold War.
  • Peaceful: the transition was largely peaceful, which made it unusual.1

How is it remembered?

  • The conference is remembered in Benin as the beginning of democratic renewal.
08

Why does it matter?

Benin's conference showed that a peaceful, negotiated transition from dictatorship was possible, and became a model for much of French-speaking Africa.

  • It was driven by pressure from below: unpaid workers and students.

09

What other events are like this?

  • 1991Mali's March Revolution — Students and democracy campaigners marched against 23 years of military rule. Soldiers fired on them — and then other soldiers refused, overthrew the dictator, and handed power to an elected government.
  • 1990Mongolia's Democratic Revolution — Young Mongolians gathered in the freezing main square of Ulaanbaatar to demand democracy. A hunger strike in March 1990 brought down the communist leadership — without a single death.
  • 1986The People Power Revolution — More than two million unarmed Filipinos filled a Manila highway to protect rebel soldiers and end Ferdinand Marcos's twenty-year rule.
