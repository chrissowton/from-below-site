The Scopes Trial

A young teacher was prosecuted for teaching evolution. The case was deliberately staged as a test of free thought, and became one of the most famous trials in American history.

When
10–21 July 1925
Where
Rhea County Courthouse, Dayton, Tennessee, USA

01

What is the background to this event?

In the 1920s the United States was divided by rapid cultural change. Cities were growing, new technologies and mass media were spreading, and a growing number of young people were attending high school. Within American Protestantism, a "Fundamentalist–Modernist" controversy divided those who accepted modern biblical scholarship and science from those who insisted on a literal reading of the Bible.

The teaching of Darwin's theory of evolution became a focus of this conflict. William Jennings Bryan — a three-time Democratic presidential candidate, former Secretary of State and champion of ordinary farmers — campaigned against it, arguing that it undermined morality and religious faith.

  • On 21 March 1925 Tennessee's governor, Austin Peay, signed the Butler Act, which made it unlawful to teach "any theory that denies the story of the Divine Creation of man as taught in the Bible" in public schools.
  • The newly founded American Civil Liberties Union (ACLU) announced that it would defend any teacher prosecuted under the Act, in order to challenge its constitutionality.
  • Ironically, the state's own approved biology textbook, George William Hunter's A Civic Biology, described and endorsed evolution.

In their own words

03

What happened?

In early May 1925, George Rappleyea met local businessmen at Robinson's Drug Store in Dayton and persuaded them that a test case would bring the town valuable publicity. They summoned John Scopes, who agreed to be charged.3 Scopes was charged on 5 May and indicted on 25 May, after three students testified to the grand jury.3 One student later told reporters:

"I believe in part of evolution, but I don't believe in the monkey business."A Dayton high school student, 19253

The trial opened on 10 July 1925 and drew hundreds of reporters. It was the first trial in American history to be broadcast nationally on radio.3

  • The judge, John Raulston, refused to allow the defence's scientific experts to testify before the jury.
  • Defence lawyer Dudley Field Malone, in one of the trial's most famous speeches, declared that "there is never a duel with the truth."3
  • Because of the heat and the crowds, proceedings on 20 July moved to the courthouse lawn, where Darrow called Bryan himself to the stand as an expert on the Bible. Darrow questioned him about the age of the earth and the literal truth of biblical stories. Bryan accused Darrow of trying to "slur the Bible".3

On 21 July the jury took only a few minutes to find Scopes guilty, and the judge fined him $100.3 The defence had in fact wanted a conviction, so that the case could be appealed to a higher court.

04

What happened next?

  • Bryan's death: William Jennings Bryan died in Dayton on 26 July 1925, five days after the trial ended.
  • The appeal: in 1927 the Tennessee Supreme Court upheld the Butler Act but overturned Scopes's conviction on a technicality: the fine should have been set by the jury, not the judge. This prevented the case reaching the US Supreme Court.
  • The chilling effect: many textbook publishers reduced or removed coverage of evolution for decades, and other states passed similar laws.
  • Repeal: Tennessee repealed the Butler Act in 1967. In 1968, in Epperson v. Arkansas, the US Supreme Court ruled that laws banning the teaching of evolution violated the Constitution's prohibition on establishing religion.
05

What do others say?

At the time

Supporters of the Butler Act argued that parents and taxpayers, through their elected representatives, had the right to decide what was taught in public schools, and that evolution threatened children's faith and morals. Bryan also feared that "survival of the fittest" ideas were being used to justify war, eugenics and the exploitation of the weak. Many people in rural Tennessee resented the condescension of northern journalists such as Mencken.

Historians' debates

  • A publicity stunt? The case was set up deliberately, and Dayton's business leaders hoped to profit from it. Some historians see it more as theatre than as a genuine prosecution.
  • The textbook problem: Hunter's Civic Biology, which Scopes was accused of teaching from, also promoted eugenics and contained openly racist statements about a hierarchy of races — a reminder that "science" in the 1920s was not free of prejudice.
  • Bryan's reputation: Edward Larson and others have argued that Bryan has been unfairly caricatured as a simple bigot, noting his lifelong support for progressive causes such as women's suffrage and the regulation of big business.
  • Inherit the Wind: the 1955 play and 1960 film, written partly as an allegory of McCarthyism, fictionalised the trial heavily, and many popular beliefs about it come from the film rather than from the record.

How is it remembered?

  • The Rhea County Courthouse in Dayton is a National Historic Landmark and houses a museum about the trial.
  • Dayton holds an annual Scopes Trial Play and Festival.
  • In 2005 a statue of Bryan was placed outside the courthouse, joined in 2017 by a statue of Darrow — a sign of the trial's continuing ability to divide opinion.
  • Inherit the Wind remains the way many people encounter the story.
08

Why does it matter?

The Scopes Trial is a landmark in the long struggle over freedom of thought, academic freedom and the relationship between religion, science and the state. It also shows how a small town, a young teacher and a group of local businessmen could set off a national debate.

  • It raised enduring questions about who decides what children are taught in public schools.
  • It demonstrated the power of the new mass media, especially radio, to turn a local case into a national event.
  • Disputes over evolution, creationism and "intelligent design" in American schools continued into the twenty-first century.
