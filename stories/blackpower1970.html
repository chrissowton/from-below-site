The Black Power Revolution

Students and workers in Trinidad marched against continuing inequality after independence, demanding Black economic power. A state of emergency followed, and part of the army mutinied.

When
February – April 1970
Where
Woodford Square, Port of Spain, Trinidad and Tobago
Facts checked
2026-11-16

See it on the map →

01

What is the background to this event?

Trinidad and Tobago became independent in 1962, but much of its economy remained in the hands of foreign and white local elites.

  • Inspired by the US civil rights movement, a Black Power movement grew from 1968.1
  • The National Joint Action Committee (NJAC) grew out of the student guild at the University of the West Indies.1
  • Trade unions were also on strike, led by figures such as George Weekes of the oilfield workers.1

03

What happened?

In 1970, a Carnival band presented "The Truth about Africa", and a series of marches and protests followed.1

  • Prime Minister Williams responded with a broadcast declaring "I am for Black Power" and introduced new economic measures, but protests continued.1
  • On 6 April, a protester, Basil Davis, was killed by police, and the movement grew.1
  • On 18 April, sugar workers went on strike, and there was talk of a general strike.1
  • On 21 April, Williams declared a state of emergency and arrested 15 Black Power leaders.1
  • Part of the Defence Force mutinied and took hostages, but surrendered on 25 April.1
04

What happened next?

  • Reforms: Williams reshuffled his cabinet and moved towards greater local ownership of the economy.1
  • Civil liberties: a Public Order Act to restrict protest was withdrawn after public opposition.1
05

Why does it matter?

The Black Power Revolution challenged the idea that independence alone had ended colonial inequality.

  • It linked students, unemployed youth and workers.
  • The prime minister reshuffled his cabinet and moved towards greater local ownership of the economy.1
  • A Public Order Act to restrict protest was withdrawn after public opposition.1

What do others say?How it was seen at the time, and where historians disagree

At the time

The government saw a threat to order; supporters saw a demand for economic justice.1

Debates since

  • Race and class: historians such as Brian Meeks discuss the movement's attempts, not always successful, to unite Afro- and Indo-Trinidadians.2
  • Violence: there were fires and clashes in Port of Spain; police killed a protester, and mutineers took hostages.1

What is it?
Press photograph
Made by, and when
Associated Press, 1970

Riot police march into Woodford Square, Port of Spain — the 'People's Parliament' where Black Power rallies were held.

Reading it

Riot police march into Woodford Square, the 'People's Parliament' where Black Power rallies were held. Sending police into that particular square was a statement in itself. The Associated Press photograph was made for readers abroad, to whom the uprising was news.
