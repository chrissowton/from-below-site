The Bambatha Rebellion

A new poll tax, designed to push Zulu men into working for white employers, provoked a rising led by Chief Bambatha. Colonial forces killed thousands and imprisoned thousands more.

When
February – July 1906
Where
Mome Gorge, Nkandla, KwaZulu-Natal, South Africa
Facts checked
2026-11-16

See it on the map →

01

What is the background to this event?

After the Second Boer War, white employers in the British colony of Natal struggled to recruit Black workers.1

  • In 1905–06, the colony added a poll tax of £1 to the existing hut tax, to push Zulu men into wage labour.1
  • African families were already suffering: tenants were evicted from white-owned land, the reserves were overcrowded, and an epidemic had killed 90% of local cattle.1

03

What happened?

In early 1906, protests broke out at poll-tax collection points. Two police officers were killed on 8 February, and Natal declared martial law.1

  • The government deposed Bambatha as chief.1
  • From 3 April, Bambatha led guerrilla attacks from the Nkandla forest.1
  • On 10 June, colonial troops surrounded his fighters at Mome Gorge and inflicted heavy casualties. Bambatha was reported killed, though his supporters believed he escaped.1
  • Chief Meseni continued the fight in the Thukela Valley until July.1

04

What happened next?

  • Repression: colonial forces carried out indiscriminate operations; 3,000–4,000 Zulus were killed, over 7,000 imprisoned and 4,000 flogged. 36 colonial soldiers died.1
  • Union: the rising increased white support for uniting the colonies, and the Union of South Africa was formed in 1910.1
05

What do others say?

At the time

The colonial government presented it as a dangerous rebellion; many Africans saw a protest against an unjust tax.1

Debates since

  • Reluctant rebellion: historian Shula Marks argued that the rising was largely provoked by colonial policy and panic.2
  • Gandhi: Gandhi, then a lawyer in Natal, encouraged Indians to support the colonial side and led a stretcher-bearer corps; in his autobiography he later wrote of being shocked by the treatment of wounded Zulus.1
  • Violence: rebels killed police and soldiers; colonial forces killed 3,000–4,000 Zulus.1

07

How is it remembered?

  • Bambatha is remembered as a hero of resistance in South Africa; the ANC saw the rising as part of its own pre-history.
08

Why does it matter?

The Bambatha Rebellion was the last armed Zulu resistance to colonial rule.

  • It shows how taxes were used to force Africans into labour for white employers.

Questions to think about

  1. Why did the poll tax provoke a rebellion?
  2. Why was the repression so much deadlier than the rising?
  3. Why did white colonists respond by uniting South Africa?
  4. How did Gandhi's experience of 1906 change his views?

What other events are like this?

  • 1898The Hut Tax War — When the British imposed a tax on every house in the new Sierra Leone Protectorate, the Temne leader Bai Bureh led a guerrilla war against them, while a separate Mende rising killed traders and missionaries. Both were crushed.
  • 1905The Maji Maji War — Forced to grow cotton for German colonial rulers, peoples across southern Tanzania united behind a sacred water medicine and rose in one of Africa's largest anti-colonial wars.
  • 1915The Chilembwe Uprising — A Baptist minister educated in the United States led a short-lived rising against forced labour, racism and the recruitment of Africans to die in Britain's war.
