The Cananea Strike

Mexican copper miners paid less than their American co-workers went on strike. The American owner called in Arizona Rangers across the border, and dozens of miners were killed.

When
1 – 3 June 1906
Where
Cananea, Sonora, Mexico
Facts checked
2026-11-16

See it on the map →

01

What is the background to this event?

Under President Porfirio Díaz, Mexican resources were often sold to foreign companies.1

  • The American William C. Greene owned the Cananea Consolidated Copper Company and much of the town itself — its food supply, banks, water and land.1
  • By 1906, the mine employed about 5,360 Mexicans and 2,200 Americans.1
  • When Greene raised American miners' pay to $5 a day, Mexican miners still earned at most $3.1

03

What happened?

On 1 June 1906, Mexican miners went on strike over pay and conditions.1

  • As they marched past the lumber yard, its American managers turned a fire hose on them and fired shots; the miners set the yard on fire and killed the two managers.1
  • Exaggerated reports reached the United States, and Arizona Rangers crossed into Mexico to help suppress the strike.1
  • Mexican rural police and federal troops also moved in.1
  • About 33 workers were killed, 22 injured and 50 arrested, and the miners returned to work without winning their demands.1

04

What happened next?

  • Symbol: Cananea and the Río Blanco textile strike of 1907 became symbols of the Díaz regime's corruption, subservience to foreigners and repression.1
  • Revolution: the jailed leaders were freed in 1911 after Díaz fell in the Mexican Revolution.1
05

What do others say?

At the time

American newspapers spread false reports of many Americans killed; Mexican opponents of Díaz saw a massacre.1

Debates since

  • A national symbol: historians such as John Mason Hart see Cananea as part of a wider revolt against foreign economic power.2
  • Violence on both sides: the American managers shot at strikers and were killed by them; Rangers, rural police and troops killed about 33 Mexican workers.1

07

How is it remembered?

  • Mexico remembers the Cananea miners as forerunners of the revolution and of labour rights.
08

Why does it matter?

Cananea was a key moment in the build-up to the Mexican Revolution.1

  • It shows the links between foreign ownership, racial pay gaps and labour struggle.

Questions to think about

What other events are like this?

  • 1907The Santa María School Massacre — Thousands of striking nitrate miners and their families were shot down in and around a school in Iquique — one of the worst massacres of workers in Latin American history.
  • 1914The Ludlow Massacre and the Colorado Coalfield War — Striking coal miners and their families were living in a tent colony when National Guard soldiers and company guards attacked. Two women and eleven children died in a pit beneath a burning tent. The armed miners' revenge turned a strike into a war.
  • 1921Rebel Patagonia — Sheep-farm workers in the far south of Argentina went on strike for better conditions. The army was sent in, and hundreds — perhaps 1,500 — were shot, many after they had surrendered.
