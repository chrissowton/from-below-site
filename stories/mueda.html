The Mueda Massacre

Makonde farmers gathered peacefully to ask the Portuguese for better prices and independence. Soldiers opened fire. The massacre convinced many that only armed struggle could end colonial rule.

When
16 June 1960
Where
Mueda, Makonde plateau, northern Mozambique
Facts checked
2026-11-15

See it on the map →

01

What is the background to this event?

Portugal ruled Mozambique for centuries and, under the dictatorship of António Salazar, refused to consider independence for its African colonies, which it called "overseas provinces".

  • Africans in Mozambique were subjected to forced labour and to forced cultivation of cotton for low prices.
  • Many Makonde people from the northern plateau worked as migrant labourers in neighbouring Tanganyika (Tanzania), where they saw a nationalist movement winning independence.
  • Makonde activists organised farmers' cooperatives and nationalist groups, including the Mozambique African National Union (MANU), one of the three organisations that later formed FRELIMO.1

03

What happened?

On 16 June 1960, Makonde people gathered in front of the district headquarters in Mueda to present their demands to the Portuguese authorities, including better prices for their crops and, according to many accounts, independence.12

  • The meeting had been agreed with the administration, according to some sources.2
  • The administrator arrested the leaders. When the crowd protested, troops opened fire.
  • An undetermined number of people were killed.2 Portuguese accounts gave a small number of deaths; nationalists later spoke of hundreds; historians continue to debate the true figure.

The reasons for the shooting are unclear; it may have been intended as a show of force to deter Mozambicans from seeking independence.2

04

What happened next?

  • Armed struggle: FRELIMO was founded in 1962 and began its war of independence in 1964, starting in the Makonde region. Resentment over Mueda helped build support.1
  • Independence: after the Carnation Revolution in Portugal in 1974, Mozambique became independent in 1975.
  • Commemoration: 16 June became a day of commemoration in Mozambique. In 1980, the new national currency, the metical, was launched on that day.2
05

What do others say?

At the time

The Portuguese authorities presented the event as the restoration of order. Nationalists saw it as a massacre of peaceful people.

Historians' debates

  • The death toll: the number of dead is disputed, and estimates vary widely; historians such as Paolo Israel have examined how the memory of the massacre grew in songs and official history.
  • Independence or prices? Some historians argue that the crowd's demands were mainly economic; others that independence was central.
  • The later war: the independence war that followed saw violence by both Portuguese forces, including massacres such as Wiriyamu in 1972, and by FRELIMO against those it saw as collaborators.
06

The evidence

  • Survivors' testimonies, many recorded after independence.
  • Portuguese colonial records.
  • Ruy Guerra's film Mueda, Memória e Massacre (1980), in which local people re-enacted the events.2
07

How is it remembered?

  • A memorial stands at the site in Mueda.
  • The massacre is re-enacted in an annual play in Mueda.
08

Why does it matter?

The Mueda massacre is remembered as the moment when many Mozambicans concluded that peaceful demands would not end Portuguese rule. It became a founding event in the story of the nation.

  • It shows how state violence against peaceful protest can lead to armed struggle.
  • It raises questions about how national memories are shaped.

Questions to think about

  1. Why do you think the Portuguese administrator ordered the shooting?
  2. Why did the massacre convince many people to support armed struggle?
