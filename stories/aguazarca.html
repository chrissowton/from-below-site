Berta Cáceres and the Fight for the Gualcarque River

Lenca communities blockaded the road to a dam on their sacred river for more than a year, forcing the world's largest dam builder to pull out. Their leader, Berta Cáceres, won the Goldman Prize — and was murdered a year later.

When
2006 – 2016
Where
Río Blanco and La Esperanza, Intibucá, Honduras

01

What is the background to this event?

The Lenca are an indigenous people of south-western Honduras. In 1993, Berta Cáceres, then a student, co-founded the Council of Popular and Indigenous Organisations of Honduras (COPINH).1

  • COPINH campaigned against illegal logging, plantation owners and US military bases on Lenca land.1
  • After a military coup in 2009, the Honduran government approved many new mining and dam projects.2
  • The Agua Zarca dam on the Gualcarque River, sacred to the Lenca, was planned by the Honduran company DESA, with the Chinese company Sinohydro and finance from the World Bank's International Finance Corporation.1

In their own words

03

What happened?

In 2006, Lenca people from Río Blanco asked Cáceres to investigate the arrival of construction equipment. She found that the dam was going ahead without their consent.1

  • From 2013, the community blockaded the road to the site, preventing construction for more than a year.1
  • Protesters faced threats, arrests and violence. In July 2013, Tomás García was shot dead by a soldier at a protest.2
  • Sinohydro withdrew from the project, and the International Finance Corporation also pulled out.1
  • In 2015, Cáceres received the Goldman Environmental Prize for her campaign.1
  • On 2 March 2016, gunmen broke into her home in La Esperanza and shot her dead. Gustavo Castro was wounded.1
  • Two more activists were killed in Honduras the same month.1

04

What happened next?

  • Convictions: in 2018, seven men, including a DESA manager and former soldiers, were convicted of her murder.2
  • The planner: in July 2021, Roberto David Castillo was found guilty as a co-conspirator, and was later sentenced to 22 years and six months.1
  • Funders: the Dutch and Finnish development banks FMO and Finnfund suspended and then ended their involvement.1
  • Danger: Honduras remains one of the most dangerous countries for land and environmental defenders.3
05

What do others say?

At the time

DESA argued that the dam would provide clean energy and that it had consulted local people. COPINH said consultation had been manipulated and opponents threatened.2

Debates since

  • Responsibility: a group of international legal experts found in 2017 that DESA's executives, security firms and state officials had worked to neutralise opposition, and criticised the banks for "willful negligence".1
  • Full justice? Cáceres's family and COPINH argue that others who ordered or funded the murder have not been tried.2
  • Violence: the movement was non-violent; violence came from soldiers, private security and hired gunmen.12

07

How is it remembered?

  • Murals and banners with Berta Cáceres's image appear across Latin America and Europe.
  • COPINH continues its work, and her daughters have taken leading roles.
08

Why does it matter?

The struggle shows the risks faced by indigenous and environmental defenders — and the responsibility of companies and international lenders.

  • A small community stopped a major international project.
  • The murder of Cáceres drew worldwide attention to the killing of land defenders.
