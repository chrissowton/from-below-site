The Railway Protection Movement

When the Qing government seized Sichuan's railway and pledged it to foreign banks, the province rose. Rallies, strikes and a tax boycott were met with gunfire in Chengdu — and the troops sent to crush the revolt left the way open for the revolution that ended 2,000 years of imperial rule.

When

Where
Chengdu, Sichuan, China

01

What is the background to this event?

From the 1890s, most of China's railways were built and run by foreign powers.1

  • In 1905, Sichuan set up its own railway company to build a line from Chengdu to Wuhan.1
  • It raised money by selling shares and by a special 3% tax on harvests, so much of Sichuan's gentry and merchant class became shareholders.1
  • The company was badly run, and only about 10 miles of track had been laid by 1911.1
  • In May 1911, the Qing government nationalised the local railways and pledged them to a group of British, German, French and American banks in return for a loan.1

In their own words

03

What happened?

On 17 June 1911, Pu Dianjun and others formed the Railway Protection League.1

  • On 11–13 August, more than 10,000 people rallied in Chengdu, and students and merchants organised strikes and boycotts.1
  • On 1 September, the railway company's shareholders called on the people of Sichuan to stop paying grain taxes.1
  • On 7 September, Zhao Erfeng arrested Pu and other leaders. When protesters marched on his office, his troops opened fire; 32 people were killed in Chengdu.1
  • With the city gates closed, protesters carved messages on oiled boards and floated them down the river to spread the news.1
  • Armed groups, mainly Gelaohui members, rose across Sichuan, and at one point numbered over 100,000.1
04

What happened next?

  • The revolution: the government moved troops from Hubei to Sichuan. This weakened its defences in Wuhan, and the Wuchang Uprising, which began on 10 October 1911, succeeded.1
  • Sichuan independent: Pu Dianjun was released, and on 27 November he declared Sichuan's independence from the Qing.1
  • The end of the empire: the revolution that followed overthrew the Qing dynasty and set up the Republic of China in 1912.1
  • No railway: the Sichuan–Hankou railway was not built for decades.1
05

What do others say?

At the time

The government saw the protests as rebellion; protesters called Zhao Erfeng 'Butcher Zhao'.1

Debates since

  • Whose movement? It began among gentry and merchant shareholders protecting their investment, but grew into a mass rising. Historians debate how far it was about money and how far about nationalism and revolution.1
  • Violence on both sides: government troops killed 32 protesters in Chengdu, and more than a thousand Gelaohui fighters when they attacked the city. In the fighting that followed, a Qing official, Duanfang, was killed by mutinous troops, and Zhao Erfeng was executed by the revolutionaries in December 1911.1

How is it remembered?

  • The Monument to the Martyrs of the Railway Protection Movement stands in the People's Park in Chengdu.
08

Why does it matter?

A dispute over a railway turned into a mass movement against foreign control and imperial rule. The movement in Sichuan helped make the 1911 Revolution possible.

Open questions

What other events are like this?

  • 1919The May Fourth Movement — When the peace conference at Versailles handed German territory in China to Japan, Beijing students took to the streets. Workers and merchants joined them in strikes, and China refused to sign the treaty.
  • 1851The Taiping Heavenly Kingdom — A failed examination candidate who believed he was the younger brother of Jesus led millions of poor Chinese against the Qing dynasty. His Heavenly Kingdom promised equality — and the war that followed was the deadliest civil war in history.
  • 1905Iran's Constitutional Revolution — Merchants, clerics, craftsmen and radicals forced the Shah to grant Iran a constitution and an elected parliament — the first in the Middle East.
