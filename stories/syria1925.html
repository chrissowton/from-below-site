The Great Syrian Revolt

Druze villagers, Damascus craftsmen and peasants from across Syria rose against French rule. France answered by shelling Damascus.

When
1925 – 1927
Where
Jabal al-Druze, Damascus and across Syria

01

What is the background to this event?

After the First World War, Britain and France divided the Arab lands of the Ottoman Empire. In 1920, French forces defeated the short-lived Arab Kingdom of Syria at the Battle of Maysalun, and the League of Nations gave France a "mandate" over Syria and Lebanon.1

  • French rule was unwelcome to most Syrians, except some Christian communities, especially the Maronites of Mount Lebanon.1
  • France divided Syria into separate states — Damascus, Aleppo, Greater Lebanon, an Alawite state and a Druze state — to weaken nationalism.1
  • In the Druze state in the south, a French governor collected taxes in full, disarmed the population and used forced labour, angering many.1
02

Who were the key people?

  • Sultan al-Atrash

    A Druze leader who launched the revolt and became its commander. He was sentenced to death in absence but pardoned in 1937.

  • Abd al-Rahman Shahbandar

    A Damascus doctor and nationalist leader of the People's Party.

  • Fawzi al-Qawuqji

    An officer who led an uprising in Hama in October 1925.

  • Hasan al-Kharrat

    A neighbourhood leader in Damascus who led rebel bands in the city and the nearby Ghouta orchards.

In their own words

03

What happened?

In the summer of 1925, the Druze rose against the French and won a series of victories, destroying a French relief column.1 On 23 August 1925, Sultan al-Atrash declared a revolution against France, and fighting soon spread to Damascus, Homs and Hama.1

  • Sunni, Druze, Shia and other Syrians joined the revolt, with the common goal of ending French rule.1
  • In October 1925, rebels entered Damascus. The French responded by shelling the city with artillery and bombing it from the air for two days. Large parts of the old city were destroyed and, by many accounts, around 1,400 people were killed.
  • Hama rose for two days in October 1925, with wide local support.1
  • The French used colonial troops and local irregulars, some of whom looted villages. Some rebel bands also attacked Christian villages they accused of collaborating with the French.

By spring 1926, much of Damascus had been destroyed by artillery fire and the nationalist leadership forced into exile.1 The Druze were defeated in 1927, and Sultan al-Atrash fled to Transjordan.1

04

What happened next?

  • Political concessions: although defeated militarily, the revolt pushed France to allow a national government and elections; a treaty promising independence was agreed in 1936 but never ratified by France.1
  • Independence: Syria became fully independent in 1946.
  • Return: Sultan al-Atrash returned to Syria in 1937 and became a national hero.1
05

What do others say?

At the time

The French portrayed the rebels as bandits and fanatics. Syrian nationalists saw the revolt as a national struggle for independence.

Historians' debates

  • From below: Michael Provence argues that the revolt was led by rural people and former Ottoman soldiers, not just urban notables.
  • National or sectarian? Historians debate how far the revolt united Syrians across religious lines; some Christian and minority communities feared or suffered from rebel attacks, while others supported the revolt.
  • Colonial violence: Daniel Neep shows how France used aerial bombing and the destruction of urban spaces to control the population.

07

How is it remembered?

  • Sultan al-Atrash is commemorated by monuments in Syria and in the Druze villages of the Golan Heights.
  • The revolt is celebrated in Syria as a founding moment of the nation.
08

Why does it matter?

The Great Syrian Revolt was the largest anti-colonial uprising in the Arab world between the wars. It brought together people of different religions and classes against foreign rule.

  • It shows how colonial powers used "divide and rule" — and how people resisted it.
  • The bombardment of Damascus caused international outrage and questions at the League of Nations.

Questions to think about
