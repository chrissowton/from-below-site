The Merthyr Rising and the Red Flag

Ironworkers and miners in Merthyr Tydfil took control of the town for days, beating back soldiers under a flag dyed in blood. The rising is said to be the first time the red flag was flown as a symbol of workers' revolt in Britain.

When

Where

01

What is the background to this event?

Merthyr Tydfil was an industrial town with a large working-class population, dominated by ironmasters such as William Crawshay.1

  • In 1831, workers faced wage cuts and unemployment, and the debtors' court seized the goods of those who could not pay.1
  • Throughout May 1831, coal miners and others who worked for the ironmaster William Crawshay took to the streets, calling for reform and protesting against lower wages.1

In their own words

03

What happened?

The protesters stormed the town and sacked the debtors' court, destroying its account books. Crowds shouted for 'cheese and bread' and 'down with the king'.1

  • On 2 June, a crowd led by Lewis Lewis marched to the Castle Inn, where employers and magistrates were meeting, to demand cheaper bread and higher wages. The demands were rejected and the crowd attacked the inn.1
  • The soldiers opened fire. After a long struggle in which hundreds were injured, some fatally, the troops withdrew and left the town to the protesters.1
  • Some 7,000 to 10,000 workers marched under a red flag. For several days they controlled Merthyr: they set up roadblocks, ambushed and disarmed soldiers and yeomanry, and sent messengers to start strikes in nearby valleys.1
  • On 6 June, 450 troops marched on a mass meeting with weapons levelled, and the meeting dispersed. By 7 June the authorities were back in control.1
04

What happened next?

  • Trials: 26 people were put on trial. Some were imprisoned, others transported to Australia, and two were sentenced to death.1
  • Dic Penderyn: although 11,000 people signed a petition for his release, Richard Lewis was hanged in Cardiff on 13 August 1831. Years later, another man reportedly confessed on his deathbed to the stabbing, and a witness said he had lied at the trial.1
  • A pardon? In 2015, the Welsh MP Ann Clwyd presented a petition to Parliament for Dic Penderyn to be pardoned.1
05

What do others say?

At the time

The government treated the rising as a dangerous riot to be crushed and punished; the people of Merthyr saw a fight for bread, wages and reform.1

Debates since

  • Violence: up to 24 protesters were killed and hundreds injured; soldiers were also wounded.1
  • Justice: whether Dic Penderyn was guilty is still disputed.1
06

The evidence

  • Trial records, petitions, newspaper reports and later testimony.1
07

How is it remembered?

  • A plaque at Cardiff Market marks where Dic Penderyn was hanged. Songs by Meic Stevens and others remember him, and a Merthyr Rising festival has been held since 2013.1
08

Why does it matter?

For a few days in 1831, workers in Merthyr governed their own town. The rising and the hanging of Dic Penderyn became founding stories of the Welsh labour movement.1

09

What other events are like this?

  • 1839The Chartists and the Newport Rising — Millions of working people signed the People's Charter demanding the vote. In Newport, thousands of miners and ironworkers marched — and soldiers opened fire.
  • 1839The Rebecca Riots — At night, Welsh farmers with blackened faces and women's clothes smashed the toll-gates that taxed every journey to market. They called themselves 'Rebecca and her daughters'.
  • 1834The Tolpuddle Martyrs — Six farm labourers formed a society to resist wage cuts and were sentenced to transportation to Australia.
