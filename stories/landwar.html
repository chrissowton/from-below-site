The Irish Land War and the First Boycott

Irish tenant farmers organised against evictions and high rents — and gave the world a new word, 'boycott'.

When
1879 – 1882
Where
County Mayo and across rural Ireland
Facts checked
2026-11-16

01

What is the background to this event?

In the nineteenth century most Irish land was owned by a relatively small number of landlords, many of them Protestant and some living in England, while the great majority of the rural population were Catholic tenant farmers or labourers. Tenants had little security: they could be evicted, and any improvements they made to their holdings could lead to higher rents.

Memories of the Great Famine of 1845–52, in which around a million people died and a million more emigrated, were still raw. In 1879 a new crisis struck:

  • Heavy rains, poor harvests and falling prices brought hunger and distress, especially in the west of Ireland.
  • Many tenants could not pay their rents and faced eviction.
  • Nationalist leaders saw an opportunity to link the land question to the cause of Irish self-government.

Tenant campaigners demanded the "Three Fs": fair rent, fixity of tenure and free sale of a tenant's interest in a holding.

02

Who were the key people?

  • Michael Davitt

    The son of evicted tenants from County Mayo, who lost an arm working in a Lancashire mill as a child and was imprisoned as a Fenian. He was the main founder of the Land League.

  • Charles Stewart Parnell

    A Protestant landowner and leader of the Irish Parliamentary Party, who became president of the Land League.

  • Anna and Fanny Parnell

    Charles Parnell's sisters, who founded and led the Ladies' Land League, which ran the campaign when the male leaders were imprisoned.

  • Captain Charles Boycott

    A land agent for Lord Erne near Lough Mask in County Mayo, who became the first target of the tactic that bears his name.

  • The tenants of Mayo

    Farmers, labourers and their families who refused to pay unjust rents, resisted evictions and ostracised those who took evicted tenants' land.

In their own words

03

What happened?

On 20 April 1879, a mass meeting at Irishtown, County Mayo, launched the agitation. On 8 June Parnell spoke to around 8,000 people at Westport, and in August the Land League of Mayo was founded at Castlebar.2 In October 1879 the Irish National Land League was established in Dublin, with Parnell as president.

In September 1880, Parnell set out the tactic that would become the movement's most powerful weapon. Speaking at Ennis, he urged that anyone who took a farm from which another had been evicted should be shunned:

"You must shun him on the roadside when you meet him, you must shun him in the streets of the town, you must shun him in the shop … by isolating him from the rest of his country as if he were the leper of old."Charles Stewart Parnell, speech at Ennis, 19 September 18803

Soon afterwards the tactic was applied to Captain Charles Boycott, a land agent near Lough Mask who had tried to serve eviction notices on tenants:4

  • His farm workers and servants left, shopkeepers refused to serve him, and the local postman stopped delivering his letters.
  • When Boycott wrote to The Times, his plight became a cause célèbre in Britain.
  • Fifty Orangemen from Cavan and Monaghan were brought to harvest his crops, protected by a regiment of soldiers and more than 1,000 members of the Royal Irish Constabulary.
  • The operation cost far more than the crops were worth. Boycott left Ireland on 1 December 1880, and his name entered the English language.

The Land League also resisted evictions, organised rent strikes, supported evicted families and set up its own courts. Its rules forbade members to take part in evictions or to deal in any way with boycotted people.2

04

What happened next?

  • Reform: Gladstone's Land Act of 1881 largely conceded the "Three Fs" and established a Land Commission to set fair rents.
  • Coercion: the government also passed coercion laws. Parnell and other leaders were imprisoned in Kilmainham Gaol in October 1881, and the Land League was suppressed; the Ladies' Land League continued the campaign until it was dissolved in 1882.
  • Land purchase: later Land Acts, especially the Wyndham Act of 1903, financed tenants to buy their land. By the early twentieth century, Ireland had been transformed from a land of tenants into a land of owner-occupiers.
  • Home Rule: the Land War helped create a mass nationalist movement that pressed for Irish self-government.
05

Why does it matter?

The Land War was one of the most successful agrarian movements in European history. Through collective organisation, Irish tenants forced a fundamental transfer of land ownership. It also gave the world the boycott — a non-violent method of social and economic pressure used by countless later movements.

  • It showed how communities could use solidarity and ostracism to resist economic power.
  • The Ladies' Land League demonstrated women's capacity to lead a mass political movement.
  • The word "boycott" now appears in dozens of languages.

What do others say?How it was seen at the time, and where historians disagree

At the time

Landlords and much of the British press presented the League as a criminal conspiracy that encouraged intimidation, cattle-maiming and murder. Some violence did occur — so-called "agrarian outrages" — and the government used it to justify coercion. The Catholic hierarchy was divided, with some bishops initially denouncing the movement.

Historians' debates

  • Who benefited? Historians have argued that the larger tenant farmers and graziers gained most from the reforms, while the poorest tenants and landless labourers gained little.
  • Boycotting: it was non-violent in principle, but a government inspector described it as a form of imprisonment for the victim, isolating them completely from the community — raising questions about its coercive character.
  • The Ladies' Land League: Anna Parnell later accused male leaders of using the women's movement and then abandoning it; historians have reassessed its importance.
How do we know?The evidence, and a source from the time
  • Newspapers, including nationalist papers such as United Ireland and the British press.
  • Police reports and official returns of evictions and "outrages".
  • Photographs of evictions, many taken by Robert French for the Lawrence collection and held by the National Library of Ireland.
  • Memoirs, especially Michael Davitt's The Fall of Feudalism in Ireland (1904) and Anna Parnell's Tale of a Great Sham.

Issued by Land League leaders from prison, the No Rent Manifesto called on tenants to pay no rent at all. Its reprinting in New York shows how much the movement relied on Irish-American support.

Reading it

The No Rent Manifesto, issued by Land League leaders from prison, called on tenants to pay no rent at all. Its reprinting in New York shows how much the movement relied on Irish-American support and money. Not every tenant obeyed: refusing all rent risked eviction, and leaders in prison did not share that risk.

How is it remembered?Memorials, anniversaries and arguments over memory
  • The Michael Davitt Museum stands at Straide, County Mayo, where he was born.
  • Elizabeth Butler's painting Evicted (1890) became an enduring image of the period.
  • The word "boycott" is perhaps the Land War's most lasting legacy.
