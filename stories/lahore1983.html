The Women's March in Lahore

When Pakistan's military ruler proposed that a woman's testimony be worth half a man's, women lawyers and activists marched in defiance of martial law. They were tear-gassed and beaten — the first public protest against the dictatorship.

When
12 February 1983
Where
Mall Road, Lahore, Pakistan
Facts checked
2026-11-16

01

What is the background to this event?

In 1977, General Muhammad Zia-ul-Haq seized power in Pakistan and imposed martial law. He launched a programme of "Islamisation" of the law.

  • The Hudood Ordinances of 1979 introduced harsh punishments and made it very difficult for women to prove rape; women who reported rape could themselves be accused of adultery.1
  • In 1981, women founded the Women's Action Forum (WAF) to resist these laws.
  • In 1983, the government proposed a new Law of Evidence that would reduce the value of a woman's testimony to half that of a man.1
  • Public gatherings were banned under martial law.1

In their own words

03

What happened?

On 12 February 1983, women gathered on Mall Road in Lahore to march to the High Court to protest against the Law of Evidence and the Hudood Ordinances.1

  • The police cordoned off the road. The poet Habib Jalib began reciting his poetry, and one protester slipped through the cordon and signalled others to follow.1
  • The police fired tear gas and charged the marchers with batons. Many women were injured, and about 50 were arrested.1
  • It was the first public demonstration by any group in defiance of General Zia's martial law.1

Religious scholars declared the women's protest un-Islamic, and some even claimed that it annulled the marchers' marriages.1 Women held solidarity protests in Karachi.1

04

What happened next?

  • The law delayed: continued protests delayed the law for almost two years. The version finally adopted in 1984 made the testimony of two women equal to that of one man in financial matters.1
  • Continuing protest: women marched along the same route every 12 February.1
  • Reform: in 2006, the Women's Protection Act reformed some of the rape provisions of the Hudood laws.
  • National Women's Day: since 2012, 12 February has been Pakistan's official National Women's Day.1
05

What do others say?

At the time

The government and clergy presented the laws as Islamic. The women argued that they were discriminatory and that the clergy's interpretation was not the only one.1

Debates since

  • Religion and rights: Pakistani feminists have debated whether to argue within Islamic frameworks or on secular grounds.
  • Class: critics noted that WAF was led largely by educated, middle-class women; its members argued that the laws affected poor women most of all.
  • The Aurat March: since 2018, the annual Aurat (Women's) March has continued the tradition, facing fierce opposition.
06

The evidence

  • Photographs and press reports of the march.

07

How is it remembered?

  • 12 February is National Women's Day in Pakistan.1
  • Asma Jahangir, who died in 2018, is remembered as one of the country's greatest human rights defenders.
08

Why does it matter?

The Lahore march was a turning point for the women's movement in Pakistan and for resistance to military rule. Women were the first to defy the dictatorship in public.

  • It shows how laws about evidence and testimony can be used to make women unequal.
  • It shows how women activists contested religious arguments on their own terms.

Open questions
