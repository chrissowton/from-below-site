The Revolt of the Ciompi

The wool workers of Florence, excluded from the guilds that ran the city, seized the government — and for a few weeks a wool-carder sat at the head of the republic.

When
June – August 1378
Where
Florence, Italy
Facts checked
2026-11-16

01

What is the background to this event?

In the fourteenth century, Florence was one of the richest cities in Europe, and its wealth was built on the manufacture of woollen cloth. The city was a republic, governed by a council called the Signoria, but political power belonged to the guilds (arti):1

  • The seven major guilds, including the powerful wool guild (the Arte della Lana), represented merchants, bankers and manufacturers. The fourteen minor guilds represented craftsmen and shopkeepers.
  • Thousands of wage workers in the cloth industry — washers, beaters and wool-carders — belonged to no guild, could not hold office, and were subject to the discipline of the wool guild's own court. Of around 14,000 people working in wool manufacture, only about 200 were eligible for guild membership.1
  • These workers were known as the ciompi, a word probably connected with the carding of wool. They were burdened by forced loans, high taxes and debt; in 1355, more than a fifth of Florentine households were classed as having almost no property at all.1

In the 1370s, an expensive war with the Pope, high taxation and divisions among the ruling families increased tensions in the city.

In their own words

03

What happened?

In June 1378, the fourteen minor guilds demanded a greater share of public offices. The leading families, feeling threatened, quadrupled the fee for admission to office-holding, which turned many of the unguilded workers against them as well.1 On 22 June, the Ciompi took up arms for the first time, and the houses of several leading citizens were burned.1

The decisive rising came in July:1

  • On 21 July, thousands of armed wool workers and other unguilded labourers besieged the Signoria in the Palazzo Vecchio. They hanged the public executioner by his feet in front of the palace.
  • They forced the government to create three new guilds — for dyers, for shirtmakers and tailors, and for the Ciompi themselves — giving them the right to hold office. The new guilds had some 13,000 members, compared with 4,000–5,000 in the old twenty-one guilds together; almost every man in Florence could now take part in government.
  • Michele di Lando, a wool-carder, entered the palace barefoot and was made Gonfaloniere of Justice, the head of the government.

The Ciompi's demands were not especially radical: they wanted the same rights as other guildsmen, protection from the wool guild's court, and more work through increased cloth production.1

Within weeks, the alliance broke apart. A radical committee, the "Eight Saints of the People of God", set itself up as a shadow government and demanded further changes. On 31 August, Michele di Lando rode out of the palace with the banner of justice; the forces of the major and minor guilds, led by the butchers, attacked the Ciompi militia in the Piazza della Signoria, and the Ciompi were massacred. On 1 September an assembly dissolved the Ciompi guild.1

04

What happened next?

  • A guild government: the two remaining new guilds, of dyers and shirtmakers, kept their place in government, and a regime dominated by the minor guilds ruled Florence for three and a half years.
  • Reaction: in January 1382 the old elite returned to power. The two new guilds were abolished, their reforms were repealed, and Michele di Lando was exiled.
  • Oligarchy and the Medici: Florence was ruled by an oligarchy of leading families until the Medici took control in 1434. Organised labour unrest in the Florentine cloth industry declined.
05

What do others say?

At the time

Chroniclers from the ruling families described the Ciompi as a dangerous rabble. A century and a half later, Niccolò Machiavelli wrote a more sympathetic account in his Florentine Histories, in which he put a dramatic speech into the mouth of an unnamed leader of the plebeians: "Strip us all naked, and you will see that we are all alike."2

Historians' debates

  • The first proletarian revolution? Marxist historians, beginning with Niccolò Rodolico in the 1890s, saw the Ciompi as an early proletariat fighting the bourgeoisie. Others argue that their aims — guild membership and political participation — belonged firmly to the medieval world.
  • Who were the rebels? Records of convicted rebels show that, as well as wool workers, they included tavern-keepers and other artisans.
  • Michele di Lando — hero or traitor? Some historians see him as a moderate who saved the revolution's gains; others, as a man who betrayed his own class.
06

The evidence

  • Contemporary chronicles and diaries by Florentine citizens, and the records of the Florentine government.
  • Court records of the trials of rebels, which reveal their names and occupations.
  • Petitions and lists of demands presented by the Ciompi to the Signoria.
07

How is it remembered?

  • In 1895, a statue of Michele di Lando by Antonio Bortone was placed in the Loggia del Mercato Nuovo in Florence.
  • The revolt was a popular subject for nineteenth-century Italian painters and for the labour movement.
  • It appears regularly in histories of the working class as one of its earliest episodes.
08

Why does it matter?

The revolt of the Ciompi was one of the most remarkable workers' movements of medieval Europe. Wage labourers with no political rights organised, seized power in one of Europe's richest cities, and briefly created a government in which almost every man had a voice.

  • It is often seen as one of the first struggles of an industrial working class, centuries before the Industrial Revolution.
  • It shows how the right to organise — here, the right to form a guild — was at the heart of workers' demands.
  • Its rapid defeat shows how fragile alliances between different groups of workers could be.

  • 1888The Matchwomen's Strike — About 1,400 women and girls walked out of a match factory, won their demands in three weeks, and founded the largest union of women in Britain.
