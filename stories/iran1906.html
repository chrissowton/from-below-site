The vote & democracy

Iran's Constitutional Revolution

Merchants, clerics, craftsmen and radicals forced the Shah to grant Iran a constitution and an elected parliament — the first in the Middle East.

When
1905 – 1911
Where
Tehran and Tabriz, Iran

See it on the map →

01

What is the background to this event?

At the beginning of the twentieth century, Iran (then usually called Persia) was ruled by the Qajar dynasty. The shahs were absolute monarchs, but their state was weak, and the country was squeezed between two empires: Russia to the north and Britain to the south.1

  • To pay for the court's spending — including royal tours of Europe — the Qajars borrowed heavily from Russia and Britain and sold concessions over Iran's resources to foreign companies.1
  • In 1891–92 a nationwide boycott of tobacco, backed by a religious ruling, had forced the Shah to cancel a monopoly granted to a British company — showing that it was possible to win against the Shah and foreign interests.1
  • Intellectuals criticised Iran's autocratic rulers, corrupt officials and arbitrary courts, and the low status of women. Merchants (the bazaaris) resented foreign competition and arbitrary taxes, and many of the clergy (ulama) opposed foreign influence.1
  • Japan's victory over Russia in 1905 — the only Asian power with a constitution defeating the only great Western power without one — and the Russian Revolution of 1905 inspired calls for a constitution.1
02

Who were the key people?

  • Sayyid Abdullah Behbahani and Sayyid Muhammad Tabatabai

    Two leading clerics in Tehran who supported the constitutional movement.

  • Mozaffar ad-Din Shah

    The Shah who agreed to an elected parliament and signed the constitution in December 1906, shortly before his death.

  • Mohammad Ali Shah

    His son and successor, who opposed the constitution and had the parliament bombarded in 1908.

  • Sattar Khan and Baqer Khan

    Popular leaders of the defence of Tabriz in 1908–09, honoured as national heroes.

  • The anjomans

    Popular councils and societies formed in towns across Iran, including women's societies that campaigned for education and supported the parliament.

03

What happened?

In December 1905, the governor of Tehran had two merchants beaten on the soles of their feet for allegedly overcharging for sugar. In protest, the merchants closed the bazaar, and clerics and merchants took sanctuary (bast) at a shrine outside the city. In January 1906 the Shah gave in and promised to create a "house of justice".1

When the promise was not kept, protests resumed, and government troops killed a sayyid (a descendant of the Prophet) during clashes.1 In the summer of 1906, the movement grew into a mass protest:1

  • Many clerics left Tehran in protest for the holy city of Qom.
  • About 12,000 to 14,000 merchants, craftsmen, guild members and students took sanctuary in the grounds of the British legation in Tehran. They set up tents, held debates and lectures, and demanded a parliament.
  • The bazaars remained closed, and the life of the capital ground to a halt.

On 5 August 1906, the Shah agreed to the creation of an elected National Consultative Assembly (the Majlis). It first met in October 1906, and the constitution was signed by the dying Shah on 30 December 1906.1 It limited the Shah's power and gave the Majlis control over loans, concessions and the budget — although voting was restricted to men of property, and women were excluded.1

The new Shah, Mohammad Ali Shah, was determined to restore his authority. In June 1908, with Russian support, the Persian Cossack Brigade bombarded the Majlis and closed it. Many constitutionalists were arrested or executed.1 But the city of Tabriz, led by Sattar Khan and Baqer Khan, held out against a royalist siege for months, and by April 1909 had driven out the Shah's forces. Constitutionalist forces from the north and the Bakhtiyari tribes of the south then marched on Tehran, captured it in July 1909, and forced Mohammad Ali Shah to abdicate in favour of his young son, Ahmad Shah. The constitution was restored.1

04

What happened next?

  • Foreign intervention: in 1907 Britain and Russia had already divided Iran into spheres of influence. In 1911, when the Majlis employed the American financial expert Morgan Shuster to reform the treasury, Russia issued an ultimatum demanding his dismissal and occupied Tabriz.
  • The end of the revolution: in December 1911, with the support of 12,000 Russian troops, the Shah's ministers expelled the deputies of the Second Majlis. The parliament did not meet again until 1914.
  • A lasting constitution: the 1906 constitution remained formally in force — although often ignored by the Pahlavi shahs — until the revolution of 1979.
05

What do others say?

At the time

Royalists and some clerics, such as Sheikh Fazlollah Nouri, argued that a Western-style constitution was contrary to Islamic law and called instead for a parliament bound by religion. Constitutionalists argued that limiting the Shah's power was compatible with Islam and necessary for the nation's survival.

Historians' debates

  • Whose revolution? Older histories focused on elite politicians and clerics. Janet Afary emphasises the role of the anjomans, radical social democrats, workers and women — "grassroots democracy".
  • The role of Britain: the British legation sheltered the protesters in 1906, but Britain later agreed with Russia to divide Iran. Historians debate British motives.
  • Success or failure? The revolution was defeated in 1911, but many historians argue that it permanently changed Iranian political culture and expectations.
06

The evidence

  • Newspapers and pamphlets, which flourished during the revolution, and the records of the Majlis.
  • The memoirs of participants, and the diplomatic records of Britain and Russia.
  • Photographs of the Majlis, the anjomans and the defenders of Tabriz.
  • Morgan Shuster's account, The Strangling of Persia (1912).
07

How is it remembered?

  • The Constitution House in Tabriz, where the constitutionalists met, is now a museum.
  • Sattar Khan and Baqer Khan are honoured as national heroes, and streets and squares across Iran bear their names.
  • The American teacher Howard Baskerville, killed fighting for the constitutionalists in Tabriz in 1909, is remembered in Iran.
08

Why does it matter?

The Constitutional Revolution created the first elected parliament and written constitution in the Middle East, and made the idea that a ruler's power should be limited by law and by the representatives of the people part of Iranian political life.

  • It brought together merchants, clerics, craftsmen, intellectuals and radicals in a broad popular movement.
  • It saw the beginnings of organised women's activism in Iran, including campaigns for girls' schools.
  • Its defeat by foreign intervention shaped Iranian suspicion of foreign powers for the rest of the century.

Questions to think about

  1. Why did such different groups — merchants, clerics, radicals — support the demand for a constitution?
  2. Why do you think the protesters took sanctuary in the British legation?
  3. Was the revolution defeated by its internal divisions, or by foreign intervention?
  4. Why were women excluded from voting, and how did they take part nonetheless?

What other events are like this?

  • 1919Egypt's 1919 Revolution — When Britain exiled the nationalist leader Saad Zaghlul, Egyptians of every class and religion rose up in strikes and demonstrations — and women marched in public for the first time.
  • 1647The Putney Debates — Ordinary soldiers and their elected representatives argued with Cromwell and the army's generals about who should have the right to vote.
  • 2010The Tunisian Revolution — A street vendor's desperate protest in a small town sparked a month of uprising that toppled a dictator of 23 years and began the Arab Spring.
