Civil rights & anti-racism

The Morant Bay Rebellion

Thirty years after slavery ended, poor Black Jamaicans marched on a courthouse demanding justice and land. The governor's response — hundreds killed, flogged and hanged — scandalised Britain.

When
11 October 1865
Where
Morant Bay, St Thomas, Jamaica
Facts checked

01

What is the background to this event?

Slavery in Jamaica ended in 1838. But freedom did not bring land or justice for most of the island's Black majority:

  • The best land remained in the hands of the planters. Many freed people struggled to rent or buy land of their own, and wages on the plantations were low and irregular.
  • Only a small minority of men could vote, because of high property qualifications, and the courts and the House of Assembly were dominated by the white elite.
  • In 1864 floods ruined many crops, and the island had recently suffered epidemics of cholera and smallpox and a two-year drought. Food prices rose sharply.1

In 1865 Edward Underhill, Secretary of the Baptist Missionary Society, wrote to the Colonial Office describing the poverty of Jamaica's people. Across the island, people met in "Underhill meetings" to discuss their grievances. When peasants in St Ann petitioned Queen Victoria for Crown land to cultivate, the reply — "the Queen's Advice" — told them to work harder for wages on the plantations. Many believed that Governor Edward Eyre had shaped it.1

George William Gordon, a mixed-race businessman and member of the House of Assembly, was a fierce critic of Eyre. He warned that "if we are to be governed by such a Governor much longer, the people will have to fly to arms and become self-governing."1

In their own words

03

What happened?

In August 1865, Paul Bogle led a deputation of peasants from St Thomas-in-the-East on an 87-kilometre march to the capital, Spanish Town, to see the governor. Eyre refused to receive them.1

On 7 October 1865, a Black man was tried at the courthouse in Morant Bay for trespassing on a long-abandoned plantation. Angry at this further example of injustice over land, Bogle's followers protested, and a fight broke out with the police. Warrants were issued for the arrest of Bogle and others.1

On 11 October, Bogle led several hundred people to the courthouse at Morant Bay. They were met by local officials and a small, inexperienced volunteer militia, which opened fire, killing seven people. The crowd attacked; they set fire to the courthouse and nearby buildings, and killed eighteen people, including the custos, Baron von Ketelhodt, and members of the militia. Over the following days, the unrest spread through the parish, and several more white residents were killed.1

Governor Eyre declared martial law over the county of Surrey and sent troops and Maroon allies to hunt down the rebels. They met no organised resistance, but killed indiscriminately:1

  • 439 Black Jamaicans were killed directly by the soldiers.
  • 354 more were arrested and later executed, many without proper trials. Paul Bogle was hanged with fourteen others, including his brother.
  • More than 600 men and women, including pregnant women, were flogged, and soldiers burned thousands of homes.

Eyre believed that George William Gordon was behind the revolt. Although Gordon was arrested in Kingston, where martial law did not apply, Eyre had him moved to Morant Bay, where he was tried by a court martial and hanged on 23 October, two days after his trial began.1

04

What happened next?

  • Inquiry: a Royal Commission found that the punishments had been excessive and the burning of homes "wanton and cruel". Eyre was recalled.
  • The Eyre controversy: in Britain, the Jamaica Committee, led by John Stuart Mill, Charles Darwin and others, tried to have Eyre prosecuted for murder; the Eyre Defence Committee, supported by Thomas Carlyle, John Ruskin, Alfred Tennyson and Charles Dickens, defended him. Grand juries refused to indict him.
  • Loss of self-government: the Jamaican House of Assembly, which had existed for two centuries, was persuaded to abolish itself, and Jamaica became a Crown colony ruled directly from London.
  • Recognition: in 1969, seven years after independence, Paul Bogle and George William Gordon were named National Heroes of Jamaica.
05

What do others say?

At the time

Governor Eyre and his supporters claimed that the island had been on the verge of a general rising like that in Haiti, and that only firm action had saved the white population. His critics argued that there had been no general conspiracy, and that the repression was out of all proportion to the threat.

Historians' debates

  • Riot or rebellion? Gad Heuman and others argue that Bogle's movement was an organised protest with clear goals — justice in the courts and access to land — rather than a riot, and that some participants sought to overthrow white rule in the parish.
  • Gordon's role: most historians agree that Gordon had no direct part in planning the events at Morant Bay, and that his execution was a political killing.
  • Race and empire: Catherine Hall and others have shown how the Eyre controversy helped shape a harsher, more openly racial view of empire in Britain.

07

How is it remembered?

  • Edna Manley's statue of Paul Bogle stands outside the rebuilt courthouse at Morant Bay.
  • Bogle and Gordon are National Heroes of Jamaica, and Bogle appears on Jamaican coins and banknotes.
  • The rebellion is remembered every October in St Thomas, and features in Jamaican literature, music and school history.
08

Why does it matter?

The Morant Bay Rebellion and its brutal suppression were a turning point in the history of Jamaica and of the British Empire. They exposed how little emancipation had changed for most formerly enslaved people, and the willingness of colonial authorities to use terror to keep order.

  • It showed that the struggle for land and justice continued long after slavery ended.
  • The debate over Governor Eyre divided British public opinion and revealed hardening racial attitudes in Victorian Britain.
  • Bogle and Gordon are now honoured as founders of the Jamaican nation.

What other events are like this?

  • 1816Bussa's Rebellion — On Easter Sunday 1816, enslaved people across south-east Barbados rose up — the largest slave revolt in the island's history, and the first of three great risings that shook British slavery.
  • 1819The Peterloo Massacre — Tens of thousands of working people gathered peacefully to demand the vote. Within ten minutes, cavalry had cleared the field with sabres.
  • 1879The Irish Land War and the First Boycott — Irish tenant farmers organised against evictions and high rents — and gave the world a new word, 'boycott'.
