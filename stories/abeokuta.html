The Abeokuta Women's Revolt

Market women taxed without representation camped outside their king's palace, singing songs mocking him, until he gave up his throne. Their leader was Funmilayo Ransome-Kuti.

When
1946 – 1949
Where
Abeokuta, Nigeria
Facts checked
2026-11-15

See it on the map →

01

What is the background to this event?

Abeokuta, a Yoruba city in south-western Nigeria, was home to the Egba people. Under British "indirect rule", the Alake (king) of Abeokuta governed with British support.1

  • Traditionally, Egba councils had included at least one woman. The colonial council created in 1898 was entirely male.1
  • From 1918, women in Abeokuta had to pay taxes separately from men — taxation without representation.1
  • Market women, who dominated local trade, were also subject to fines, price controls and the seizure of goods, especially during the Second World War.

In their own words

03

What happened?

In 1946, the Abeokuta Ladies' Club, led by Funmilayo Ransome-Kuti, became the Abeokuta Women's Union (AWU), uniting working-class market women with middle-class women.1

  • The AWU proposed replacing the flat tax on women with taxes on foreign companies, investment in transport, sanitation and education, and a representative government that included women.1
  • When the Alake increased the tax on women, the AWU began mass protests. In October 1946, Ransome-Kuti led nearly a thousand women in a march to the palace.1
  • In 1947, the AWU published its grievances against the Alake and the colonial system, and held demonstrations outside the palace lasting two days and nights.1
  • The women sang songs mocking the Alake, refused to pay taxes, and closed the markets.1

The protests continued through 1948. In January 1949, the Alake abdicated and left the city.

04

What happened next?

  • Results: the separate taxation of women was ended, and four women were given seats on the local council.1
  • National movement: Ransome-Kuti founded the Nigerian Women's Union and campaigned for women's suffrage and independence.
  • Return: the Alake returned in 1950.
  • Later life: Ransome-Kuti was injured in 1977 when soldiers raided the home of her son Fela, and died in 1978.
05

Why does it matter?

The Abeokuta Women's Revolt is one of the most important women's movements in African history. Market women and educated women together challenged both colonial rule and male power.

  • It shows how women used traditional forms of protest — songs, sit-ins, market closures — in new ways.
  • It linked women's rights with anti-colonial nationalism.
  • The separate taxation of women was ended, and four women were given seats on the local council.1
  • Funmilayo Ransome-Kuti went on to found the Nigerian Women's Union and campaign for women's suffrage and independence.

What do others say?How it was seen at the time, and where historians disagree

At the time

The colonial authorities and the Alake saw the protests as disorder. The women argued that they were demanding fair taxation and representation.

Historians' debates

  • Class and gender: historians note how the AWU bridged divisions between elite and market women.
  • Tradition and modernity: scholars debate how far the women drew on Yoruba traditions of women's political action, and how far on new nationalist and feminist ideas.
  • Non-violence: the protests were largely non-violent, though police used force against the women.
How do we know?The evidence, and a source from the time
  • The AWU's petitions and records.
  • Colonial government files.
  • Wole Soyinka's memoir Aké: The Years of Childhood (1981), which describes the protests.

Reading it

Funmilayo Ransome-Kuti with her family, a few years before she led the women of Abeokuta. A family photograph presents her as a respectable, middle-class mother — which she was. It tells us nothing of her politics, or of how she came to stand with the market women.
