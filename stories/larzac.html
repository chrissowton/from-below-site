Gardarem lo Larzac: Farmers against the Army

When the French army announced it would take their land for a military camp, 103 sheep farmers swore an oath not to sell. Ten years of non-violent resistance followed — and they won.

When
1971 – 1981
Where
The Larzac plateau, Aveyron, France
Facts checked
2026-11-16

See it on the map →

01

What is the background to this event?

The Larzac is a high limestone plateau in southern France, used for raising sheep whose milk makes Roquefort cheese. Since 1902, a military training camp had occupied part of the plateau.1

  • On 28 October 1971, Defence Minister Michel Debré announced that the camp would be extended from 3,000 to 17,000 hectares, expropriating land from 107 farms.1
  • The government argued that the land was poor and thinly populated. One minister described the farmers as raising a few sheep while living as if in the Middle Ages.1
  • The farmers — both old local families and younger newcomers — argued that their farms were viable and that the camp would bring no lasting jobs.1

In their own words

03

What happened?

On 28 March 1972, 103 of the 107 farmers threatened with expropriation signed a pact — the "Oath of the 103" — promising that no farmer would be forced off the land against his will.1 The slogan Gardarem lo Larzac ("We will keep the Larzac"), in the Occitan language, appeared across the region.1

The farmers used inventive, non-violent tactics:1

  • They drove tractors to Paris in 1973, and in 1974 brought 60 sheep to graze under the Eiffel Tower.
  • Mass rallies on the plateau in 1973 and 1974 drew between 60,000 and more than 100,000 people.
  • Supporters bought small shares in threatened land, making expropriation more complicated.
  • Farmers refused to pay part of their taxes, returned their military papers, and built a sheepfold without permission on land the army wanted.
  • In 1978, a second march to Paris was joined by around 40,000 supporters.

The protests were almost entirely non-violent, though there were clashes with riot police, and Maoist militants threw stones at the Socialist leader François Mitterrand when he visited a rally in 1974.1

04

What happened next?

  • Victory: in 1981, the newly elected president, François Mitterrand, who had opposed the extension, abandoned the project.1
  • Collective land: the land the state had already bought was handed to a collective body of farmers to manage — an unusual experiment in shared land use.
  • A new movement: the Larzac became a centre of the alter-globalisation movement. In 1999, José Bové and other farmers dismantled a McDonald's under construction in nearby Millau, and in 2003 a huge rally on the Larzac drew hundreds of thousands of people.
05

What do others say?

At the time

The government argued that the extension was vital for national defence and would benefit the local economy. Opponents saw it as militarism and a threat to their way of life.

Historians' debates

  • Local or national? The arrival of tens of thousands of left-wing activists, hippies and others brought support but also tensions with conservative farming families. Even Bové admitted the effect of the giant rallies on public opinion was mixed.1
  • Why did they win? Historians debate whether non-violent tactics or the change of government in 1981 was decisive.
  • Non-violence: the movement is often held up as a model of non-violence, though there were some clashes and acts of property damage.

How is it remembered?

  • The sheepfold of La Blaquière, built illegally by the farmers, is a symbol of the struggle.
  • The documentary Tous au Larzac (2011) won a César award.
08

Why does it matter?

The Larzac struggle is one of Europe's most successful campaigns of non-violent resistance. A few hundred farmers, with support from across France, defeated the army and the state.

  • It linked peace, land and rural life.
  • It showed the creativity of non-violent action.
  • It also linked to the revival of Occitan language and culture.
