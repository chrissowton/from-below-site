The Bosnian Spring and the Plenums

Workers whose factories had been stripped and closed set government buildings on fire. Then citizens gathered in open assemblies — the plenums — to decide their demands together, across ethnic lines.

When
February – April 2014
Where
Tuzla, Sarajevo and across Bosnia and Herzegovina
Facts checked

01

What is the background to this event?

The Bosnian War of 1992–95 killed around 100,000 people. The Dayton peace agreement created a complex political system dividing power between Bosniak, Serb and Croat parties.

  • Tuzla had been a major industrial city in socialist Yugoslavia, known for its multi-ethnic character.1
  • In the 2000s, state companies in Tuzla — making detergents, furniture and chemicals — were privatised. The new owners stripped their assets, stopped paying wages and declared bankruptcy, leaving workers without jobs, pensions or health insurance.1
  • Unemployment stood at between 27 and 47 per cent, and one in five people lived below the poverty line.1

03

What happened?

On 4 February 2014, former workers of privatised companies protested peacefully outside the cantonal government building in Tuzla, demanding unpaid wages, health insurance and pension contributions.1

  • The next day, protests turned violent as hundreds clashed with police.1
  • From 6 February, the protests spread to Sarajevo, Zenica, Mostar, Bihać and other cities in solidarity.1 A protester in Zenica said: "Today we fought for Tuzla, tomorrow we fight for all of us."1
  • On 7 February, protesters set fire to government buildings, including the cantonal government in Tuzla and part of the Presidency building in Sarajevo, where historical archives were damaged. Around 100 police officers were injured, mostly by stones.1
  • It was the most violent unrest since the end of the war.1

Several cantonal governments resigned. Citizens then organised plenums — open assemblies in which anyone could speak and vote — in Tuzla, Sarajevo and other towns. The plenums drew up demands, including the review of privatisations, the end of politicians' privileges and the formation of expert governments.

04

What happened next?

  • Floods: in May 2014, catastrophic floods hit the region, and protesters and plenum activists turned to organising aid.
  • Fading: by April, the protests had faded as participation declined.1 The plenums gradually stopped meeting, and few of their demands were met.
  • Workers' takeover: in Tuzla, workers of the Dita detergent factory later occupied and restarted their factory.
05

What do others say?

At the time

Some politicians blamed hooligans and claimed the protests were a plot by rival ethnic parties. Protesters insisted that their anger was about corruption and poverty, not ethnicity.

Debates since

  • Violence: the burning of government buildings and the destruction of archives were widely condemned, including by some protesters; others argued that years of peaceful protest had been ignored.
  • Ethnic divisions: the protests were strongest in the Bosniak-Croat Federation and weak in the Republika Srpska, showing the limits of cross-ethnic mobilisation.1
  • Success or failure? Commentators debate whether the plenums achieved anything lasting.

How is it remembered?

  • The 2014 protests are remembered as a moment when Bosnians protested together across ethnic lines.
08

Why does it matter?

The Bosnian protests of 2014 were remarkable for focusing on social and economic grievances rather than ethnic divisions. The plenums were an experiment in direct democracy.

  • They show how privatisation after socialism affected working people.
  • They raise questions about whether direct democracy can overcome entrenched political systems.

Questions to think about

  1. Why did workers in Tuzla begin the protests?
  2. Why do you think the protests turned violent?
  3. What were the plenums, and why were they important?
