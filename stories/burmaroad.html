The Burma Road Riot

Bahamian labourers building wartime airfields found that American workers doing the same job were paid twice as much. Their protest became a riot in which five Bahamians were shot — and the beginning of modern Bahamian politics.

When
1 June 1942
Where
Nassau, Bahamas
Facts checked
2026-11-16

See it on the map →

01

What is the background to this event?

During the Second World War, the British authorised the building of two military airfields in the Bahamas. The contract went to an American firm, the Pleasantville Construction Company.1

  • The company offered Bahamian workers 8 shillings a day, but British officials objected, and the rate was cut to 4 shillings.1
  • American workers on the same project were paid the full 8 shillings.1
  • When Bahamians protested, they were told to take it or leave it.1
  • The Bahamas were ruled by a small white merchant elite, the "Bay Street Boys".

In their own words

03

What happened?

On 1 June 1942, workers who had walked off the project marched on Burma Road in Nassau.1

  • The Attorney-General told them they would be heard if they sent a representative, but warned them not to "spoil the good impression" they had made. Many took this as a threat that they would be replaced.1
  • About 800 people rioted. Shops and buildings were damaged and looted, and the post office and library were set on fire.1
  • The authorities read the Riot Act and sent in the Volunteer Defence Force and the police.1
  • Five Bahamians were shot dead by the security forces, about 30 British personnel were injured, and 114 people were arrested.1

04

What happened next?

  • A settlement: the Duke of Windsor returned, imposed a curfew, and agreed to raise pay to 5 shillings a day with a free lunch. Half the workforce returned by 4 June.1
  • Unions and parties: the riot helped spur trade unions and the Progressive Liberal Party, founded in 1953.
  • Majority rule: in 1967 the PLP, led by Lynden Pindling, ended the rule of the white minority; independence followed in 1973.
05

What do others say?

At the time

The Duke of Windsor blamed communists and men avoiding the draft; workers blamed unfair pay.1

Debates since

  • Violence: rioters burned public buildings and looted shops; the five people killed were shot by the security forces.1
  • The Duke: some praise him for negotiating; historians note his racist attitudes.2

07

How is it remembered?

  • The riot is remembered as a turning point in the struggle for majority rule.
08

Why does it matter?

The Burma Road riot was the first major challenge to white minority rule in the Bahamas.

  • It shows how unequal pay could expose racial inequality.
  • It belongs to a wave of labour unrest across the British Caribbean.

What other events are like this?

  • 1937The Butler Riots: Trinidad's Oilfield Strike — Oil workers led by the preacher 'Buzz' Butler struck against low wages and racism. The violence that followed killed police and strikers alike — and the island's labour movement was born.
  • 1934Antonio Soberanis and the Labourers of Belize — With mahogany exports collapsing and Belize City still in ruins after a hurricane, a barber stood up in Battlefield Park and demanded work and a minimum wage. 'I'd rather be a dead hero than a living coward,' he said.
  • 1865The Morant Bay Rebellion — Thirty years after slavery ended, poor Black Jamaicans marched on a courthouse demanding justice and land. The governor's response — hundreds killed, flogged and hanged — scandalised Britain.
