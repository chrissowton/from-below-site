The January Events in Vilnius

When Soviet tanks rolled towards Lithuania's television tower and parliament, tens of thousands of unarmed people came out to defend them. Fourteen civilians were killed — but the parliament was never taken.

When
11 – 13 January 1991
Where
Vilnius TV Tower and the Parliament, Vilnius, Lithuania

See it on the map →

01

What is the background to this event?

The Soviet Union annexed Lithuania in 1940; Western governments never recognised the occupation.1

  • The movement Sąjūdis, founded in 1988, led a peaceful campaign for independence.
  • On 11 March 1990, Lithuania declared the restoration of its independence.1
  • Moscow imposed an economic blockade from April to June 1990. Prices rose sharply.1
  • On 10 January 1991, Soviet leader Mikhail Gorbachev demanded that Lithuania restore the Soviet constitution. Special forces, including the Alpha Group, were flown in.1
02

Who were the key people?

  • Vytautas Landsbergis

    Chairman of the Supreme Council, who called on people to defend the parliament and other buildings.

  • Loreta Asanavičiūtė

    A 23-year-old woman crushed by a tank, one of the fourteen civilians killed.

  • The defenders

    Tens of thousands of unarmed people who surrounded key buildings day and night.

  • Viktor Shatskikh

    A Soviet Alpha officer killed, probably by his own side's fire.

03

What happened?

On 11–12 January 1991, Soviet troops seized the Press House and other buildings in Vilnius. Landsbergis called on people to gather around the parliament, the radio and television building, the TV Tower and the telephone exchange.1

  • People came from all over Lithuania and formed human walls around the buildings.1
  • After midnight on 13 January, a column of tanks and armoured vehicles headed for the TV Tower.1
  • The tanks fired blank rounds; soldiers fired into the crowd. Fourteen civilians were killed, most of them shot and two crushed by tanks. More than 140 were injured.1
  • Soviet forces took the radio and television building; its last pictures showed a soldier switching off the camera. A small studio in Kaunas went on air instead.1
  • Around the parliament, defenders built barricades and stayed, singing and praying. The army did not attack.

04

What happened next?

  • Referendum: in February 1991, more than 90 per cent of voters supported independence.
  • Recognition: after the failed coup in Moscow in August 1991, the Soviet Union recognised Lithuania's independence.
  • Trial: in 2019, a Lithuanian court convicted 67 people, most in their absence, for their part in the January events, including the former Soviet defence minister Dmitry Yazov.
05

What do others say?

At the time

Soviet officials claimed that a pro-Moscow "National Salvation Committee" had asked for help, and that the defenders had shot first.1

Debates since

  • Gorbachev's role: historians still debate whether he ordered the attack or lost control of hardliners.2
  • Non-violence: the defenders were unarmed; the killings were carried out by Soviet forces.1
06

The evidence

  • Television and amateur video footage of the night of 13 January.
  • Evidence presented at the trial in Vilnius.
07

How is it remembered?

  • 13 January is the Day of the Defenders of Freedom in Lithuania.1
  • Crosses and a museum at the TV Tower remember the dead.
08

Why does it matter?

Unarmed civilians faced down tanks and protected their parliament, helping to bring about the end of the Soviet Union.

  • It is one of the clearest examples of mass non-violent defence.

Questions to think about

  1. Why did the Soviet leadership send troops in January 1991?
  2. Why did people gather around buildings rather than fight?
  3. Why did the army not attack the parliament?
  4. Who was responsible for the deaths?
09

What other events are like this?

  • 1989The Baltic Way — Around two million people joined hands in a human chain 675 kilometres long, across Estonia, Latvia and Lithuania, to demand freedom from the Soviet Union.
  • 19899 April 1989: The Tbilisi Massacre — Thousands of Georgians gathered peacefully in the heart of Tbilisi to demand independence. Before dawn, Soviet soldiers attacked them with spades and gas, killing 21 people, most of them young women.
  • 1988Estonia's Singing Revolution — Night after night in June 1988, crowds gathered at Tallinn's Song Festival Grounds to sing forbidden songs. The artist Heinz Valk gave the movement its name: the Singing Revolution.
