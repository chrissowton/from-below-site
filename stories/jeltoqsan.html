Jeltoqsan: The December Protests of 1986

When Moscow replaced Kazakhstan's leader with an outsider, Kazakh students marched into the main square of Alma-Ata — one of the first mass protests of the Gorbachev era, crushed by force.

When
16 – 19 December 1986
Where
Alma-Ata (Almaty), Kazakhstan
Facts checked
2026-11-16

01

What is the background to this event?

Kazakhstan had been part of the Russian Empire and then of the Soviet Union. Under Soviet rule, the Kazakh people suffered enormously: around a third or more died in the famine caused by collectivisation in the early 1930s, and many more were exiled or repressed. Large numbers of Russians, Ukrainians and deported peoples were settled in Kazakhstan, and by the 1980s Kazakhs were a minority in their own republic.

  • Dinmukhamed Kunaev, a Kazakh, had led the Communist Party of Kazakhstan since 1964.
  • In 1985 Mikhail Gorbachev became leader of the Soviet Union and began a campaign against corruption and stagnation, removing many regional leaders.
  • On 16 December 1986, Kunaev was dismissed and replaced by Gennady Kolbin, a Russian who had not previously lived or worked in Kazakhstan.1

03

What happened?

On the morning of 17 December 1986, 200–300 students gathered in front of the Communist Party's Central Committee building on Brezhnev Square in Alma-Ata to protest against Kolbin's appointment. Their numbers grew to between 1,000 and 5,000 as students from other universities and institutes joined them.1 The Soviet news agency TASS reported that "a group of students, incited by nationalistic elements", had taken to the streets.1

The authorities responded with force:1

  • Police and troops, joined by volunteer militia units, broke up the protest with batons and fire hoses in freezing temperatures.
  • On the second day, the protests turned into clashes in the streets, universities and student hostels, and spread to other cities.
  • Official reports later put the number of protesters at 3,000; other estimates put it at 30,000–40,000, and the movement's leaders said that over 60,000 people took part across Kazakhstan.

According to the Kazakh government at the time, two people died. The writer Mukhtar Shakhanov later claimed that a KGB officer had testified that 168 protesters were killed. Thousands were detained, many students were expelled from their universities, and dozens were sentenced to prison or labour camps.1

04

What happened next?

  • "Kazakh nationalism": in 1987 the Soviet Communist Party officially condemned the protests as a manifestation of "Kazakh nationalism", and many participants faced continued discrimination.
  • Re-examination: under glasnost, a commission of the Kazakh Supreme Soviet investigated the events in 1989–90, and the condemnation was withdrawn.
  • Kolbin replaced: in 1989 Kolbin was replaced by Nursultan Nazarbayev, a Kazakh, who later became the first president of independent Kazakhstan.
  • Independence: Kazakhstan declared its independence on 16 December 1991 — exactly five years after the start of Jeltoqsan.
05

What do others say?

At the time

Soviet authorities described the protesters as drunken hooligans, drug addicts and nationalists. Participants insisted that it was a peaceful protest against an undemocratic appointment.

Historians' debates

  • Nationalism or democracy? Some participants rejected the label of nationalism, saying they were protesting against the appointment of an outsider, not against Russians or the Soviet Union. Others see it as an awakening of national consciousness.
  • Spontaneous or organised? Some accounts suggest that the protests were encouraged by officials loyal to Kunaev; most historians emphasise the spontaneous anger of students.
  • How many died? The number of deaths and the treatment of those arrested remain disputed, and many archives remain closed.

07

How is it remembered?

  • The Dawn of Liberty monument in Almaty, unveiled in 2006, commemorates the protests.
  • Kazakhstan's Independence Day, 16 December, coincides with the anniversary.
  • Streets across Kazakhstan are named Jeltoqsan, and the protests are commemorated each December.
08

Why does it matter?

Jeltoqsan was one of the first mass protests in the Soviet Union under Gorbachev, and the first major challenge from a non-Russian nation. Young Kazakhs demanded the right to be governed by people who understood their country — and their protest is now seen as a forerunner of the national movements that broke up the Soviet Union.

  • It showed the limits of glasnost when people actually took to the streets.
  • It is remembered in Kazakhstan as the first step towards independence.
  • The struggle to recognise the victims continues.

What other events are like this?

  • 1916The 1916 Central Asian Revolt (Ürkün) — When the Tsar ordered Central Asian men to be conscripted for labour behind the front lines, the region rose in revolt. The repression, and the flight across the mountains to China, cost well over a hundred thousand lives.
  • 1989The Baltic Way — Around two million people joined hands in a human chain 675 kilometres long, across Estonia, Latvia and Lithuania, to demand freedom from the Soviet Union.
  • 1989Tiananmen Square, 1989 — Students and workers occupied the heart of Beijing for seven weeks, calling for freedom of speech and an end to corruption, until the army cleared the streets with tanks and gunfire.
