The Zapatista Uprising

On the day the North American Free Trade Agreement came into force, masked Indigenous rebels seized towns in southern Mexico and declared 'Enough!'

When
1 January 1994
Where
Chiapas, Mexico
Facts checked

01

What is the background to this event?

Chiapas, in the far south of Mexico, is rich in natural resources but was one of the poorest states in the country. Much of its population is Indigenous — Tzeltal, Tzotzil, Ch'ol, Tojolabal and others — and many communities lacked land, health care, schools and political rights.

  • In the 1970s and 1980s, the Catholic diocese of San Cristóbal de las Casas, led by Bishop Samuel Ruiz, trained thousands of Indigenous catechists, and many communities organised politically.1
  • The Zapatista Army of National Liberation (EZLN) was founded on 17 November 1983 by members of an urban guerrilla group from northern Mexico and Indigenous people from the Lacandon Jungle region. Over the following decade it grew among the Indigenous communities.1
  • In 1992 the Mexican government amended Article 27 of the Constitution — the article that had embodied the land reform of the revolution — to allow communal ejido land to be privatised and to end land redistribution.1
  • The North American Free Trade Agreement (NAFTA) between Mexico, the United States and Canada, due to come into force on 1 January 1994, threatened small maize farmers with competition from subsidised US agriculture.

In their own words

03

What happened?

In the early hours of 1 January 1994 — the day NAFTA came into force — around 3,000 armed insurgents of the EZLN seized the city of San Cristóbal de las Casas and other towns in Chiapas, including Ocosingo, Las Margaritas and Altamirano.1 They issued the First Declaration of the Lacandon Jungle, which declared war on the Mexican government and listed their demands: "work, land, housing, food, health care, education, independence, freedom, democracy, justice and peace".1 The Declaration began:

"We are a product of 500 years of struggle… Today we say ¡Basta! — Enough!"First Declaration of the Lacandon Jungle, 1 January 199412

The Mexican army responded with force. Fighting lasted around twelve days; the bloodiest clashes took place in Ocosingo, and more than a hundred people were killed, including civilians.4 Huge demonstrations in Mexico City called for peace, and on 12 January the government declared a ceasefire and agreed to negotiate.2

The Zapatistas quickly became famous around the world:1

  • Subcomandante Marcos's communiqués, full of humour, stories and poetry, were widely read, and the movement used the early internet to spread its message.
  • Women's demands were central: the EZLN's Women's Revolutionary Law, published in January 1994, declared women's right to choose their partners, decide how many children to have, hold positions of leadership and live free from violence.
  • The Zapatistas invited supporters from around the world to "encuentros" (gatherings) in their territory.

04

What happened next?

  • The San Andrés Accords: in February 1996 the EZLN and the government signed accords on Indigenous rights and culture. But the constitutional reform passed in 2001 fell far short of them, and the Zapatistas rejected it.
  • Violence: the army and paramilitary groups continued to operate in Chiapas. In December 1997, paramilitaries massacred 45 members of the pacifist group Las Abejas, many of them women and children, at Acteal.
  • Autonomy: the Zapatistas built their own autonomous municipalities. In 2003 they created five regional centres (caracoles) with "Good Government Councils", running their own schools, clinics and cooperatives.
  • Continuing struggle: the movement still exists; in 2023 it reorganised its autonomous structures, and it continues to issue statements on Mexican and world affairs.
05

What do others say?

At the time

The Mexican government first described the rebels as a small group of foreign-led "professionals of violence" manipulating Indigenous people. Supporters in Mexico and abroad celebrated the Zapatistas as a new kind of revolutionary movement.

Historians' and commentators' debates

  • Who led? Critics argued that Marcos, an educated mestizo from the north, overshadowed the Indigenous leadership; the Zapatistas insisted that the Indigenous committee (CCRI) was in charge, and Marcos later "retired" his persona in 2014.
  • Armed or peaceful? The EZLN began as an armed movement, but after twelve days of fighting it largely relied on political means. Commentators debate how far its weapons were essential to its success.
  • Has autonomy worked? Supporters point to Zapatista schools, health care and the improved position of women; critics point to continuing poverty, isolation and divisions within communities.

The evidence

  • The EZLN's own declarations and communiqués, published in the Mexican newspaper La Jornada and online.
  • Reports by human rights organisations, such as the Fray Bartolomé de Las Casas Human Rights Center.
  • Journalism, photography and video from 1994 onwards, and the accounts of visitors to Zapatista communities.

How is it remembered?

  • The Zapatistas still mark 1 January with celebrations in their communities.
  • Zapatista murals, embroidered dolls and images of masked rebels are found across Chiapas and around the world.
  • Their influence can be traced in the global justice movement, in Indigenous movements across Latin America, and in the World Social Forum.
08

Why does it matter?

The Zapatista uprising was one of the first major rebellions against neoliberal globalisation, and one of the most influential movements of the late twentieth century. It placed Indigenous rights and autonomy on the national agenda in Mexico, and inspired movements across the world.

  • Its slogan "a world in which many worlds fit" and its practice of "leading by obeying" (mandar obedeciendo) influenced the global justice movement of the 1990s and 2000s.
  • It built one of the longest-lasting experiments in self-government by Indigenous communities.
  • It linked the history of the Mexican Revolution — Zapata's demand for land — to new struggles over trade, land and identity.

What other events are like this?

  • 1911Zapata and the Plan of Ayala — The peasant revolutionary Emiliano Zapata declared that the land, woods and waters taken from Mexico's villages must be returned — a demand that shaped the Mexican Revolution.
  • 2000The Cochabamba Water War — When Bolivia sold its city water system to a multinational consortium and bills soared, farmers, factory workers and residents rose up — and won.
  • 2016Standing Rock and the Dakota Access Pipeline — Indigenous 'water protectors' and their allies camped beside the Missouri River to oppose an oil pipeline, in the largest gathering of Native Americans in more than a century.
