The Anti-Hindi Agitation in Tamil Nadu

When Hindi was due to become India's sole official language, Tamil students rose in protest. Months of unrest, police shootings and self-immolations forced the government to keep English — and changed Indian politics.

When
1937 – 1940 and January – March 1965
Where
Madras (Chennai), Madurai and across Tamil Nadu, India

See it on the map →

01

What is the background to this event?

India is home to hundreds of languages. Hindi is the most widely spoken, but the languages of South India, such as Tamil, belong to a different language family, and Tamil has a literary tradition more than two thousand years old.

  • In 1937, the Congress government of Madras Presidency, led by C. Rajagopalachari (Rajaji), made Hindi compulsory in 125 secondary schools.1
  • E. V. Ramasamy, known as Periyar, and the Justice Party led a campaign of protest from 1937 to 1940. More than a thousand protesters were arrested, and two, Natarasan and Thalamuthu, died in prison; the order was withdrawn in 1940.1
  • After independence, the Constitution of 1950 made Hindi the official language of India, with English to continue as an associate official language for fifteen years — until 26 January 1965.1
  • Many non-Hindi-speaking people, especially in the south, feared that they would be disadvantaged in government jobs and education. In 1963, Prime Minister Jawaharlal Nehru passed the Official Languages Act to allow the continued use of English beyond 1965, but many doubted that his assurances would be honoured.1
02

Who were the key people?

  • E. V. Ramasamy (Periyar)

    A social reformer and leader of the Self-Respect Movement, who led the first agitation against Hindi in 1937–40.

  • C. N. Annadurai

    Founder of the Dravida Munnetra Kazhagam (DMK), which led the opposition to Hindi. He became chief minister of Madras State in 1967.

  • The students

    College and university students across Madras State, who formed the backbone of the 1965 protests.

  • Lal Bahadur Shastri

    Prime Minister of India, who gave assurances that English would continue.

  • The 'language martyrs'

    Protesters who were killed by police or took their own lives in protest, including several who set themselves on fire.

03

What happened?

As 26 January 1965 approached, the anti-Hindi movement gained momentum, with growing support from college students.1 The DMK called for 26 January — Republic Day — to be observed as a day of mourning.

  • On 25 January, a minor clash between students and Congress party members in Madurai triggered a riot, which spread across the state.1
  • For two months, the state saw demonstrations, strikes, arson, looting, police firing and lathi (baton) charges.1
  • Several protesters set themselves on fire or took poison in protest against Hindi.
  • The state government called in paramilitary forces. About 70 people were killed by official estimates, including two policemen; unofficial estimates were much higher.1

To calm the situation, Prime Minister Lal Bahadur Shastri assured the country that English would continue to be used as an official language as long as the non-Hindi-speaking states wanted it.1

04

What happened next?

  • Political change: in the 1967 state election, the DMK won a landslide victory, and Annadurai became chief minister. The Congress party has never since governed the state on its own.
  • English protected: in 1967, the Official Languages Act was amended to guarantee the indefinite use of both Hindi and English as official languages of the Union.
  • Two languages: in 1968, Madras State adopted a two-language policy in schools — Tamil and English — rejecting the national "three-language formula" that included Hindi. In 1969 the state was renamed Tamil Nadu.
  • Continuing debates: further protests took place in 1968 and 1986, and the question of Hindi in education remains politically sensitive today.
05

What do others say?

At the time

Supporters of Hindi argued that a single Indian language was needed for national unity, and that English was a colonial language. Opponents argued that imposing Hindi would make non-Hindi speakers second-class citizens, and that Tamil was as old and rich as any Indian language.

Historians' debates

  • Language or politics? Historians such as Sumathi Ramaswamy explore how devotion to Tamil became a powerful political and emotional force; others emphasise the role of the DMK in mobilising the protests for electoral advantage.
  • The violence: the protests involved attacks on property and on Congress offices, the killing of two policemen, and harsh police and paramilitary repression that killed far more protesters. Historians debate how far the violence was organised.
  • Self-immolation: the self-sacrifice of young protesters has been honoured as martyrdom, but has also raised difficult questions about the glorification of suicide in political movements.
06

The evidence

  • Government records and the debates of the Indian Parliament and the Madras Legislative Assembly.
  • Tamil and English newspapers, and the speeches and writings of Periyar and Annadurai.
  • Tamil poetry and songs written in praise of the language and of the martyrs.
07

How is it remembered?

  • 25 January is observed in Tamil Nadu as Language Martyrs' Day.
  • The language martyrs are commemorated with memorials in Chennai and elsewhere.
08

Why does it matter?

The anti-Hindi agitations were among the most important language movements in the world after independence from colonial rule. They established that India would remain a multilingual country, in which no single language would be imposed on all.

  • They transformed the politics of Tamil Nadu, bringing Dravidian parties to power.
  • They helped to secure the continued use of English, with lasting effects on Indian education and economic life.
  • They raise enduring questions about how a diverse country can balance national unity and linguistic equality.

What other events are like this?

  • 1952The Bengali Language Movement — Students in Dhaka defied a ban on protest to demand that Bengali be a national language of Pakistan. Police opened fire. The date is now International Mother Language Day.
  • 1956The Galle Face Satyagraha and the 'Sinhala Only' Act — When Ceylon's parliament made Sinhala the only official language, Tamil leaders sat in peaceful protest outside. A mob attacked them — and the violence that followed was the first step on the road to civil war.
  • 1976The Soweto Uprising — Thousands of Black schoolchildren marched against being taught in Afrikaans. Police opened fire, and the uprising spread across the country.
