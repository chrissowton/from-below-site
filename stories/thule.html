The Inughuit and the Thule Relocation

To make way for an American air base, the world's northernmost people were moved from their homes in four days, in winter. Fifty years later, they won their case in Denmark's Supreme Court.

When
1953 – 2003
Where
Uummannaq (Dundas) and Qaanaaq, north-west Greenland
Facts checked
2026-11-16

See it on the map →

01

What is the background to this event?

The Inughuit are the northernmost Indigenous people in the world, living in north-west Greenland. They are descended from the Thule people who spread across the Arctic around the eleventh century.1

  • Their main settlement was Uummannaq, known to Europeans as Thule or Dundas.1
  • Greenland was a Danish colony; in 1953 it became part of the Danish kingdom.
  • During the Cold War, the United States built Thule Air Base near Uummannaq, between 1951 and 1953, as part of its defence against the Soviet Union.2

03

What happened?

In 1953, the US Air Force planned to expand the base with an air defence site near the village.2 To prevent contact between Inughuit and soldiers — which it considered "unhealthy" — the Danish government relocated about 130 inhabitants.2

  • The move took place within four days, in winter.2
  • Families were moved about 100 kilometres north to a new settlement, Qaanaaq ("New Thule").2
  • They lost access to their hunting grounds, which were central to their way of life. The move was disastrous for their cultural and social life.1

For decades, the relocation was barely discussed. In 1968, a US B-52 bomber carrying nuclear weapons crashed near the base, spreading radioactive material; many local Greenlandic workers took part in the clean-up.1

04

What happened next?

  • The court case: the relocated families and their descendants, organised as Hingitaq 53, took the Danish state to court. In 1999, a Danish court ruled that the relocation was a serious violation, and the Danish prime minister apologised.
  • The Supreme Court: on 28 November 2003, Denmark's Supreme Court confirmed that the relocation was an expropriation. The Thule tribe was awarded 500,000 kroner, and individuals 15,000 or 25,000 kroner each.2
  • Not enough: the Inughuit argued that this was far too little and that they had a right to return to their land. In 2006, the European Court of Human Rights declared their case inadmissible.
  • Return of land: in 2003, the Dundas peninsula was returned to Danish jurisdiction.2
05

Why does it matter?

The Thule relocation is an example of how Cold War militarisation affected Indigenous peoples. Its people sought justice for decades through the courts.

  • It connects to Greenland's wider struggle for self-government, which led to home rule in 1979 and self-rule in 2009.
  • It raises questions about whether compensation can make up for the loss of land and way of life.
  • In 1999 a Danish court ruled the relocation a serious violation, and the Danish prime minister apologised.

What do others say?How it was seen at the time, and where historians disagree

At the time

The Danish government presented the move as voluntary and in the Inughuit's interest. The Inughuit later said they were given no real choice.

Debates since

  • Voluntary or forced? The courts recognised that the move was not voluntary in the full sense.2
  • Indigenous rights: the courts did not recognise the Inughuit as a separate Indigenous people with land rights, which they and their supporters contest.
  • Colonialism: the case is part of a wider re-examination of Danish colonialism in Greenland, including forced contraception of Greenlandic girls in the 1960s–70s.
