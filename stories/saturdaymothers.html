The Saturday Mothers

Every Saturday at noon, mothers of people who disappeared in state custody sit silently in an Istanbul square holding their photographs. They have kept coming for decades, despite bans and arrests.

When

Where

01

What is the background to this event?

In the 1980s and 1990s, during military rule and the conflict in Turkey's mainly Kurdish south-east, many people disappeared in state custody or were killed by unknown perpetrators.1

  • The Human Rights Association recorded 792 forced disappearances and killings in eastern Turkey between 1992 and 1996.1
  • Families could get no answers about what had happened.

03

What happened?

Inspired by the Mothers of the Plaza de Mayo, the first sit-in took place on 27 May 1995.1

  • Every Saturday at noon, the group sits for half an hour in front of Galatasaray High School, holding photographs of the missing.1
  • They faced police attacks almost every week, and in March 1999 halted their vigil after especially harsh attacks.1
  • They resumed on 31 January 2009, and grew from about 30 people to thousands of supporters.1
  • In 2011, Prime Minister Erdoğan met the mothers and promised to help.1
  • In August 2018, at their 700th vigil, the gathering was banned; police used force and detained participants.1
04

What happened next?

  • Prosecution: in 2020, prosecutors sought prison terms of up to three years for protesters; the trial began in 2021.1
  • Awards: the group received the Hrant Dink Award in 2013.1
05

Why does it matter?

The Saturday Mothers are one of the world's longest-running non-violent protests.

  • They demand truth and justice for the disappeared.
  • In 2020 prosecutors sought prison terms of up to three years for protesters.1
  • Each vigil is numbered, a running count of how long the families have waited for answers.

What do others say?How it was seen at the time, and where historians disagree

At the time

The authorities treated the vigil as an illegal gathering; supporters see it as a model of civil disobedience.1

Debates since

  • Demands: the mothers ask for state archives to be opened, for an end to time limits on prosecuting political killings, and for Turkey to sign the UN convention against enforced disappearance.1
  • Violence: the mothers have never used violence; they have faced police violence, detentions and prosecution.12

Reading it

A vigil at Galatasaray in 1998. After heavy police attacks the mothers halted their vigils in March 1999 and resumed them only in January 2009. The photographs of the missing that they hold are their whole argument: a face and a name in place of an official silence.
