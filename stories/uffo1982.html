Uffo: The Hargeisa Volunteers and the Stone-Throwing

In the early 1980s, young professionals in Hargeisa rebuilt their city's run-down hospital with their own money and labour. The military government saw their self-help group as a threat and put them on trial. The city answered with three days of stone-throwing.

When
1981 – March 1982
Where
Hargeisa, Somaliland

01

What is the background to this event?

From 1969, Somalia was ruled by the military dictatorship of Siad Barre.1

  • After the Ogaden War with Ethiopia (1977–78), the regime's policies bore down hard on the Isaaq, the main clan of the north-west, around Hargeisa.1
  • Public services in the north were neglected; the central hospital in Hargeisa was in poor condition.1
  • The north-west was governed by General Mohamed Hashi Gani, a cousin of Siad Barre, who ruled with an iron fist.1

In their own words

03

What happened?

In the early 1980s, a volunteer movement, officially the Hargeisa Hospital Group, grew up in the city.1

  • Its members imported medicines and equipment with private money and worked without pay, renovating the whole of the central hospital.1
  • People nicknamed it 'Uffo' — the wind before the storm. It had no formal structure, membership list or dues, and became a symbol of self-help and of opposition to the government.1
  • The governor decided it had to be uprooted. Police arrested Uffo sympathisers and anyone linked to them, often on the basis of family ties, friendships or rumours.1
  • On 20 February 1982, the arrested volunteers were put on trial; on 6 March they were sentenced to long prison terms.1
  • The trial set off three days of rioting in Hargeisa, remembered as the dhagaxtuur, 'the stone-throwing'. When people threw stones at the police guarding the court, the police opened fire, killing about 30 civilians. The governor sent in tanks and armoured personnel carriers.1
04

What happened next?

  • Students: demonstrations spread across the northern regions. Human rights monitors wrote that the trial radicalised students and turned schools into battlegrounds between the government and students.1
  • Armed struggle: the Somali National Movement (SNM), founded in 1981, built up its fighters in Ethiopia from 1982 and fought a guerrilla war through the 1980s.1
  • Destruction: after an SNM offensive in 1988, the government shelled and bombed Hargeisa and Burao. Hargeisa was about 90% destroyed. Estimates of civilian deaths in 1987–89 range from 50,000 to 200,000, and up to 500,000 people fled to Ethiopia.3
  • Somaliland: on 18 May 1991, after the fall of Siad Barre, the SNM declared the north-west independent as the Republic of Somaliland. It is not recognised by most of the world.1
05

Why does it matter?

Uffo began not as a protest but as people doing a job the state had abandoned. The government's decision to treat volunteers as enemies turned a self-help group into a symbol of resistance — and its violent response marked a turning point on the road to civil war.

  • Human rights monitors wrote that the volunteers' trial radicalised students and turned schools into battlegrounds.1
  • The Somali National Movement fought a guerrilla war through the 1980s.1
  • After 1988, government forces destroyed much of Hargeisa; a fighter jet shot down over the city is now its war memorial.

What do others say?How it was seen at the time, and where historians disagree

At the time

The government treated Uffo as an anti-government organisation; the volunteers and their supporters saw themselves as serving their community.1

Debates since

  • Clan and politics: historians discuss how far the conflict in the north was driven by clan grievances, and how far by opposition to dictatorship.1
  • Violence on all sides: police shot protesters in 1982, and the government's campaign of 1987–89 has been described as genocide. The SNM's fighters also carried out attacks, including on refugee camps of Ogaden Somalis, whom they saw as a force used by the state.13
