The Katipunan

A secret society founded by a Manila clerk and his friends grew to tens of thousands of members. When the Spanish discovered it in 1896, its members tore up their identity papers and began the Philippine Revolution.

When
1892 – 1897
Where
Manila and Pugad Lawin, Philippines

See it on the map →

01

What is the background to this event?

The Philippines had been ruled by Spain for over three centuries.

  • In 1892, the reformist writer José Rizal was arrested and deported to Mindanao.1
  • Reformers had asked for Filipino representation in the Spanish parliament; many now decided that only armed revolution could win independence.1
02

Who were the key people?

  • Andrés Bonifacio

    Founder and leader of the Katipunan, from Tondo in Manila.

  • Gregoria de Jesús

    Early member of the women's section; married Bonifacio.

  • Melchora Aquino

    Known as the 'Grand Old Woman of Balintawak' for helping rebels.

  • Emilio Aguinaldo

    Revolutionary general who became president of the revolutionary government.

03

What happened?

The Katipunan was founded in Manila in July 1892 by Andrés Bonifacio and others, with the aim of independence through armed revolution.1

  • It was a secret society with initiation rites; women were later admitted and guarded its papers.1
  • Estimates of its membership by 1896 range from 15,000 to 400,000; historian Teodoro Agoncillo estimated about 30,000.1
  • In August 1896, the Spanish authorities discovered it.1
  • At a meeting in late August, members tore up their cedulas (identity papers) to show their commitment, and the revolution began.1

04

What happened next?

  • Spanish repression: Spain executed José Rizal in December 1896, though he had refused to support the revolution.
  • Division: in March 1897, at Tejeros, the revolutionaries replaced the Katipunan with a republic, and Bonifacio lost the presidency to Aguinaldo.1
  • Bonifacio's death: in May 1897, Bonifacio was executed by Aguinaldo's revolutionary government.
  • Empire again: in 1898, the United States took the Philippines from Spain and fought a war against Filipino independence.
05

What do others say?

At the time

Spain saw a criminal conspiracy; members saw themselves as "sons of the people".1

Debates since

  • Where and when? The date and place of the "Cry" that began the revolution are still disputed.1
  • Class: some historians see Bonifacio as a leader of the working class, sidelined by wealthier provincial leaders; others question this view.2
  • Violence on all sides: the revolution brought killings by both revolutionaries and Spanish forces, and the execution of Bonifacio by his own side.
06

The evidence

  • Katipunan documents and its newspaper Kalayaan.1
07

How is it remembered?

  • Bonifacio Day, 30 November, is a national holiday in the Philippines.
08

Why does it matter?

The Katipunan began the first revolution in Asia against a European colonial power.

  • Historians such as Reynaldo Ileto stress the popular, religious ideas that inspired ordinary members.2

Questions to think about

  1. Why did the Katipunan turn from reform to revolution?
  2. Why was tearing up the cedulas so symbolic?
  3. What roles did women play?
  4. Why did the revolutionaries execute Bonifacio?
09

What other events are like this?

  • 1763Gabriela Silang and the Ilocano Revolt — When Britain captured Manila, Diego Silang rose against Spanish rule in the north. After he was assassinated, his widow Gabriela took command — and became a symbol of Filipino women's resistance.
  • 1825Diponegoro and the Java War — A Javanese prince with visions of a holy war led peasants and religious teachers against Dutch rule. The war lasted five years, and at least 200,000 Javanese civilians died.
  • 1986The People Power Revolution — More than two million unarmed Filipinos filled a Manila highway to protect rebel soldiers and end Ferdinand Marcos's twenty-year rule.
