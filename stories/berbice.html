The Berbice Rebellion

Enslaved Africans led by Cuffy took control of almost an entire Dutch colony and held it for nearly a year. Cuffy proposed dividing the land with the Dutch. It was South America's first great slave revolt — three decades before Haiti.

When
February 1763 – April 1764
Where
Berbice, Dutch Guiana (now Guyana)

See it on the map →

01

What is the background to this event?

Berbice was a small Dutch colony of sugar, coffee and cacao plantations along the Berbice River, owned by a company of Amsterdam merchants.1

  • In 1762, its population included 3,833 enslaved Africans, 244 enslaved Indigenous people and only 346 Europeans.1
  • The Seven Years' War had cut supplies, and the enslaved were going hungry. Disease had killed many soldiers.1
  • Treatment on the plantations was brutal.
02

Who were the key people?

  • Cuffy (Kofi)

    Born in West Africa and enslaved; leader of the rebellion and now Guyana's national hero.

  • Accara and Atta

    Rebel captains, who later turned against Cuffy.

  • Wolfert Simon van Hoogenheim

    The Dutch governor of Berbice.

  • Kalina and Lokono fighters

    Indigenous allies of the Dutch, who harried the rebels in the interior.

03

What happened?

The rising began in late February 1763 on plantations along the Canje and Berbice rivers.1

  • More than 2,500 people joined, led by Cuffy and his deputy Accara. They seized firearms and attacked plantations.1
  • At the plantation Peereboom, the rebels told the besieged Europeans they could leave, then killed many of them as they did so.1
  • The Dutch abandoned most of the colony and held out near the coast.
  • On 2 April 1763, Cuffy wrote to the governor proposing peace: the Dutch would keep the coast, and the free Africans the interior.1
  • The governor played for time. Soldiers arrived from Suriname in March, and six ships from the Netherlands on 1 January 1764.1
  • The rebels quarrelled. Atta rose against Cuffy, who took his own life in October 1763.1
  • Indigenous soldiers prevented the rebels from setting up free communities in the forest.1

By April 1764 the revolt was crushed. Accara changed sides and helped the Dutch hunt the last rebels.1 More than a hundred captured rebels were executed, many by burning or breaking on the wheel.2

04

What happened next?

  • Slavery restored: the plantations were rebuilt, and slavery continued in the colony until 1834, under British rule.
  • A national day: when Guyana became a republic in 1970, it chose 23 February, the anniversary of the rising, as Republic Day.1
05

What do others say?

At the time

The Dutch saw only a murderous mutiny. The rebels' letters show they sought a negotiated freedom.2

Debates since

  • Violence on both sides: the rebels killed European men, women and children, and some rebel leaders forced other Africans to work for them; the Dutch tortured and executed prisoners on a large scale.12
  • Divisions: Marjoleine Kars shows that many enslaved people tried to stay out of the fighting, and that differences of origin and aims split the rebels.2
06

The evidence

  • Cuffy's letters to the governor.
  • The governor's journal and the interrogations of some 900 captured rebels, in the Dutch National Archives.2
07

How is it remembered?

  • The 1763 Monument, a statue of Cuffy by Philip Moore, stands in Georgetown.1
08

Why does it matter?

For almost a year, formerly enslaved people governed a colony and negotiated with a European power as equals.

  • It was the first major slave revolt in South America.1
  • The records of the trials preserve the voices of hundreds of enslaved people.

Questions to think about

  1. Why did the rebellion succeed at first?
  2. What did Cuffy hope to achieve by proposing to divide the colony?
  3. Why did the rebels fall out with each other?
  4. Why did Indigenous people fight for the Dutch?
09

What other events are like this?

  • 1791The Haitian Revolution — The only revolution in history in which enslaved people overthrew their enslavers and founded an independent state.
  • 1605Palmares: The Free Kingdom in the Hills — For almost a century, people who had escaped slavery built a free society in the hills of north-east Brazil and resisted repeated attempts to destroy it.
  • 1762The Saramaka Maroons: Freedom a Century Early — Enslaved Africans escaped from Suriname's plantations into the rainforest and fought the Dutch to a standstill. In 1762 — a century before emancipation — the Dutch signed a treaty recognising their freedom.
