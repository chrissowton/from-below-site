The Bagaudae

Peasants, runaway slaves and deserters in late Roman Gaul and Spain formed armed bands against landlords and tax collectors. For nearly two centuries the Bagaudae rose again and again.

When
c. 284 – 450s
Where
Gaul and Hispania (Armorica and the Ebro valley)
Facts checked
2026-11-16

See it on the map →

01

What is the background to this event?

In the late third century, the Roman Empire faced civil war, invasion and economic crisis.1

  • Taxes increased, and small farmers fell into debt and dependence on great landowners.1
  • The Bagaudae were peasants, runaway slaves, deserters and other poor people who rebelled in Gaul and Hispania.1
02

Who were the key people?

  • Amandus and Aelianus

    Leaders of the Bagaudae in the 280s.

  • Tibatto

    Leader of an uprising in Armorica in 435.

  • Basilius

    A leader of the Bagaudae in the Ebro valley in the 440s.

  • Maximian

    Roman co-emperor who crushed the first rising.

03

What happened?

In the 280s, bands of Bagaudae rose in Gaul under the leaders Amandus and Aelianus.1

  • They were crushed in 286 by Maximian.1
  • A speech in praise of Maximian (the Panegyric of 289) describes farmers taking up arms as soldiers.1
  • In the fifth century the Bagaudae rose again. In 435, Tibatto led a revolt in Armorica (north-west Gaul).1
  • In the 440s and 450s, Bagaudae were active in the Ebro valley of Spain, and fought Roman and Visigothic armies.1

04

What happened next?

  • The end of Roman Gaul: Roman rule in the West collapsed in the later fifth century.
  • Brittany: Armorica later became Brittany, settled by Britons.
05

What do others say?

At the time

Roman writers described them as bandits; the Christian writer Salvian of Marseille argued that Roman injustice had driven people to become Bagaudae.

Debates since

  • Social revolution? Marxist historians saw a class struggle; others see local self-defence led by landowners when Roman authority failed.12
  • Violence: the Bagaudae attacked towns and landowners; Roman and Visigothic armies suppressed them brutally.1
06

The evidence

  • Short mentions in Roman chronicles and panegyrics.1
  • Salvian, On the Government of God.
07

How is it remembered?

  • The Bagaudae have been remembered by French and Spanish historians, and by the left, as early rebels of the poor.
08

Why does it matter?

The Bagaudae are one of the few glimpses we have of the rural poor resisting the late Roman Empire.

  • They raise questions about how empires fall, and who benefits.

Questions to think about

  1. Why did peasants and slaves join the Bagaudae?
  2. Why do we know so little about them?
  3. Were the Bagaudae rebels, bandits or local self-government?
  4. What does their story tell us about the fall of Rome?
09

What other events are like this?

  • 73 BCESpartacus and the Slave Revolt — Seventy gladiators broke out of a training school armed with kitchen knives. Within two years, an army of some 120,000 enslaved people had defeated Rome's legions again and again.
  • 135 BCEEunus and the First Sicilian Slave War — Enslaved workers on Sicily's great estates rose up under a Syrian slave and prophet named Eunus. They took cities, crowned him king, and held off Roman armies for three years.
  • 69 CEThe Revolt of the Batavi — A people of the Rhine delta who supplied Rome with soldiers turned their weapons on the empire. Led by a prince who had served Rome for decades, they destroyed two legions before making peace.
