Workers & unions

The Matchwomen's Strike

About 1,400 women and girls walked out of a match factory, won their demands in three weeks, and founded the largest union of women in Britain.

When
July 1888
Where
Bryant & May factory, Bow, East London

See it on the map →

01

What is the background to this event?

Bryant & May's Fairfield Works in Bow was one of the largest factories in the East End of London and a highly profitable business: its original £5 shares were trading at more than £18 by 1888. Most of its match-makers were women and teenage girls, many from Irish Catholic families in the surrounding streets.

Conditions in the factory were harsh:

  • Working days of up to fourteen hours, for piece-rates that could leave a young worker with only a few shillings a week.
  • A system of fines deducted from wages — for example 3d for having dirty feet (many workers could not afford shoes), for talking or for an untidy bench, and a shilling for leaving burnt matches on the bench.
  • Exposure to white phosphorus, which caused "phossy jaw", a form of bone necrosis that could disfigure and kill. Safer alternatives existed but were more expensive.

Trade unions at this time were dominated by skilled male craftsmen. Unskilled and casual workers, and especially women, were widely regarded as impossible to organise.

02

Who were the key people?

  • Sarah Chapman

    A machine operator in her mid-twenties who was elected to the strike committee and then to the first committee of the new union. In 2022 English Heritage unveiled a blue plaque at the factory site in her honour, alongside the strike.

  • Annie Besant

    A socialist, secularist and journalist whose article in The Link exposed conditions at the factory, and who helped to organise publicity and funds once the strike began.

  • Herbert Burrows

    A socialist who worked closely with Besant on the investigation and the strike.

  • The strike committee

    Elected representatives of the workforce who negotiated directly with the company and with Members of Parliament.

  • The 1,400

    Women and girls, many in their teens, who walked out without a union, without strike funds and without the vote.

03

What happened?

On 23 June 1888, Annie Besant published an article in her halfpenny weekly, The Link, titled "White Slavery in London", based on interviews with match workers about their wages, fines and conditions.1 In her autobiography she recalled the case of a sixteen-year-old who earned four shillings a week and lived on bread, butter and tea, and who "related with dancing eyes that once a month she went to a meal where 'you get coffee and bread and butter, and jam and marmalade, and lots of it.'"2

The company responded by trying to force its workers to sign a statement denying the article's claims. The women refused. According to Besant, a delegation told her:

"You had spoke up for us, and we weren't going back on you."Matchwomen's delegation to Annie Besant, July 1888, recorded in Besant, An Autobiography (1893)2

Around 2 July 1888 a worker was dismissed on a pretext. In response, roughly 1,400 women and girls stopped work, and by 6 July the whole factory had come to a halt.3 The strike that followed was notable for its organisation and public reach:

  • The strikers marched to Besant's office in Fleet Street to ask for help, and a strike fund was opened; donations were published in The Link.3
  • On 11 July a deputation of matchwomen met Members of Parliament at the House of Commons, and Charles Bradlaugh MP raised the case in Parliament.3
  • The London Trades Council agreed to act as an intermediary with the company.

At a meeting on 16 July, terms were agreed: fines and deductions were abolished, grievances could be taken directly to management, and the strikers returned to work.3 The workers then founded the Union of Women Match Makers — in Besant's words, for some years "the strongest woman's Trades Union in England."2

04

What happened next?

The strike's success had effects far beyond Bow:

  • It became one of the starting points of "New Unionism", the organisation of unskilled and semi-skilled workers in general unions. The gas workers organised in 1889, and the Great London Dock Strike of the same year won the "dockers' tanner".
  • Public pressure over phosphorus poisoning continued. The International Berne Convention of 1906 prohibited white phosphorus in matches, and Britain banned it by the White Phosphorus Matches Prohibition Act 1908, which came into force in 1910.
  • Bryant & May eventually built a separate drying room and later moved to safer processes; the Bow factory closed in 1979 and is now a housing development, Bow Quarter.
05

What do others say?

At the time

Bryant & May denied the claims made in The Link, threatened Annie Besant with legal action, and blamed outside agitators for the strike. Some newspapers sympathised with the company and portrayed the workers as rough and disorderly.

Historians' debates

  • Who led the strike? For many years the story centred on Besant. Louise Raw's research argued that the matchwomen took the decision to strike themselves and had their own leaders and traditions of protest. Other historians have accepted much of this while continuing to stress the importance of Besant's publicity.
  • What was it about? Lowell Satre has argued that the workers were more concerned with pay and fines than with the dangers of phosphorus, which became central to later campaigns.
  • How significant was it? Some historians see it as the beginning of 'New Unionism'; others note that the matchwomen's union declined in the 1890s and that the dock strike of 1889 was more influential.
06

The evidence

  • Besant's articles in The Link and her later Autobiography — valuable, but written by someone with a stake in the story.
  • The strike fund accounts and the register of strikers, which name individual women.
  • Company statements, newspaper reports and parliamentary questions.
  • Photographs of the strike committee, and later medical reports on phosphorus poisoning.

Very few of the matchwomen left written accounts, so historians have used census records and family histories to reconstruct their lives.

07

How is it remembered?

  • The strike was long remembered mainly through Besant's role; renewed interest since the 2000s has focused on the workers themselves.
  • In 2022 English Heritage unveiled a blue plaque at the former factory, now the Bow Quarter housing development, in the presence of Sarah Chapman's descendants.
  • A musical, The Matchgirls (1966), and later plays have dramatised the strike.
08

Why does it matter?

For many years the strike was told as a story of middle-class reformers rescuing helpless working girls. The historian Louise Raw's research in the 2000s helped to change that interpretation, showing that the matchwomen had a tradition of collective action, that they took the decision to strike themselves, and that their own leaders, such as Sarah Chapman, played a central role. This shift — from the famous names to the people in the crowd — is precisely what history from below seeks to achieve.

  • It demonstrated that unskilled women workers could organise and win.
  • It showed the power of combining industrial action with publicity, consumer pressure and political lobbying.
  • It contributed directly to the regulation of industrial poisons in the workplace.

Questions to think about

  1. Why do you think the strike was for so long remembered mainly as Annie Besant's achievement?
  2. How did the strikers use publicity and public opinion to win?
  3. What can census and family records tell us that newspapers cannot?
  4. Are there workers today whose conditions might be compared to those of the matchwomen?
