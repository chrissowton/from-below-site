The Street Overturns a Stolen Election: Côte d'Ivoire, 2000

A military ruler barred his main rivals from an election, then claimed victory when he lost anyway. Tens of thousands marched into soldiers' gunfire and forced him to flee — but the violence that followed turned on other Ivorians.

When
22 – 27 October 2000
Where
Abidjan, Côte d'Ivoire
Facts checked
2026-11-18

See it on the map →

01

What is the background to this event?

In December 1999, General Robert Guéï seized power in a coup and became head of a military government.12

  • The idea of ivoirité ("Ivorian-ness"), promoted under the previous president, Henri Konan Bédié, helped disqualify the opposition leader Alassane Ouattara.3
  • The Supreme Court disqualified 14 candidates, including Ouattara and six from Bédié's party. Of the major opposition figures, only Laurent Gbagbo of the Ivorian Popular Front was allowed to stand.31
  • The two excluded parties boycotted the election, and some of their leaders backed Gbagbo to block Guéï.13

03

What happened?

The election was held on 22 October 2000, with historically low turnout.3

  • Guéï claimed to have won in the first round. It soon emerged that Gbagbo had won about 59% of the vote.1
  • On 24 October, Guéï dissolved the electoral commission, while soldiers tried to unplug its computer servers.3
  • On 25 October, tens of thousands of people took to the streets. Soldiers fired on them, but the protesters forced Guéï to flee.3
  • On 26 October, Gbagbo was sworn in as president.3
  • Supporters of Ouattara's party then took to the streets, demanding a new election in which their candidate could stand. They clashed violently with supporters of Gbagbo's party.3
  • On 27 October, a mass grave containing 57 bodies was found in Yopougon, a district of Abidjan. They were supporters of Ouattara's party, killed by militias aligned with Gbagbo's party.34
04

What happened next?

  • Civil war: in September 2002, the First Ivorian Civil War broke out. Guéï, his wife and several members of his family were killed on its first day.2
  • No election for ten years: the next presidential election was not held until 2010.1
  • Another disputed election: in 2010, both Gbagbo and Ouattara claimed the presidency, and about 3,000 people were killed in the conflict that followed. Gbagbo was arrested in April 2011.4
05

What do others say?

At the time

Gbagbo's supporters saw a victory for democracy over military rule; Ouattara's supporters argued that an election from which their candidate had been barred could not be legitimate.13

Debates since

  • A people's victory? The protests forced out a military ruler, but the election itself excluded most of the opposition.1
  • Violence: about 300 people were killed in the unrest after the election, according to the French-language Wikipedia, including the 57 found in the Yopougon mass grave.3
06

The evidence

  • Election results, the discovery of the Yopougon grave, and reports by journalists and human rights organisations.34
07

How is it remembered?

  • Gbagbo became the first elected president of Côte d'Ivoire who was not a member of the PDCI (Democratic Party of Côte d'Ivoire).3
08

Why does it matter?

In October 2000, ordinary people in Abidjan refused to accept a stolen election and drove out a military ruler. But the exclusion of candidates on grounds of identity, and the killings that followed, foreshadowed a decade of conflict.134

Open questions

  1. Why did people risk soldiers' gunfire to defend an election result?
  2. Was an election from which leading candidates were barred a fair one?
  3. Why did the protests of one party's supporters lead to attacks on another's?
  4. How can an idea like ivoirité, about who belongs to a nation, lead to violence?

What other events are like this?

  • 1949The Women's March on Grand-Bassam — When French authorities jailed leaders of the anti-colonial movement without trial, hundreds of women walked from Abidjan to the prison at Grand-Bassam to demand their release.
  • 1990Benin's National Conference — Strikes by unpaid teachers, students and civil servants forced Benin's military ruler to call a national conference. The delegates declared themselves sovereign, took power peacefully — and inspired democratic movements across Africa.
  • 2016Solo Sandeng and the Fall of Yahya Jammeh — An opposition activist led a small protest for electoral reform and died in custody the next day. His death set off protests, mass arrests — and, eight months later, the defeat of a ruler who had held power for 22 years.
