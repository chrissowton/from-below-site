The Paris Commune

After defeat in war, the working people of Paris took over their city and governed it for 72 days. The Commune ended in 'Bloody Week', when the army killed thousands — and Communards shot their hostages.

When
18 March – 28 May 1871
Where
Paris, France
Facts checked

01

What is the background to this event?

In 1870, Emperor Napoleon III led France into war with Prussia and was quickly defeated. A republic was proclaimed in Paris on 4 September 1870, and the Prussian army besieged the city.1

  • During the four-month siege, Parisians suffered hunger and bombardment; people ate horses, rats and even animals from the zoo.
  • The city was defended by the National Guard, a citizen militia of about 300,000 men, mostly from working-class neighbourhoods such as Belleville and Ménilmontant, where radical ideas spread.1
  • In January 1871 the government signed an armistice. Elections in February produced a conservative, monarchist majority, reflecting the views of rural France; Adolphe Thiers became head of government.1
  • Parisians felt humiliated and betrayed. The new government ended the National Guard's pay and the moratorium on rents and debts.

Who were the key people?

  • Louise Michel

    A teacher and anarchist who fought with the National Guard and organised ambulances. She was deported to New Caledonia, where she supported the Kanak revolt of 1878.

  • Eugène Varlin

    A bookbinder and leader of the International Workingmen's Association, killed by soldiers during Bloody Week.

  • Élisabeth Dmitrieff and Nathalie Lemel

    Organisers of the Women's Union for the Defence of Paris and Care of the Wounded.

  • Adolphe Thiers

    Head of the French government, who ordered the army to retake Paris.

  • Georges Darboy

    The Archbishop of Paris, taken hostage by the Commune and shot on 24 May 1871.

  • The Communards

    Tens of thousands of workers, artisans and National Guardsmen, men and women.

In their own words

03

What happened?

Before dawn on 18 March 1871, Thiers sent troops to seize the National Guard's cannon on the hill of Montmartre. Local people, many of them women, surrounded the soldiers, who refused to fire and fraternised with the crowd. Two generals, Claude Lecomte and Clément Thomas, were captured and shot by soldiers and guardsmen. Thiers withdrew the government and army to Versailles.1

The Central Committee of the National Guard organised elections, and on 26 March the Commune of Paris was elected. Its members included workers, artisans, journalists and radicals of many kinds. In its 72 days it decreed:1

  • the separation of church and state, and free, secular education;
  • the remission of rents owed during the siege, and the return of tools and household goods from pawnshops;
  • the abolition of night work in bakeries, and the right of workers to take over workshops abandoned by their owners as cooperatives;
  • the abolition of child labour.

Women played a prominent part, in the Women's Union, in clubs, as nurses and on the barricades. On 16 May the Commune pulled down the Vendôme Column, a monument to Napoleon's wars.1

On 21 May the army of the Versailles government entered Paris. In the "Bloody Week" (semaine sanglante) that followed, Communards fought from barricade to barricade. Government forces killed or executed an estimated 10,000 to 15,000 Communards, many of them shot after surrender. Communards set fire to the Tuileries Palace, the Hôtel de Ville and other buildings, and in the Commune's final days executed the Archbishop of Paris and about a hundred hostages, mostly gendarmes and priests.1 On 28 May, 147 Communards were shot against a wall in Père Lachaise cemetery, now known as the Communards' Wall.

04

What happened next?

  • Trials and deportation: the army took 43,522 prisoners, including 1,054 women. About 13,500 were found guilty; 95 were sentenced to death, and more than a thousand were deported, mostly to New Caledonia.
  • Amnesty: surviving prisoners and exiles were pardoned in 1880, and many returned to political life.
  • The Third Republic: the conservative republic survived, and over the following decades introduced free, secular primary education and the separation of church and state (1905) — two of the Commune's demands.
  • Sacré-Cœur: the basilica of Sacré-Cœur was built on Montmartre, partly as an act of atonement for the Commune.
05

What do others say?

At the time

The French government and most of the press described the Communards as criminals, arsonists and murderers, emphasising the killing of the generals and the hostages and the burning of Paris. Supporters, such as Marx, presented the Commune as a heroic workers' government drowned in blood.

Historians' debates

  • How many died? Historians such as Robert Tombs have argued for lower figures, of between 6,000 and 7,000 deaths, based on a 2012 study of cemetery records; others, such as John Merriman, support higher estimates. All agree that the great majority of deaths were caused by government forces, many in summary executions.
  • The Commune's violence: the execution of the generals and of the hostages, and the burning of public buildings, are part of the story; historians debate how far these were the acts of a minority in the chaos of defeat.
  • What kind of revolution? Marxists, anarchists and republicans have all claimed the Commune. Historians emphasise that its members held many different views, and that it was also a patriotic protest against defeat.
06

The evidence

  • The Commune's own decrees and newspapers, and the records of its meetings.
  • The records of the courts martial and of the official inquiry.
  • Photographs of barricades and of the destruction, and photographs of dead Communards, among the earliest photographs of political violence.
  • Memoirs by Communards such as Louise Michel and Prosper-Olivier Lissagaray.

07

How is it remembered?

  • Every year, people gather at the Communards' Wall in Père Lachaise cemetery.
  • The song "L'Internationale" was written by Eugène Pottier, a Communard, in 1871.
  • The 150th anniversary in 2021 sparked debate in France over how the Commune should be commemorated.
08

Why does it matter?

The Paris Commune was the first time that working people took control of a great modern city and tried to govern it in their own interests. Its policies — secular education, workers' cooperatives, the rights of tenants and women's participation — inspired socialists, anarchists and labour movements around the world.

  • Karl Marx described it as the first example of a workers' government.
  • Its brutal suppression was one of the bloodiest episodes in French history.
  • It remains a reference point for debates about revolution, democracy and violence.

Questions to think about

  1. Why do you think the National Guard and the people of Paris refused to give up their cannon?
  2. Which of the Commune's policies seem most radical to you, and which have since become normal?
  3. How should we judge the violence committed both by the Communards and by the government?
  4. Why has the Commune been claimed by so many different political traditions?

What other events are like this?

  • 1789The Women's March on Versailles — Angry at the price of bread, thousands of Paris market women marched twelve miles in the rain to Versailles — and brought the King of France back to Paris.
  • 1378The Revolt of the Ciompi — The wool workers of Florence, excluded from the guilds that ran the city, seized the government — and for a few weeks a wool-carder sat at the head of the republic.
  • 1956The Hungarian Revolution of 1956 — Students, workers and soldiers rose against Hungary's Soviet-backed dictatorship and pulled down Stalin's statue. For twelve days they won — until Soviet tanks returned.
