Gabon 1990: Strikes, a National Conference and the End of One-Party Rule

For 22 years Omar Bongo had ruled Gabon as a one-party state. In 1990, strikes by students and workers forced him to call a national conference. Then the mysterious death of an opposition leader set Port-Gentil alight.

When
January – October 1990
Where
Libreville and Port-Gentil, Gabon

01

What is the background to this event?

Omar Bongo became president of Gabon in 1967 and made it a one-party state under his Gabonese Democratic Party (PDG).1

  • Gabon was rich in oil, much of it produced by the French company Elf, and Bongo had strong backing from France.2
  • An opposition group demanding multiparty democracy, the Movement for National Renewal, was suppressed in 1982.3
  • In the mid-1980s, falling oil prices caused an economic downturn, and the government imposed austerity.3
02

  • Students and workers

    Whose strikes and demonstrations began the movement.

  • Joseph Rendjambe

  • The people of Port-Gentil

    Gabon's oil town, the centre of the May riots.

  • President Omar Bongo

    Who agreed to reforms but stayed in power.

03

What happened?

In early 1990, students and workers held strikes and demonstrations, some of them violent, demanding better pay and political freedom.4

  • Bongo negotiated with workers sector by sector and granted large wage increases.4
  • He promised a national conference, held in March–April 1990. The PDG and 74 political organisations took part.4
  • The conference agreed sweeping reforms, including freedom of assembly and of the press, and the end of exit visas.4
  • Opposition parties were legalised on 21 April, and parliament approved a multiparty system in May.5
  • On 23 May, Joseph Rendjambe was found dead in a Libreville hotel in unclear circumstances. Riots broke out in Libreville and Port-Gentil, where protesters suspected the government.25
04

What happened next?

  • French troops: France sent some 300 extra soldiers to protect French citizens, and Elf shut down its offshore oil production for six days.56 Order was restored in Port-Gentil by 29 May.5
  • Elections: in September–October 1990, Gabon held its first multiparty elections in almost 30 years. The PDG won 63 of 120 seats.45
  • A new constitution with a bill of rights took effect in 1991, but it kept strong powers for the president.4
  • Bongo stayed: he remained president until his death in 2009, and was succeeded by his son, Ali Bongo, who was overthrown in a military coup in 2023.
05

Why does it matter?

Gabon was one of many African countries where protests in 1990 ended one-party rule. Its story shows both what such movements won — freedom to organise and speak — and how long-serving rulers could adapt to keep power.

  • In 1990 Gabon held its first multiparty elections in almost 30 years.45
  • France sent some 300 extra soldiers after unrest in Port-Gentil, showing how closely the former colonial power still watched Gabon.56
  • Port-Gentil's international airport has been named after the opposition leader Joseph Rendjambe since 2025.7

What do others say?How it was seen at the time, and where historians disagree

At the time

Protesters accused the government of involvement in Rendjambe's death; the government denied it, and France said its troops were there only to protect its citizens.25

Debates since

  • Real or façade? Some analysts argue that the reforms of 1990 changed little, since Bongo and his party kept control; others stress the new freedoms that were won.1
  • Violence on both sides: rioters in Port-Gentil held seven Elf and three Shell employees hostage for several hours; some five people were reported killed in the violence there.56
