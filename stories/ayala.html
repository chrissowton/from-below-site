Zapata and the Plan of Ayala

The peasant revolutionary Emiliano Zapata declared that the land, woods and waters taken from Mexico's villages must be returned — a demand that shaped the Mexican Revolution.

When
28 November 1911
Where
Ayala, Morelos, Mexico

See it on the map →

01

What is the background to this event?

Under the long dictatorship of Porfirio Díaz (1876–1911), Mexico's economy grew rapidly, but most of the benefits went to a small elite and to foreign investors. In the countryside, land was concentrated in the hands of a few great estates (haciendas):

  • In the small state of Morelos, just south of Mexico City, sugar plantations expanded dramatically, taking over the land, woods and water of Indigenous and mestizo villages, many of which held communal land titles dating back to the colonial period.
  • Villagers who lost their land were forced to work on the haciendas for low wages, and some villages disappeared altogether.
  • Emiliano Zapata, a farmer and horse trainer from the village of Anenecuilco, was elected in 1909 to lead his village's defence of its land titles.

In 1910 Francisco Madero, a wealthy landowner from the north, called for a revolution against Díaz in his Plan of San Luis Potosí, which promised that land taken unjustly would be returned. Zapata and many of the villagers of Morelos joined the revolution, and in May 1911 Díaz was forced into exile.1

02

Who were the key people?

  • Emiliano Zapata

    The leader of the peasant revolution in Morelos (1879–1919). He was assassinated in 1919 on the orders of President Venustiano Carranza.

  • Otilio Montaño

    A village schoolteacher and Zapata's mentor, who helped draft the Plan of Ayala.

  • Francisco Madero

    The leader of the revolution against Díaz, elected president in November 1911, whom the Plan denounced as a traitor.

  • The villagers of Morelos

    The peasants of Morelos and neighbouring states who formed the Liberation Army of the South.

03

What happened?

After the fall of Díaz, Madero met Zapata on 8 June 1911 and demanded that Zapata's army disarm as a precondition for any discussion of land reform. Zapata refused. Madero was elected president in November, and the federal army moved against the Zapatistas.1

On 28 November 1911, only three weeks after Madero became president, Zapata issued the Plan of Ayala in the village of Ayala, Morelos. It had been drafted with the help of Otilio Montaño.1 The Plan had fifteen points. Among other things, it:1

  • denounced Madero for betraying the revolution and declared that he was no longer recognised as president;
  • declared that land, woods and water taken by landowners and political bosses should be returned immediately to the villages and citizens who held titles to them, and that they should defend this with arms in hand;
  • declared that one third of the land of the great monopolies should be expropriated, with compensation, so that villages and landless people could have common lands and fields;
  • ordered that the property of landowners who opposed the Plan be nationalised.

The Plan concluded with an appeal to the Mexican people:

"Mexicans: consider that the cunning and bad faith of one man is shedding blood in a scandalous manner, because he is incapable of governing; consider that his system of government is choking the fatherland and trampling with the brute force of bayonets on our institutions…"The Plan of Ayala, 28 November 19111

The Plan was signed with the slogan "Reforma, Libertad, Justicia y Ley" ("Reform, Liberty, Justice and Law"). The Zapatistas' later rallying cry, "Tierra y Libertad" ("Land and Liberty"), became one of the most famous slogans of the revolution.1

04

What happened next?

  • Years of war: the Zapatistas fought Madero, then the military dictator Victoriano Huerta (who had Madero murdered in 1913), and then Venustiano Carranza. The Plan was amended in June 1914 to continue the struggle against Huerta.
  • Land redistributed: in Morelos, the Zapatistas returned land to the villages in the areas they controlled.
  • The 1917 Constitution: Article 27 of the new constitution gave the nation ultimate ownership of land and water and allowed land to be redistributed to villages as ejidos (communal holdings).
  • Assassination: on 10 April 1919 Zapata was lured into a trap at the hacienda of Chinameca and killed on Carranza's orders. His followers later negotiated a land reform in Morelos with the next president, Álvaro Obregón.
  • Land reform: under President Lázaro Cárdenas (1934–1940), millions of hectares were distributed to peasant communities.
05

What do others say?

At the time

The Mexico City press and the landowners of Morelos described Zapata as a bandit and "the Attila of the South", and the federal army fought the Zapatistas with great brutality, burning villages and deporting their inhabitants.

Historians' debates

  • Local or national? John Womack's classic study describes the Zapatistas as "country people who did not want to move and therefore got into a revolution" — a deeply local movement. Others stress their national and radical significance.
  • Who wrote the Plan? Historians debate the exact roles of Zapata, Montaño and other advisers in drafting it.
  • Was the land reform a success? Mexico's land reform redistributed huge areas, but critics argue that ejidos were often too small, poorly supported and controlled by the ruling party. Constitutional changes in 1992 allowed ejido land to be sold.
06

The evidence

  • Original copies and manuscripts of the Plan of Ayala, including one held in the Centro de Estudios de Historia de México.
  • Zapatista correspondence and decrees, held in the Archivo General de la Nación.
  • Photographs, including the work of Agustín Casasola, and the oral testimony of Zapatista veterans collected in later decades.
07

How is it remembered?

  • Zapata is honoured throughout Mexico, with statues, streets and towns named after him, and a museum at his birthplace in Anenecuilco.
  • He appears in the murals of Diego Rivera, including those in the Palace of Cortés in Cuernavaca.
  • The 1994 Zapatista uprising in Chiapas took his name and his slogan.
08

Why does it matter?

The Plan of Ayala made land reform the central demand of the Mexican Revolution. It expressed the demand of peasant communities that the land, woods and water on which they depended should belong to those who worked them — a demand that echoed across Latin America and the world in the twentieth century.

  • It shaped Article 27 of the 1917 Constitution and Mexico's land reform programme.
  • Zapata became one of the most powerful symbols of peasant struggle, and his name was taken by the Zapatista movement of the 1990s.
  • The idea that land should belong to those who work it — la tierra es de quien la trabaja — remains a powerful one.

Questions to think about

  1. Why do you think the Zapatistas refused to disarm before land reform had been agreed?
  2. What do the words 'land, woods and water' tell us about how villagers depended on their environment?
  3. Should land belong to those who work it? What might be the arguments for and against?
  4. Why has Zapata remained such a powerful symbol more than a century later?
