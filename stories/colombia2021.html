Colombia's National Strike

In the middle of the pandemic, the government proposed new taxes on food and utilities. Colombians struck and blockaded the roads; the tax plan was withdrawn within days, but weeks of protest and police violence followed.

When
28 April – June 2021
Where
Cali, Bogotá, Medellín and towns across Colombia
Facts checked
2026-11-17

01

What is the background to this event?

In 2021, Colombia was suffering one of the worst COVID-19 outbreaks in Latin America and its worst economic performance in fifty years.1

  • Forty-two per cent of Colombians earned less than US$90 a month, and one in four people under 28 was unemployed.1
  • President Iván Duque proposed a tax reform to fund an expanded basic income programme and reduce the deficit. It would have extended VAT to more food and utilities and brought some middle-class earners into a higher tax bracket.1
  • The government also proposed changes to the health care system.1

In their own words

03

What happened?

Tens of thousands of people began demonstrating on 28 April 2021, despite a court ban on protests because of COVID-19.1

  • In Cali, Misak protesters tore down the statue of the Spanish conquistador Sebastián de Belalcázar. Two people were killed on the first day.1
  • The mayor of Cali asked the president to withdraw the reform: "the tax reform is dead. We don't want it to cause more deaths."1
  • The government sent 4,000 troops and police to Cali. The defence minister linked violence in the city to criminal and 'terrorist' groups.1
  • On 2 May, Duque withdrew the tax reform, but protests continued, driven by anger at police violence.1
  • By 3 May, the ombudsman reported 19 deaths related to the protests, while the human rights organisation Temblores reported 21 and the teachers' union 27.1
  • Road blockades continued through May, causing shortages of food and fuel. By 21 May, protesters alleged more than 2,000 cases of police brutality, including 27 cases of sexual violence, and around 200 people had been reported missing.1
04

What happened next?

  • Abuses investigated: the UN human rights office and Human Rights Watch documented abuses by police against protesters.1
  • A new government: in the 2022 election, held in the aftermath of the protests, Gustavo Petro became the first left-wing candidate in Colombia's recent history to be elected president, with Francia Márquez the first Afro-Colombian vice-president.4
05

Why does it matter?

The National Strike of 2021 brought young people, workers and Indigenous communities onto the streets in the middle of a pandemic. The 2022 election, which brought the left to power for the first time in Colombia's recent history, was held in its aftermath.14

  • The UN human rights office and Human Rights Watch documented abuses by police against protesters.1
  • In 2022 Gustavo Petro became the first left-wing candidate in Colombia's recent history to be elected president.4
  • Francia Márquez became the first Afro-Colombian vice-president.4

What do others say?How it was seen at the time, and where historians disagree

At the time

Protesters denounced the tax reform and police brutality; the government and the former president Álvaro Uribe defended the police and blamed criminal groups for violence.1

Debates since

  • Blockades: supporters saw road blockades as a legitimate form of strike; critics pointed to the shortages of food and fuel they caused.1
  • Violence: casualty figures from the ombudsman, human rights groups and unions differed; police officers were also among the dead and injured.1
How do we know?The evidence, and a source from the time
  • Videos and photographs shared on social media, and reports by the ombudsman, Temblores, the UN and Human Rights Watch.1

Reading it

Human rights defenders try to talk with riot police on the first day of the strike. Their presence was meant to record abuses and calm confrontations. As the weeks went on, the behaviour of the police became a reason for protest in its own right.
