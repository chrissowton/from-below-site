The July Uprising in Bangladesh

A student protest over government job quotas grew into a nationwide uprising. The crackdown killed as many as 1,400 people, and on 5 August 2024, Prime Minister Sheikh Hasina fled the country after fifteen years in power.

Ongoing situationLast reviewed 16 November 2026

The new BNP government's programme differs in places from the reforms in the July Charter, and it is not yet clear how much of the Charter will be put into law.1 Hasina remains in India.

Still unresolved:

  • How far the July Charter reforms will be carried out
  • Whether trials for the 2024 killings will meet fair-trial standards
  • Whether the Awami League will be allowed back into politics

This is recent history. Accounts, casualty figures and outcomes may change as more evidence emerges.

01

What is the background to this event?

Sheikh Hasina's Awami League had governed Bangladesh since 2009.2

  • Its election victories in 2014, 2018 and 2024 were widely seen as unfair; major opposition parties boycotted the 2014 and 2024 elections.2
  • Opposition politicians and critics faced arrests, and the media worked under heavy pressure.2
  • Government jobs were highly sought after. A system of quotas reserved 30% of them for descendants of the fighters of the 1971 war of independence. Students saw this as favouring families close to the ruling party.2
  • In June 2024, a court ruling reinstated the quotas.2

In their own words

03

What happened?

Students began protesting against the quotas in early July 2024, with road blockades they called the "Bangla Blockade".2

  • Hasina referred to the protesters as "grandchildren of Razakars", the name for those who collaborated with Pakistan in 1971. The remark turned the protests against the government itself.2
  • Police, paramilitary forces and activists of the ruling party's student wing attacked the protesters. On 16 July, Abu Sayed was shot dead by police in Rangpur.2
  • The Supreme Court soon cut back the quotas, but the protests continued, now demanding justice for those killed.2
  • On 3 August, the student coordinators announced a single demand: Hasina's resignation.2
  • On 5 August, as crowds marched on Dhaka, Hasina resigned and fled to India.2

A UN fact-finding report estimated that as many as 1,400 people may have been killed between 15 July and 5 August, most of them shot by security forces, and that 12–13% of those killed were children.45 The interim government's own count was lower, at 834.6 Bangladesh police reported that 44 of their officers were killed.5 After Hasina fell, there were revenge attacks on Awami League supporters and on Hindus and other minorities.4

04

What happened next?

  • Interim government: Muhammad Yunus led an interim government. In August 2025 it issued the "July Declaration", promising a free election and a referendum on a charter of constitutional reforms.2
  • Trials: on 17 November 2025, Bangladesh's International Crimes Tribunal sentenced Hasina and her former home minister to death in absentia for crimes against humanity. Human Rights Watch welcomed accountability but raised concerns about a trial held in absentia and the death penalty. India has not extradited her.789
  • Election: on 12 February 2026, the Bangladesh Nationalist Party (BNP) led by Tarique Rahman won a two-thirds majority, in an election from which the Awami League was barred. Voters also approved the "July Charter" of constitutional reforms in a referendum.1019
05

What do others say?

At the time

The protesters saw a fight against discrimination and dictatorship. Official reports at the time put the death toll at 215.2 In exile, Hasina has said the tribunal's trials were never intended to achieve justice.11

Debates since

  • Death toll: estimates range from the interim government's 834 to the UN's estimate of as many as 1,400.46
  • Justice: supporters of the trials see accountability for mass killing; critics, including rights groups, worry about fair trials and the death penalty.7
  • What kind of revolution? Some see a democratic revolution; others fear that the old two-party struggle has simply returned, with one party excluded.1

07

How is it remembered?

  • The uprising is officially commemorated, and the "July Declaration" of August 2025 gave it constitutional recognition.2
08

Why does it matter?

The July Uprising showed how a narrow student grievance could grow, through repression, into a movement that brought down a long-entrenched government. It raises hard questions about revolutionary justice and whether the new order will avoid repeating the old.

Open questions

  1. How did a protest about job quotas become a movement to remove the government?
  2. Why did Hasina's 'Razakars' remark cause such anger?
  3. Can a trial held in absentia deliver justice?
  4. How should a country deal with revenge violence after a revolution?

What other events are like this?

  • 1952The Bengali Language Movement — Students in Dhaka defied a ban on protest to demand that Bengali be a national language of Pakistan. Police opened fire. The date is now International Mother Language Day.
  • 2025Nepal's Gen Z Uprising — When the government banned 26 social media platforms, young Nepalis angry at corruption and 'nepo kids' took to the streets. Police shot protesters dead, the parliament was burned, and the prime minister resigned within two days.
  • 2024Reject Finance Bill: Kenya's Gen Z Protests — Young Kenyans organised online against a bill that would have taxed bread and sanitary pads. They forced the president to withdraw it, but at least 60 people were killed in 2024, and dozens more when protests returned in 2025.
