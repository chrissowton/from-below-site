Mé 67: The Pointe-à-Pitre Massacre

Construction workers struck for a 2.5 per cent pay rise. When negotiations broke down, French police opened fire. The official toll was 7 dead; decades later France admitted at least 87.

When
March – May 1967
Where
Pointe-à-Pitre and Basse-Terre, Guadeloupe
Facts checked
2026-11-15

See it on the map →

01

What is the background to this event?

Guadeloupe, a former French slave colony in the Caribbean, became an overseas department of France in 1946. But deep inequalities remained between the white elite and the Black majority, descendants of enslaved Africans.1

  • Much of the land and business was owned by white families, while many Black Guadeloupeans faced poverty and unemployment.
  • Inspired by decolonisation movements elsewhere, independence groups such as GONG (the Guadeloupe National Organisation Group) formed. The French government was not willing to negotiate.1
  • On 20 March 1967, a white shop owner in Basse-Terre, a local leader of the Gaullist party, set his dog on an elderly, disabled Black shoemaker, Raphaël Balzinc, using a racist insult. The incident sparked riots in Basse-Terre and Pointe-à-Pitre.1
02

Who were the key people?

  • The construction workers

    Who went on strike on 24 May 1967 for a 2.5 per cent pay rise and equal social rights.

  • Jacques Nestor

    A GONG activist, the first person killed on 26 May.

  • The young people of Pointe-à-Pitre

    Many of whom were killed or injured over two days.

  • The gendarmes and riot police

    French security forces sent to the island, who opened fire.

03

What happened?

On 24 May 1967, construction workers went on strike for a 2.5 per cent pay rise and parity in social rights with mainland France.1

On 26 May, a crowd gathered outside the Chamber of Commerce in Pointe-à-Pitre during negotiations.1

  • When news came that talks had broken down, a rumour spread that an employers' representative had said that when Black people were hungry, they would go back to work.1
  • Gendarmes fired tear gas; demonstrators threw conch shells, stones and bottles.1
  • After a gendarme was seriously injured, the mobile gendarmerie opened fire, killing Jacques Nestor.1 The authorities claimed that demonstrators fired first.1
  • Shooting continued into the night and the next day, as security forces fired on young people in the streets.

The official toll at the time was 7 or 8 dead.1 More than 30 gendarmes and riot police were injured.1 In 1985, a French minister for overseas territories acknowledged a toll of at least 87. Most estimates put the number of dead between 80 and 200.1

04

What happened next?

  • Trials: independence activists, rather than security forces, were put on trial in Paris for endangering state security; most were acquitted or given light sentences.
  • Silence: many archives were destroyed or remained classified, making it hard to establish the truth.1
  • Inquiry: in 2016, a commission of historians led by Benjamin Stora examined the events, but was unable to establish a definitive death toll.
  • Strikes continue: in 2009, a general strike against the high cost of living paralysed Guadeloupe for 44 days.
05

What do others say?

At the time

The French authorities blamed independence agitators for the violence. Guadeloupeans saw it as a colonial massacre.

Debates since

  • Who fired first? The authorities said demonstrators opened fire; most witnesses and historians dispute this.1
  • A planned massacre? Some sources argue that the use of lethal force was approved in Paris; this is disputed.1
  • Violence by demonstrators: demonstrators threw stones and conch shells and injured more than 30 security officers, but the response with live ammunition was out of all proportion.1
  • The death toll: because of destroyed archives, the toll remains uncertain.1

07

How is it remembered?

  • A mural in Pointe-à-Pitre commemorates the victims, painted in 2007.1
  • Commemorations in 2017, on the fiftieth anniversary, demanded the opening of classified archives.1
  • The events are remembered in Guadeloupean music and literature.
08

Why does it matter?

Mé 67 is a key event in the history of Guadeloupe and of France's overseas territories. It shows how a labour dispute, in a society shaped by slavery and racism, could become a massacre.

  • It raises questions about how states account for violence by their own forces.
  • It remains central to Guadeloupean identity and the independence movement.

Questions to think about

  1. Why do you think a small pay dispute led to such violence?
  2. Why was the official death toll so low?
  3. Why does it matter to establish the true number of deaths?
  4. How should France account for violence in its overseas territories?
