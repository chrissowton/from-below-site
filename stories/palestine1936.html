The Palestinian General Strike and Revolt

Palestinian Arabs began a six-month general strike against British rule and Jewish immigration, which became a three-year revolt. It was crushed with great force, and violence struck Arabs, Jews and Britons alike.

01

What is the background to this event?

After the First World War, Britain governed Palestine under a League of Nations mandate. The mandate incorporated the Balfour Declaration of 1917, in which Britain promised to support "a national home for the Jewish people" in Palestine, while protecting the rights of its existing, mainly Arab population.

  • Jewish immigration grew rapidly in the 1930s, as Jews fled persecution in Europe, especially after the Nazis came to power in 1933. Between 1931 and 1936 the Jewish population more than doubled, from 175,000 to 370,000.1
  • Arab peasants were losing land as estates were sold, often by absentee landlords, to Jewish settlement agencies; many landless people crowded into shanty towns in Jaffa and Haifa.1
  • Both Palestinian Arabs and the Jewish community (the Yishuv) were developing national movements. The Yishuv was building the institutions of a future state, including a covert militia, the Haganah.1
  • Violence between Arabs and Jews had recurred since 1920, including the riots of 1929. In 1935 the preacher Izz ad-Din al-Qassam, who had organised armed groups among the poor, was killed by British police.1

In their own words

03

What happened?

In April 1936, after the killing of two Jews by armed Arabs and reprisal killings of two Arabs, rioting in Jaffa killed a number of Jews. Local national committees called a general strike, and on 21 April the leaders of the five main Arab parties endorsed it.3 The Arab Higher Committee resolved to continue the strike until Britain changed its policy "in a fundamental manner". Its demands included an end to Jewish immigration, a ban on the transfer of land to Jews, and a national government responsible to a representative council.3

The strike, beginning on 19–20 April 1936, lasted until October and became one of the longest general strikes in history:3

  • Arab workers, transport and shopkeepers stopped work; the port of Jaffa was paralysed, and Arabs were called on not to pay taxes.
  • Britain responded with heavy collective fines on villages and towns. In Jaffa, under the guise of urban renewal, the British demolished hundreds of homes in the old city.
  • Strikers also used sabotage — throwing tacks on roads and attacking railways and the oil pipeline — and armed bands attacked British forces and Jewish settlements and travellers.

The strike was called off in October 1936, after appeals by the rulers of Iraq, Saudi Arabia and Transjordan.3 A British Royal Commission, the Peel Commission, recommended in 1937 that Palestine be partitioned. The proposal was rejected by the Arab leadership, and the revolt resumed in late 1937 as a mainly peasant-led insurgency. By 1938 rebels controlled much of the countryside and, briefly, the Old City of Jerusalem.1

The British responded with overwhelming force: collective punishment, the demolition of homes and villages, mass detention, and military courts. According to British figures, the army and police killed more than 2,000 Arabs in combat and 108 were hanged.1 The historian Walid Khalidi estimated some 5,000 Arab dead in total, including about 1,200 killed in internal violence among Palestinians. Estimates of the number of Jews killed are up to several hundred, and many British soldiers and police were also killed.1

04

What happened next?

  • The White Paper: in May 1939, Britain issued a White Paper limiting Jewish immigration to 75,000 over five years, restricting land sales and promising an independent Palestine within ten years. It was rejected by both the Arab leadership and the Zionist movement — which saw it as closing Palestine to Jews fleeing Nazi persecution.
  • A weakened society: by one estimate, ten per cent of the adult male Arab population had been killed, wounded, imprisoned or exiled; much of the Palestinian leadership was in exile, and the society was deeply divided.
  • Strengthened Jewish forces: Britain had armed and trained Jewish auxiliaries, and the Haganah grew stronger. Jewish militant groups, notably the Irgun, carried out bombings in Arab markets that killed many civilians.
  • 1948: the outcome of the revolt shaped the balance of forces in the war of 1947–49, which saw the establishment of the State of Israel and the displacement of more than 700,000 Palestinians — the Nakba.
05

What do others say?

At the time

Britain described the revolt as "disturbances" and the rebels as bandits and terrorists. Palestinian Arabs saw a national revolt against colonial rule and dispossession. The Zionist leadership saw an attack on the Jewish community and its right to build a national home; David Ben-Gurion acknowledged that Arabs feared growing Jewish economic power and mass immigration.

Historians' debates

  • Violence against whom? Historians record violence in many directions: rebel attacks on British forces, on Jewish civilians and settlements, and on Arabs accused of collaboration or belonging to rival factions; British repression, including killings, torture and destruction of villages; and attacks by Jewish groups on Arab civilians. Every side has its victims, and none of these can be left out.
  • Leaders and peasants: Ted Swedenburg and others emphasise the role of peasants and the urban poor, often acting independently of — or against — the traditional Palestinian elite.
  • British methods: Matthew Hughes has documented the systematic use of collective punishment and abuse by British forces.
  • Consequences: historians debate how far the defeat of the revolt determined the outcome of 1948.

07

How is it remembered?

  • The revolt is commemorated by Palestinians as a central event in their national history, and its songs and figures, such as al-Qassam, remain important symbols.
  • In Israeli memory, the "events of 1936–39" are remembered as a time of Arab attacks and Jewish self-defence.
  • The British use of collective punishment and emergency regulations remains the subject of public and legal debate.
08

Why does it matter?

The general strike and revolt of 1936–39 were the largest uprising in Palestine during British rule, and a defining event for Palestinian national identity. Ordinary townspeople and peasants sustained a six-month strike and a long rebellion against colonial rule and the loss of their land.

  • It is remembered by Palestinians as the "Great Revolt" and a forerunner of later struggles.
  • It is also remembered by Israelis as a period of attacks on Jewish communities, and of the building of Jewish defence forces.
  • The British methods used to crush it — collective punishment, home demolitions and detention without trial — were used again in later conflicts.

What other events are like this?

  • 1919Egypt's 1919 Revolution — When Britain exiled the nationalist leader Saad Zaghlul, Egyptians of every class and religion rose up in strikes and demonstrations — and women marched in public for the first time.
  • 1948Al-Wathbah: The Baghdad Uprising of 1948 — When Iraq's government signed a new treaty with Britain, students, workers and the urban poor of Baghdad rose up — 'the leap' — and forced it to back down.
  • 1952The Mau Mau Uprising — An armed rebellion over land and freedom was met with mass detention and torture. Decades later, the British government paid compensation to survivors.
