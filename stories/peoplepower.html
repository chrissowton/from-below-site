The People Power Revolution

More than two million unarmed Filipinos filled a Manila highway to protect rebel soldiers and end Ferdinand Marcos's twenty-year rule.

When
22 – 25 February 1986
Where
EDSA, Metro Manila, Philippines
Facts checked

01

What is the background to this event?

Ferdinand Marcos was elected President of the Philippines in 1965 and re-elected in 1969 amid allegations of fraud. Barred from a third term, he declared martial law in September 1972. He closed Congress, shut down critical media and ruled by decree.

Under martial law, which formally lasted until 1981, and the rule that followed:

  • Thousands of opponents were imprisoned, tortured or killed. Historians commonly cite figures of around 70,000 imprisoned, 35,000 tortured and over 3,000 killed.
  • Marcos, his wife Imelda and their associates amassed enormous wealth.
  • By 1984–85 the economy was in deep crisis, contracting sharply for two years.

On 21 August 1983, the leading opposition politician, Benigno "Ninoy" Aquino Jr., was shot dead as he stepped off a plane at Manila airport, returning from exile. The assassination outraged Filipinos and brought hundreds of thousands onto the streets. Under pressure, Marcos called a "snap" presidential election for February 1986. Ninoy's widow, Corazon "Cory" Aquino, became the opposition candidate.

03

What happened?

The snap election was held on 7 February 1986. It was marred by widespread reports of violence, vote-buying and tampering.1 On 9 February, thirty computer technicians working for the Commission on Elections (COMELEC) walked out in protest at the deliberate manipulation of the official count.1 The Catholic bishops issued a statement condemning the election as fraudulent. Nevertheless, on 15 February the National Assembly proclaimed Marcos the winner; all fifty opposition members walked out in protest.1

On 22 February, Defence Minister Juan Ponce Enrile and Lieutenant General Fidel Ramos, whose plans for a coup had been discovered, announced that they were withdrawing their support from Marcos and barricaded themselves in two military camps on Epifanio de los Santos Avenue (EDSA) in Manila.1 That evening, Cardinal Jaime Sin appealed over the Catholic station Radio Veritas for people to go to EDSA to protect them.

Over the following three days:1

  • Hundreds of thousands, and eventually more than two million, unarmed civilians filled EDSA.
  • When tanks and marines were sent against the camps, nuns, priests and ordinary people knelt in front of them, praying the rosary and offering flowers and food to the soldiers.
  • Units of the armed forces defected one after another.

On 25 February both Corazon Aquino and Ferdinand Marcos held inauguration ceremonies. That night, Marcos, his family and close associates fled the Malacañang Palace and were flown by the United States to Clark Air Base and then into exile in Hawaii.1

04

What happened next?

  • Democracy restored: Corazon Aquino became President. A new constitution in 1987 limited presidents to a single six-year term and restored civil liberties.
  • Instability: Aquino's government faced several coup attempts, many led by some of the same military officers who had helped bring her to power.
  • Inspiration: the revolution's example inspired democracy movements across Asia and Eastern Europe in the late 1980s.
  • The Marcoses return: the Marcos family returned to the Philippines in the 1990s, and in 2022 Ferdinand "Bongbong" Marcos Jr., the dictator's son, was elected President.
05

What do others say?

At the time

Marcos and his supporters insisted that he had won the election fairly and denounced the rebels as traitors. The United States government was initially reluctant to abandon a long-standing ally; President Reagan at first said there had been fraud "on both sides".

Historians' debates

  • People or soldiers? Some historians argue that the revolution was essentially a military revolt that the crowds protected; others stress that without the crowds, the rebel soldiers would have been crushed.
  • Who benefited? Critics argue that power returned to the country's traditional elite families, and that the poor saw little change; land reform, for example, remained limited.
  • Memory and disinformation: historians and journalists have documented online campaigns since the 2010s that minimised martial-law abuses and presented the Marcos era as a "golden age", contributing to the family's political comeback.

The evidence

  • Television footage and photographs taken by Filipino and foreign journalists.

  • Testimonies of martial-law victims, including those collected by the Human Rights Victims' Claims Board after 2013.

07

How is it remembered?

  • The EDSA Shrine, a church built on the site in 1989, and the People Power Monument mark the revolution.
  • The Bantayog ng mga Bayani in Quezon City honours those who fought against martial law.
  • The anniversary on 25 February was long a public holiday; in 2024 it was left off the list of official holidays, prompting criticism.
08

Why does it matter?

The People Power Revolution showed that a mass, non-violent popular uprising could bring down a dictatorship almost without bloodshed. The phrase "people power" entered languages around the world, and the image of civilians facing down tanks with rosaries and flowers became one of the defining images of the late twentieth century.

  • It restored democracy in the Philippines after fourteen years of authoritarian rule.
  • It inspired later non-violent movements, from South Korea in 1987 to Eastern Europe in 1989.
  • The return of the Marcos family to power shows that the memory of such events can be fragile and contested.

What other events are like this?

  • 1980The Gwangju Uprising — Students and citizens in Gwangju rose against a military coup. Paratroopers killed hundreds, but the uprising became the foundation of South Korean democracy.
  • 1980The Gdańsk Shipyard Strike and Solidarity — A strike over the sacking of a crane operator grew into the first independent trade union in the Soviet bloc, with ten million members.
  • 1977The Mothers of the Plaza de Mayo — The mothers of Argentina's 'disappeared' walked in silent circles in front of the presidential palace, in open defiance of a military dictatorship.
