#ThisFlag

A pastor posted a video with the national flag around his neck, asking what its colours meant when the country was broken. It went viral, and on 6 July 2016 Zimbabweans stayed at home in a national shutdown.

When
April – July 2016
Where

01

What is the background to this event?

Zimbabwe had faced economic crisis since the 1990s, under President Robert Mugabe.1

  • Unemployment and corruption were widespread, and public workers often went unpaid.
02

Who were the key people?

  • Pastor Evan Mawarire

    Who started the movement with an online video.

  • Young Zimbabweans

    Who shared videos and joined the protests.

  • President Robert Mugabe

    Whose government the movement criticised.

03

What happened?

On 19 April 2016, Evan Mawarire posted a video on Facebook reflecting on what Zimbabwe's flag stood for, and how the government was failing it.1

  • The video went viral, especially among young people, and led to a 25-day online campaign.1
  • Citizens posted their own videos criticising corruption and lost hopes.1
  • On 6 July 2016, Mawarire called for a national "stay-away", and many people stayed home from work.1
  • The government accused him of betraying Zimbabwe, and banned wearing the flag around the neck.1
04

What happened next?

  • Arrest and exile: Mawarire was arrested, then released after a court hearing, and spent time abroad.
  • Mugabe's fall: Mugabe was removed by the military in November 2017.
05

Why does it matter?

#ThisFlag showed how social media and a national symbol could be used for peaceful protest under an authoritarian government.

  • Its founder, Evan Mawarire, was arrested, released after a court hearing, and spent time abroad.
  • Mugabe was removed by the military in November 2017.
  • The hashtag is still used by Zimbabweans calling for change.

What do others say?How it was seen at the time, and where historians disagree

At the time

The government called Mawarire a traitor working for foreign interests; supporters saw a patriot.1

Debates since

  • Impact: the movement did not bring down the government, but changed how Zimbabweans protested.
  • Violence: the movement was non-violent; the state arrested and threatened activists.1
