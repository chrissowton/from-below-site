The Mothers of the Plaza de Mayo

The mothers of Argentina's 'disappeared' walked in silent circles in front of the presidential palace, in open defiance of a military dictatorship.

When
From 30 April 1977
Where
Plaza de Mayo, Buenos Aires, Argentina

01

What is the background to this event?

On 24 March 1976 the Argentine armed forces overthrew the government of Isabel Perón and installed a military junta. The regime launched what it called the "National Reorganisation Process", a campaign of state terror against anyone it regarded as subversive.

  • Trade unionists, students, journalists, priests, lawyers and activists were seized by security forces, often in the night, and taken to secret detention centres.
  • Most were tortured and murdered; many were drugged and thrown alive from aircraft into the River Plate or the Atlantic in so-called "death flights".
  • The authorities denied all knowledge of their fate. They became los desaparecidos — "the disappeared". Human rights organisations estimate their number at around 30,000.
  • Babies born to detained women were taken and given to families associated with the regime.

Families who searched for their missing relatives went from police stations to barracks to ministries and churches, and received no answers. Public gatherings were banned.

In their own words

03

What happened?

On 30 April 1977, Azucena Villaflor and about a dozen other mothers walked to the Plaza de Mayo, the main square of Buenos Aires, in front of the Casa Rosada presidential palace.1 Because gatherings were forbidden, the police ordered them to move on. They began instead to walk in pairs, arm in arm, around the square's central monument, the May Pyramid — a walk, not a demonstration.

They returned week after week, and their numbers grew:1

  • They established a regular march every Thursday afternoon, which continues to this day.
  • To recognise one another at a religious pilgrimage to Luján in 1977, they wore white headscarves, originally cloth nappies, later embroidered with the names of their children. The headscarf became the symbol of the movement.
  • On 10 December 1977, International Human Rights Day, they published a newspaper advertisement listing the names of their missing children.1

Days earlier, the regime had struck back. A young naval officer, Alfredo Astiz, had joined the group pretending to be searching for a missing brother. Between 8 and 10 December 1977, acting on his information, security forces abducted twelve people connected with the Mothers, including Azucena Villaflor, Esther Ballestrino, María Ponce and the French nuns Alice Domon and Léonie Duquet.12 They were taken to the ESMA naval school, tortured and killed on a "death flight".

The Mothers continued to march. Hebe de Bonafini later reflected:

"It was the strength of women, of mothers, that kept us going."Hebe de Bonafini1

04

What happened next?

  • The end of the dictatorship: the junta collapsed in 1983 after its defeat in the Falklands/Malvinas War. The Trial of the Juntas in 1985 convicted senior commanders, including General Jorge Videla.
  • Impunity and its reversal: amnesty laws and presidential pardons in the late 1980s and 1990s freed many perpetrators. In 2003 Congress repealed the amnesty laws, and in 2005 the Supreme Court ruled them unconstitutional, allowing trials to resume. Alfredo Astiz was sentenced to life imprisonment in 2011.
  • Finding the dead: in 2005 forensic anthropologists identified the remains of Azucena Villaflor, Esther Ballestrino and María Ponce, which had washed ashore in December 1977. Villaflor's ashes were buried in the Plaza de Mayo.
  • Finding the living: the Grandmothers of the Plaza de Mayo, using pioneering genetic techniques, had restored the identities of 140 grandchildren stolen as babies by July 2025.

In 1986 the Mothers divided into two organisations — the Mothers of the Plaza de Mayo Association and the Founding Line — over questions of strategy, but both have continued to campaign.

05

What do others say?

At the time

The junta dismissed the Mothers as las locas — "the madwomen" — and claimed that the disappeared had gone abroad or joined guerrilla groups. Many Argentinians, fearful or supportive of the regime, kept silent or argued that the military was fighting a "dirty war" against terrorism.

Historians' debates

  • The number of disappeared: the 1984 CONADEP report documented around 8,960 cases; human rights groups estimate around 30,000. The figure became a political issue in the 2010s and 2020s, with some politicians questioning the higher number.
  • "Two demons" theory: some argue that the violence of left-wing guerrillas must be considered alongside state repression; most historians and human rights organisations reject any equivalence between guerrilla violence and systematic state terror.
  • The Mothers' politics: the split of 1986 reflected disagreements about whether to accept compensation and exhumations, and about wider political positions; Hebe de Bonafini's later statements were highly controversial.

How is it remembered?

  • Painted white headscarves on the paving of the Plaza de Mayo mark the route of the Mothers' walk.
  • The former ESMA navy school is now a memorial and became a UNESCO World Heritage Site in 2023.
  • 24 March is a national Day of Remembrance for Truth and Justice.
08

Why does it matter?

The Mothers turned private grief into public, political defiance, at enormous personal risk, at a time when almost no one else in Argentina dared to speak. Their insistence on remembering, and on demanding the truth, helped to bring down a dictatorship and later to bring its leaders to justice.

  • They showed that the identity of "mother", which the regime claimed to honour, could become a powerful basis for resistance.
  • Their white headscarf has become a symbol for human rights movements across Latin America and beyond, including the Thursday protests (Aksi Kamisan) in Indonesia.
  • The work of the Grandmothers contributed to the development of forensic genetics and to the recognition, in international law, of the right to identity.
