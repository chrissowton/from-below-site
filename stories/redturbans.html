The Red Turban Rebellions

Labourers conscripted to repair the Yellow River's banks rose against the Mongol Yuan dynasty, inspired by the promise of a coming Buddha. From their ranks rose a former beggar-monk who founded the Ming dynasty.

When
1351 – 1368
Where
Henan, Anhui and the Yellow River plain, China

See it on the map →

01

What is the background to this event?

China had been ruled by the Mongol Yuan dynasty since the 1270s.

  • In the 1340s, the Yellow River flooded repeatedly, and famine spread. The government failed to cope.1
  • Millenarian teachings of the White Lotus sect spread, promising the coming of the Maitreya Buddha, who would end suffering.1
  • In 1351, the government conscripted 150,000 labourers to rechannel the Yellow River.1
02

Who were the key people?

  • Han Shantong

    A Red Turban leader who proclaimed the coming of the Maitreya; executed in 1351.

  • Liu Futong

    His adviser, who led the northern Red Turbans.

  • Zhu Yuanzhang

    An orphan, beggar and monk who rose through the Red Turbans to found the Ming dynasty.

  • The labourers

    Conscripted workers on the Yellow River.

03

What happened?

Han Shantong and Liu Futong recruited among the conscripted labourers, sparking a wave of rebellion in 1351.1

  • The rebels wore red turbans or headbands, and proclaimed the coming of Maitreya.1
  • Han Shantong was captured and executed, but his son Han Lin'er escaped and was proclaimed emperor of a restored "Song" dynasty.1
  • Red Turban armies spread across northern and central China; in 1358 they took Kaifeng.1
  • The movement split into rival warlord regimes that fought one another.1
  • Zhu Yuanzhang defeated his rivals and, in 1368, drove the Mongols out of Beijing and founded the Ming dynasty.

04

What happened next?

  • The Ming: Zhu Yuanzhang ruled as the Hongwu Emperor, and the Ming dynasty lasted until 1644.
  • Repression: once in power, he banned the White Lotus and ruled harshly.
05

What do others say?

At the time

The Yuan government called the rebels bandits and heretics.1

Debates since

  • Ethnic or social? Some historians see a Han Chinese revolt against Mongol rule; others stress famine and class.2
  • Violence on all sides: the wars between Yuan forces and rebel regimes, and among the rebels, killed vast numbers of people.1
06

The evidence

  • The History of Yuan and History of Ming.
  • Coins issued by rebel regimes.
07

How is it remembered?

  • Zhu Yuanzhang's rise from beggar to emperor became one of China's most famous stories.
08

Why does it matter?

The Red Turbans show how disaster, forced labour and religious hope could combine to bring down an empire.

  • Their story also shows how a revolution from below can produce a new emperor.

Questions to think about

  1. Why did conscripted labourers rebel?
  2. Why was the promise of Maitreya so powerful?
  3. Was this a national revolt against Mongol rule, or a social revolt?
  4. Why did a rebel leader become an authoritarian emperor?
09

What other events are like this?

  • AD 184The Yellow Turban Rebellion — Inspired by a Daoist healer who promised a new age of 'Great Peace', hundreds of thousands of peasants rose against the Han dynasty — a revolt that helped to bring the empire down.
  • AD 18The Red Eyebrows — When the Yellow River flooded and Wang Mang's land reforms failed, starving farmers painted their eyebrows red to tell friend from foe. Their rebellion helped bring down an emperor — and was joined by a mother avenging her son.
  • 209 BCThe Dazexiang Uprising — Nine hundred conscripts held up by floods faced execution for being late. Their leaders asked: 'Are kings, generals and ministers born to their station?' The first great peasant revolt in Chinese history helped bring down the Qin empire.
