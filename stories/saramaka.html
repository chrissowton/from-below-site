The Saramaka Maroons: Freedom a Century Early

Enslaved Africans escaped from Suriname's plantations into the rainforest and fought the Dutch to a standstill. In 1762 — a century before emancipation — the Dutch signed a treaty recognising their freedom.

When

Where

01

What is the background to this event?

Suriname, on the north coast of South America, became a Dutch colony in 1667. Its sugar and coffee plantations depended on enslaved Africans, who suffered some of the cruellest conditions in the Americas.

  • The ancestors of the Saramaka were Africans sold as plantation slaves in Suriname in the late seventeenth and early eighteenth centuries.1
  • Coming from many different African peoples and languages, they escaped into the dense rainforest — individually, in small groups, and sometimes in large collective rebellions.1
  • The word "maroon" (from Spanish cimarrón) was used across the Americas for people who escaped slavery and set up independent communities.1

03

What happened?

For decades, the Saramaka and other maroon groups raided plantations to free other enslaved people and seize tools and weapons, while the Dutch sent military expeditions into the forest to destroy their villages.

  • The maroons built villages in remote areas, grew crops, and organised themselves into matrilineal clans (lo), each descended from an original band of escaped slaves.1
  • They developed their own language, Saramaccan, mixing West and Central African languages with English, Portuguese and other elements.1
  • Unable to defeat them, the Dutch made peace. In 1762, a full century before the general emancipation of slaves in Suriname, the Saramaka won their freedom through a treaty with the Dutch that acknowledged their territorial rights and trading privileges.1

The treaty had a price: like other maroon treaties, it required the Saramaka to stop accepting new runaways and to return them to the colonists.

04

What happened next?

  • Emancipation: slavery in Suriname was abolished only in 1863, with a further ten years of forced "apprenticeship".
  • Threats to the land: in the 1960s, the Afobaka dam flooded many Saramaka villages, and later logging and mining concessions were granted on their lands.
  • Civil war: in the Surinamese civil war of 1986–92, maroon villages were attacked by the army, including the Moiwana massacre of 1986.
  • The 2007 ruling: after the Saramaka authorities brought a case in the 1990s, the Inter-American Court of Human Rights ruled in 2007 that Suriname must recognise their collective land rights.1
05

What do others say?

At the time

The Dutch saw the maroons as dangerous rebels; the maroons saw themselves as free people defending their liberty.

Historians' debates

  • Oral history: Richard Price showed how the Saramaka preserve detailed memories of the "First-Time" — the era of escape and war — and how these can be compared with Dutch archives.
  • The treaty's price: historians debate the treaty clauses that required the return of new runaways, which set maroons against those still enslaved.
  • Violence: maroon raids on plantations killed planters and others, while Dutch expeditions destroyed villages and executed captured maroons, often with terrible cruelty.

07

How is it remembered?

  • The Saramaka still govern themselves through a paramount chief (gaamá) and headmen, a system dating from the treaty.1
  • Knowledge of the First-Time is carefully guarded by elders.
08

Why does it matter?

The Saramaka are one of the most remarkable examples of people who freed themselves from slavery and built a lasting society. Their story challenges the idea that freedom was granted by enslavers.

  • It shows the creativity of maroon communities in building new cultures.
  • Their 2007 victory is a landmark for the land rights of tribal and Indigenous peoples.
