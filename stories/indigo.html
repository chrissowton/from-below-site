The Indigo Revolt

Bengali farmers forced to grow indigo for European planters, on their own land and for almost nothing, refused to plant it. Their revolt, and a play about it, forced the colonial government to act.

When
1859 – 1860
Where
Chaugachha, Nadia, West Bengal, India
Facts checked
2026-11-16

See it on the map →

01

What is the background to this event?

By 1830, there were more than a thousand indigo factories in Bengal, supplying blue dye to Europe.1

  • European planters forced peasants to grow indigo instead of food on their own land.1
  • Planters gave loans (dadon) at high interest that trapped farmers in debt for life.1
  • They paid only about 2.5% of the market price, and destroyed the property of those who refused.1
  • The law favoured the planters, and landlords sided with them.1

03

What happened?

In March 1859, peasants in Chaugachha village near Krishnanagar, Nadia, refused to grow indigo.1

  • The revolt spread to Murshidabad, Birbhum, Burdwan, Pabna, Khulna and Jessore.1
  • Thousands of ryots (tenant farmers) refused advances and refused to plant.1
  • Village headmen often led the peasants, sometimes with former employees of the planters.1
  • Peasants fought off the armed men (lathials) planters sent to force them, burned indigo depots, and attacked planters and landlords.1

04

What happened next?

  • The Indigo Commission: appointed in 1860, it heard that "not a chest of indigo" reached England without human blood on it.1
  • Reform: the commission led to reforms of an exploitative system, and indigo planting in Bengal declined.1
  • Nil Darpan: the play was banned, and James Long was jailed and fined for publishing it.1
05

What do others say?

At the time

Planters called the farmers lawless; the Bengali middle class and press supported them.1

Debates since

  • Against whom? Historians note the farmers targeted planters, not the colonial state; Subhas Bhattacharya argued it began and ended as a struggle against the planters.1
  • Violence on both sides: planters' armed men beat and intimidated farmers; rebels burned depots and some planters were put on public trial and killed.1
06

The evidence

  • The Indigo Commission report and government papers.1
  • The play Nil Darpan (1860).

Primary source: Printed play

What is it?
Printed play
Made by, and when
Dinabandhu Mitra; the play was first published in 1860

The title page of Nil Darpan ('The Mirror of Indigo'), a Bengali play about the suffering of farmers forced to grow indigo for European planters. Its English translation, published in 1861, so angered the planters that they prosecuted the missionary James Long for libel. The play helped turn the indigo farmers' revolt into a public issue.

Questions to ask of this source

  1. Why might a play reach people that a petition or report could not?
  2. Why did an English translation cause such anger among the planters?
  3. Whose voices does a play like this give us — the farmers', or the writer's?
07

How is it remembered?

  • Nil Darpan shaped the growth of Bengali theatre and remains a classic.1
08

Why does it matter?

The Indigo Revolt was one of the most remarkable peasant movements in Indian history.1

  • Its refusal to plant was an early form of the mass non-co-operation later used by Gandhi.1

Questions to think about

  1. Why did growing indigo leave farmers in permanent debt?
  2. Why was refusing to plant such a powerful weapon?
  3. Why did a play become so important?
  4. Was this a revolt against planters or against colonial rule?
09

What other events are like this?

  • 1855The Santal Hul — Santal farmers who had cleared the forests were trapped by moneylenders and landlords backed by colonial courts. Led by four brothers and two sisters, they rose in their tens of thousands.
  • 1817The Paika Rebellion — Odisha's peasant militia had lost their land and status under East India Company rule. In 1817 they rose, sacking Company offices and spreading revolt across much of Odisha.
  • 1930The Salt March — A 24-day march to the sea to make salt in defiance of British law set off a campaign of civil disobedience across India.
