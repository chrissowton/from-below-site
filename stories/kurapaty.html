Kurapaty and the Belarusian Memory Movement

In 1988, a historian revealed mass graves of Stalin's victims in a forest near Minsk. The discovery sparked Belarus's democracy movement, and young people later camped through a winter to stop a road being built over the graves.

When
1988 – 2002
Where
Kurapaty forest, Minsk, Belarus

See it on the map →

01

What is the background to this event?

Between 1937 and 1941, the Soviet secret police (NKVD) shot large numbers of people in the Kurapaty forest on the edge of Minsk.1

  • Estimates of the dead range from 7,000 to over 250,000; the NKVD archives in Belarus remain closed.1
  • For decades, the killings were hidden.
02

Who were the key people?

  • Zianon Pazniak

    Historian who discovered the graves and led the Belarusian Popular Front.

  • Villagers near Kurapaty

    55 of whom gave testimony about the NKVD trucks and executions.

  • Young activists of the Belarusian Popular Front and Zubr

    Who occupied the site in 2001–02.

03

What happened?

In 1988, the historian Zianon Pazniak revealed the mass graves, and the remains were exhumed.1

  • Investigations, based on confessions by former NKVD members and villagers' testimony, confirmed that the NKVD was responsible.1
  • The discovery gave momentum to the movement for democracy and independence in the last years of the Soviet Union.1
  • In October 1988, a commemorative march to Kurapaty was broken up by police with tear gas.
  • In 2001, when a road-widening scheme threatened the site, young activists occupied it and stayed through a bitter winter.1

Sources

  1. Wikipedia, 'Kurapaty', on the executions, the estimates, the discovery, the investigations and the occupation of 2001–02. Link ↗
04

What happened next?

  • Independence: Belarus became independent in 1991.
  • Heritage: in 2004, the graves were listed as cultural heritage of the highest category.1
  • Continuing struggle: under Alexander Lukashenko, the memory of Kurapaty remains contested, and memorial crosses have been removed by the authorities.
05

What do others say?

At the time

Some Soviet officials claimed the Nazis had carried out the killings; investigations confirmed the NKVD was responsible.1

Debates since

  • Numbers: estimates vary enormously because the archives remain closed.1
  • Violence: the memory movement was peaceful; the violence was the state's, both in the 1930s and in the policing of commemorations.1
06

The evidence

  • Exhumations and testimony from local villagers.1
07

How is it remembered?

  • Belarusians gather at Kurapaty each year on the Day of Remembrance (Dziady) in late October or early November.
08

Why does it matter?

Kurapaty shows how uncovering hidden crimes can spark a movement for truth and democracy.

Questions to think about

  1. Why had the Kurapaty graves been hidden for so long?
  2. Why did the discovery fuel a democracy movement?
  3. Why do estimates of the number of victims vary so much?
  4. Why is the memory of Kurapaty still contested today?

  • 2020The Belarusian Protests of 2020 — When Alexander Lukashenko claimed 80 per cent of the vote after 26 years in power, Belarusians came out in the largest protests in their history. Three women led the challenge. Tens of thousands were arrested.

  • 1977The Mothers of the Plaza de Mayo — The mothers of Argentina's 'disappeared' walked in silent circles in front of the presidential palace, in open defiance of a military dictatorship.
