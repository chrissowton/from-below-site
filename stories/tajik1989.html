Tajik, Rastokhez and the Dushanbe Protests

In 1989, after campaigns by writers and scholars, Tajik became the state language of Soviet Tajikistan. A new movement, Rastokhez ('Revival'), called for democracy. But in February 1990 protests in Dushanbe turned into riots, and the movement was banned.

01

What is the background to this event?

Tajikistan was one of the republics of the Soviet Union, and one of its poorest.

  • Until 1989, Russian was the only official language; Tajik had the lower status of 'national language'.2
  • Under Mikhail Gorbachev's reforms, people in many Soviet republics campaigned for their languages and cultures.
  • Tajik is closely related to Persian, and Tajik nationalism grew in the late 1980s.1

03

What happened?

On 22 July 1989, a law made Tajik the state language.2

  • The law officially linked Tajik with Persian, adding the word 'Farsi' after 'Tajik', and called for the gradual return of the Perso-Arabic alphabet.1
  • On 14 September 1989, members of the Tajik intelligentsia founded Rastokhez, a moderate nationalist, secular and liberal democratic movement.3
  • In February 1990, a false rumour spread that thousands of Armenian refugees were being given scarce new housing in Dushanbe.4
  • Protesters demanded radical economic and political reform. The protests turned violent: government buildings and shops were attacked, and Armenians, Russians and other minorities were targeted.4
  • Soviet troops put down the riots. In a few days, 26 people were killed and 565 injured.4

04

What happened next?

  • Blame: Rastokhez was made the main scapegoat for the riots and banned, which helped the Communists win the elections.3
  • Independence: Tajikistan declared independence on 9 December 1991, as the Soviet Union collapsed.4
  • Civil war: a civil war began in 1992. Rastokhez, by then underground, joined the United Tajik Opposition with democratic and Islamic groups until the peace agreement of 1997.3
  • Language: in 1999 the word 'Farsi' was removed from the language law.1
05

What do others say?

At the time

The Communist authorities blamed Rastokhez and nationalists for the riots; the protesters blamed the government's failure to reform.34

Debates since

  • Who was responsible? Historians disagree about how far the violence was organised, and by whom; Rastokhez's role is still disputed.3
  • Violence on all sides: rioters attacked ethnic minorities and abused Tajik women who wore European clothes. Soviet troops used force to end the riots, and a deputy prime minister criticised the party leader's reliance on the army.4

How is it remembered?

  • Rastokhez ceased to exist after the 1997 peace agreement; most of its members joined the Democratic Party.3
08

Why does it matter?

The Tajik language law was part of a wave of language movements across the Soviet Union in the late 1980s. The events of 1990 also show how a reform movement can be overtaken by rumour and violence — and then blamed for it.
