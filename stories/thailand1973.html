The Thai Student Uprising of 14 October 1973

When thirteen activists were arrested for handing out leaflets calling for a constitution, half a million people filled the streets of Bangkok and brought down a military dictatorship.

When
6 – 15 October 1973
Where
Bangkok, Thailand
Facts checked
2026-11-16

01

What is the background to this event?

Thailand had been ruled by military governments almost continuously since 1947. Field Marshal Thanom Kittikachorn became prime minister in 1963, ruling with his deputy, Praphas Charusathien. In 1971 Thanom staged a coup against his own government, abolished the constitution and dissolved parliament.1

  • The number of university students grew rapidly: from 15,000 in 1961 to 150,000 in 1972.1
  • Students who worked in rural development programmes during their holidays saw the poverty of the countryside for themselves.1
  • The National Student Center of Thailand (NSCT), founded in 1968, became more political after 1972 under its secretary-general, Thirayuth Boonmee, campaigning against Japanese goods, corruption and the dictatorship.1
  • In 1973, students exposed a scandal in which military officers and businessmen used army helicopters to hunt in a wildlife reserve.1
02

Who were the key people?

  • Thirayuth Boonmee

    An engineering student and NSCT leader, one of the thirteen arrested on 6 October 1973.

  • Seksan Prasertkul

    A Thammasat University student leader who led the march on 13–14 October. After the 1976 massacre he joined the communist insurgency in the jungle.

  • Field Marshal Thanom Kittikachorn

    The prime minister and military ruler, who fled the country on 15 October 1973.

  • Praphas Charusathien and Narong Kittikachorn

    Thanom's deputy and his son — together the 'three tyrants' in the eyes of the protesters.

  • King Bhumibol Adulyadej

    The king, whose intervention on 14 October ended the crisis.

  • The 'Thirteen Rebels'

    The activists arrested for distributing leaflets calling for a constitution.

03

What happened?

On 6 October 1973, Thirayuth Boonmee and others were arrested while handing out leaflets in Bangkok calling for a permanent constitution. Thirteen people were held and denied bail, and the deputy prime minister, Praphas, accused them of plotting to overthrow the government.1 The arrests provoked a growing wave of protest:1

  • On 9 October, more than 2,000 students demonstrated at Thammasat University; by 11 October the rally had grown to 50,000.
  • On 12 October, the government offered to release the thirteen on bail; the students demanded their unconditional release.
  • On 13 October, a crowd of more than 400,000, including many members of the public, marched to the Democracy Monument. The government released the prisoners and promised a constitution within a year.

Around 200,000 people refused to disperse, and the student leader Seksan Prasertkul led them towards the royal palace. Early on 14 October, as the crowds were leaving at the king's request, violence broke out between police and students near the palace.1 The government brought in tanks, helicopters and soldiers. Seventy-seven people were killed and 857 injured, and buildings along Ratchadamnoen Avenue were set on fire. The number of protesters swelled to more than 500,000.1

In the evening, the soldiers withdrew. At about 7.15 p.m., the king announced on radio and television that Thanom's government had resigned and appointed Sanya Dharmasakti, the rector of Thammasat University, as prime minister. On 15 October, as violence continued, it was announced that Thanom, Praphas and Narong had left the country.1

04

What happened next?

  • A democratic opening: a new constitution was adopted in 1974, and free elections were held in 1975. Students, farmers and workers organised as never before, and the number of strikes rose sharply.
  • Backlash: right-wing groups, backed by parts of the army and police, attacked activists; dozens of farmers' leaders were assassinated.
  • The 1976 massacre: when Thanom returned to Thailand as a monk in September 1976, students protested. On 6 October 1976, police and right-wing paramilitaries attacked Thammasat University, killing at least forty-six people, and the army seized power that evening.
  • A recurring pattern: Thailand has since experienced further popular uprisings and military coups, including in 1992, 2006 and 2014.
05

What do others say?

At the time

The government accused the students of being communists plotting to overthrow the state. The students presented themselves as patriotic and loyal to the king, demanding only a constitution.

Historians' debates

  • The role of the king: the king's intervention ended the crisis and greatly increased the prestige of the monarchy. Historians debate whether this was a turning point that made the palace the decisive arbiter of Thai politics.
  • Who won? Some historians argue that the uprising also reflected divisions within the military, some of whose leaders were happy to see Thanom and Praphas go.
  • 1973 and 1976: 14 October has long been celebrated, while the 1976 massacre was long silenced. Thongchai Winichakul, himself a student leader in 1976, has written about this "unforgetting".

07

How is it remembered?

  • The 14 October 1973 Memorial, on Ratchadamnoen Avenue near the Democracy Monument, was completed in 2001.
  • The anniversary is marked each year in Bangkok.
  • The Democracy Monument has remained the gathering place for later protest movements, including the youth-led protests of 2020.
08

Why does it matter?

The uprising of 14 October 1973 was the first time in Thailand's history that a mass popular movement brought down a government. Students, with the support of hundreds of thousands of ordinary people, ended a decade of military dictatorship and opened a brief period of democracy.

  • It made students a major force in Thai politics.
  • The democratic years that followed saw an explosion of organising by farmers and workers.
  • Its violent reversal in 1976 shows how fragile democratic gains can be.

Questions to think about

  2. What role did the king play, and how might it have shaped Thai politics afterwards?
  3. Why did the democratic period after 1973 end in violence in 1976?

What other events are like this?

  • 1988The 8888 Uprising — Students, monks, workers and civil servants rose against a quarter-century of military rule. On 8.8.88 the whole country went on strike — until the army struck back.
  • 1980The Gwangju Uprising — Students and citizens in Gwangju rose against a military coup. Paratroopers killed hundreds, but the uprising became the foundation of South Korean democracy.
  • 1986The People Power Revolution — More than two million unarmed Filipinos filled a Manila highway to protect rebel soldiers and end Ferdinand Marcos's twenty-year rule.
