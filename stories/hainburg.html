The Occupation of the Hainburger Au

Thousands of Austrians camped in a frozen Danube wetland to stop a hydroelectric dam. The police beat them with batons, the public turned against the government, and the wetland became a national park.

When
December 1984
Where
Stopfenreuth, near Hainburg, Austria

See it on the map →

01

What is the background to this event?

The Hainburger Au is a large natural floodplain on the Danube east of Vienna.1

  • In 1983, the government and the regional power company planned a hydroelectric power plant that would flood much of it.1
  • The WWF began a campaign to save the wetlands.1
  • The Nobel Prize-winning scientist Konrad Lorenz backed a petition to protect the area as a national park.1
02

Who were the key people?

  • Günther Nenning

    Journalist who helped lead the campaign.

  • Gerhard Heilingbrunner

    Student union leader who co-led the campaign.

  • Konrad Lorenz

    Scientist who supported the campaign.

  • Chancellor Fred Sinowatz

    Who declared a 'Christmas peace'.

03

What happened?

Site clearance began in December 1984.1

  • On 8 December, about 8,000 people joined a demonstration, and several hundred stayed in the wetlands, stopping the work.1
  • On 19 December, 800 police with batons cleared part of the area from about 3,000 occupiers; 19 people were officially injured.1
  • The violence caused public outrage.1
  • On 22 December, the chancellor announced a "Christmas peace", and thousands spent Christmas in the wetlands; a priest held Christmas Mass there.1

04

What happened next?

  • Halt: in January 1985, the courts stopped further clearance work, and the dam was never built.1
  • National park: since 1996, the area has been part of the Donau-Auen National Park.1
05

What do others say?

At the time

The government and unions argued the dam was needed for energy and jobs; protesters argued for nature and democracy.1

Debates since

  • Green politics: the occupation helped give rise to Austria's Green Party.
  • Violence: the protesters were non-violent; police used batons against them.1
06

The evidence

  • Press coverage, including in the mass-market Kronen Zeitung.1
07

How is it remembered?

  • The Donau-Auen National Park itself is the movement's memorial.
08

Why does it matter?

Hainburg was a turning point for environmental awareness in central Europe, and changed how Austrians thought about democracy and energy.1

Questions to think about

  1. Why did people occupy the wetlands in winter?
  2. Why did police violence turn opinion against the government?
  3. How did the occupation change Austrian politics?
  4. When is occupying land a legitimate form of protest?

  • 1971Gardarem lo Larzac: Farmers against the Army — When the French army announced it would take their land for a military camp, 103 sheep farmers swore an oath not to sell. Ten years of non-violent resistance followed — and they won.
  • 1971The Green Bans — Construction workers refused to build. At the request of residents, the Builders Labourers Federation banned work on 54 projects that would have destroyed bushland, historic buildings and working-class homes — and saved much of old Sydney.
