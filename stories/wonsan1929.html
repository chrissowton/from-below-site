Workers & unions

The Wonsan General Strike

When a foreign oil company broke its promises, the dockers and workers of Wonsan stopped the whole port. For nearly three months, 2,200 workers in 24 unions held out against the company, the employers and the Japanese colonial police — the largest labour dispute in Korea under Japanese rule.

When
13 January – 6 April 1929
Where
Wonsan, Kangwon, North Korea
Facts checked

01

What is the background to this event?

Korea was ruled by Japan from 1910 to 1945, and Wonsan was an important port on its east coast.1

  • In September 1928, at the Rising Sun Petroleum Company's oil depot near Wonsan (a British-owned firm), a Japanese supervisor assaulted and mistreated Korean workers.1
  • The workers struck, demanding his dismissal, and transport workers joined them in sympathy.1
  • On 28 September the company accepted their demands and promised to improve conditions within three months.1
  • In December the company broke this promise and refused to recognise any union.1

03

What happened?

On 13 January 1929, the Wonsan Labour Federation launched a strike.1

  • The workers demanded a minimum wage, an eight-hour day, the dismissal of the supervisors and the right to bargain collectively.1
  • About 300 workers at the oil depot struck first. The company sacked them and brought in Chinese labourers to break the strike.1
  • The Chamber of Commerce refused to hire federation members from 21 January. The next day the federation called a general strike.1
  • In all, 2,200 workers in 24 unions joined, and transport and the port came to a complete standstill.1
  • Solidarity strikes took place in Seoul (then Keijo), Pyongyang, Busan, Sinpo and Kobe in Japan.1
04

What happened next?

  • Repression: the Japanese police brought in firefighters, reservists and about 300 soldiers from an infantry unit in Hamhung to suppress the union.1
  • Wider support: Korean farmers, students and business people backed the strikers, as did unions in Japan, China, the Soviet Union and France. Strike funds were estimated at more than 30,000 yen.1
  • The end: on 6 April 1929, the workers agreed to return to work unconditionally.1
05

What do others say?

At the time

The Korean press, such as The Chosun Ilbo, reported the strike closely; the employers saw the federation as a threat to be broken.1

Debates since

  • Labour or national struggle? Historians discuss how far the strike was a dispute over wages and conditions, and how far it was resistance to Japanese rule, since the supervisors and police were Japanese.
06

The evidence

  • Reports in Korean newspapers of 1929, such as The Chosun Ilbo.1
  • Japanese police and colonial records, which call it the 'Gensan dispute'.1

The Chosun Ilbo, Seoul, 1 February 1929

A Korean-language newspaper reports on the general strike in Wonsan, three weeks after it began. Korean newspapers were allowed under Japanese rule, but they were censored and could be suspended.

07

How is it remembered?

  • The strike is remembered as the largest labour dispute of the colonial period in Korea.1
08

Why does it matter?

Although the workers went back without winning their demands, the Wonsan strike showed that Korean workers could organise on a huge scale under colonial rule. It is seen as a turning point for the Korean labour movement of the late 1920s and 1930s.1

Open questions

  1. Why did the dispute at one oil depot become a general strike?
  2. Why did the employers bring in workers from China?
  3. Why might the strike have been about colonial rule as well as wages?
  4. Was the strike a failure, since the workers went back without winning?
09

What other events are like this?

  • 1925The Canton–Hong Kong Strike — After British police shot protesters in Shanghai, a quarter of a million workers left Hong Kong and boycotted British goods. The colony became a ghost town.
  • 1919The March First Movement — Koreans read out a declaration of independence and poured into the streets in peaceful protests against Japanese rule. Up to two million people took part, and thousands were killed.
  • 1913The Dublin Lockout — When 300 employers locked out some 20,000 workers to destroy a new union of the unskilled, Dublin's poorest families held out for five months, fed by food ships from British workers.
