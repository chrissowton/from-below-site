Lebanon's 17 October Revolution

A tax on WhatsApp calls sparked a nationwide uprising against a political system built on sectarian division. 'All of them means all of them,' protesters chanted — demanding the removal of the entire political class.

When
October 2019 – 2020
Where
Martyrs' Square, Beirut, Tripoli and across Lebanon
Facts checked

01

What is the background to this event?

Since the end of the civil war in 1990, Lebanon's government has been shared out between religious communities under the Taif Agreement. Many leaders were former civil war commanders.1

  • Corruption was widespread, and the state failed to provide reliable electricity, water and rubbish collection.1
  • Unemployment was high, and the economy was in deep trouble.1
  • On 17 October 2019, the government announced new taxes, including on petrol, tobacco and calls made through apps such as WhatsApp.1

In their own words

03

What happened?

On the night of 17 October, protests broke out across Lebanon. The government quickly withdrew the WhatsApp tax, but the protests grew.1

  • Protesters filled Martyrs' Square and Riad al-Solh Square in Beirut, and squares in Tripoli, Nabatieh, Tyre and elsewhere.1
  • They demanded the removal of the whole political class, an end to sectarian politics and a government of independent experts.1
  • Protesters held concerts, debates and community kitchens, and on 27 October formed a human chain across the country.
  • Supporters of Hezbollah and the Amal Movement attacked protest camps in Beirut and the south.1
  • On 29 October, Prime Minister Saad Hariri resigned.1
  • Banks closed and then restricted withdrawals; anger at the banks grew, and some protesters attacked bank branches.

04

What happened next?

  • New government: a cabinet led by Hassan Diab took office in January 2020.1
  • Collapse: Lebanon's currency lost most of its value, and much of the population fell into poverty.
  • The port explosion: on 4 August 2020, a huge explosion of stored ammonium nitrate at Beirut port killed more than 200 people. Diab's government resigned.1
  • Elections: in 2022, candidates from the protest movement won several seats in parliament.
05

What do others say?

At the time

Hezbollah's leader argued that the government should not resign and suggested foreign powers were exploiting the protests; protesters said the movement was home-grown.1

Debates since

  • Violence: the protests were mainly peaceful; some protesters were killed, including by gunfire at protests, and party supporters attacked protest camps. Security forces used tear gas and rubber bullets, and some protesters attacked banks and security forces.12
  • Success or failure? The movement brought down a government but not the system, and the economy collapsed.

07

How is it remembered?

  • Activists mark 17 October each year; the movement is remembered in songs, art and graffiti.
08

Why does it matter?

The uprising was the largest cross-sectarian movement in Lebanon's history, challenging a system that divides citizens by religion.

  • It showed new forms of protest, including community kitchens and public debates.
  • Its failure to change the system raises questions about how entrenched elites can be removed.

What other events are like this?

  • 1956The Galle Face Satyagraha and the 'Sinhala Only' Act — When Ceylon's parliament made Sinhala the only official language, Tamil leaders sat in peaceful protest outside. A mob attacked them — and the violence that followed was the first step on the road to civil war.
  • 2014The Bosnian Spring and the Plenums — Workers whose factories had been stripped and closed set government buildings on fire. Then citizens gathered in open assemblies — the plenums — to decide their demands together, across ethnic lines.
  • 2010The Tunisian Revolution — A street vendor's desperate protest in a small town sparked a month of uprising that toppled a dictator of 23 years and began the Arab Spring.
