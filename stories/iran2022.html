Woman, Life, Freedom

The death in custody of a young Kurdish woman arrested for 'improper' hijab sparked the largest protests in Iran in decades, led by women and girls under the slogan 'Woman, Life, Freedom'.

When
September 2022 – 2023
Where
Saqqez, Tehran and across Iran

See it on the map →

01

What is the background to this event?

After the 1979 revolution, the Islamic Republic of Iran made the hijab compulsory for women in public, and rolled back many legal rights that women had gained. Yet over the following decades Iranian women became highly educated, and many came to resent restrictions on their dress, work and lives.1

  • The "Guidance Patrol", known as the morality police, enforced the dress code, detaining women it considered improperly dressed.
  • Iran had seen several waves of protest, including the Green Movement of 2009 and the protests of November 2019, when more than 1,500 people were reported killed.1
  • Kurds, who make up around 15 per cent of the population, and other minorities such as the Baluch, faced discrimination and poverty.1
02

Who were the key people?

  • Mahsa (Jina) Amini

    A 22-year-old Kurdish woman from Saqqez, arrested in Tehran on 13 September 2022 for allegedly wearing her hijab improperly. She died in custody on 16 September.

  • Iranian women and girls

    Who led the protests, removing and burning headscarves and cutting their hair in public; schoolgirls demonstrated in large numbers for the first time.

  • Protesters across Iran

    Students, workers, shopkeepers and people from many ethnic groups, in more than a hundred cities.

  • The security forces

    Police, the Basij militia and the Revolutionary Guards, who suppressed the protests.

  • Narges Mohammadi

    An imprisoned Iranian human rights activist awarded the Nobel Peace Prize in 2023.

In their own words

03

What happened?

Mahsa Amini was arrested by the Guidance Patrol on 13 September 2022 while visiting Tehran from Saqqez. She collapsed in custody and was pronounced dead on 16 September. Her family and witnesses said she had been beaten; the authorities denied this.1

Protests began in Tehran while she was in hospital, and spread from her funeral in Saqqez to Kurdish cities and across Iran:1

  • Women removed and burned their headscarves and cut their hair in public. The signature slogan was "Woman, Life, Freedom" — in Kurdish, Jin, Jiyan, Azadî — which had its roots in the Kurdish women's movement.
  • Students, workers, shopkeepers and schoolgirls joined in. The protests spread across social classes and regions; many came to call for the end of the Islamic Republic.
  • The government responded with internet blackouts, mass arrests and live fire. In Zahedan, in the Baluch region, dozens of people were killed on a single day, 30 September 2022, which became known as "Bloody Friday".

Human rights groups reported that at least 551 people, including 68 children, had been killed by the security forces by September 2023. An estimated 19,000–20,000 people were arrested.1 The government also reported that dozens of members of the security forces were killed. After trials widely condemned as unfair, several protesters were executed, beginning in December 2022.2

04

What happened next?

  • Suppression: by 2023 the street protests had largely been suppressed, but many women continued to defy the hijab law in public.
  • Executions: several protesters were executed after trials condemned by human rights organisations, and many others received long prison sentences.
  • International recognition: in 2023 the European Parliament awarded its Sakharov Prize to Mahsa Amini and the Woman, Life, Freedom movement, and the Nobel Peace Prize was awarded to the imprisoned activist Narges Mohammadi.
  • A UN finding: in 2024, a UN fact-finding mission concluded that the state's repression of the protests involved serious human rights violations, some amounting to crimes against humanity.
05

What do others say?

At the time

The Iranian government described the protests as "riots" instigated by the United States, Israel and other foreign enemies, and pointed to attacks on security forces and public property. Protesters, human rights organisations and the UN described an overwhelmingly peaceful movement met with lethal force.

Commentators' debates

  • Violence: the great majority of those killed were protesters and bystanders, many shot by security forces. There were also incidents in which members of the security forces were killed; the government used these to justify executions, though trials were widely criticised as unfair and confessions as coerced.
  • Reform or revolution? Commentators debate whether the movement sought reform of the hijab law or the overthrow of the Islamic Republic, and whether its lack of leadership was a strength or a weakness.
  • The diaspora: diaspora communities organised huge solidarity protests, but some in Iran felt that exiled voices claimed too much of the movement.
06

The evidence

  • Videos and photographs shared on social media by protesters, despite internet shutdowns.
  • Reports by Amnesty International, Human Rights Watch, Iran Human Rights and the UN fact-finding mission.
  • Testimonies of detainees and families of victims.
07

How is it remembered?

  • The anniversary of Mahsa Amini's death, 16 September, is marked by Iranians around the world.
  • The slogan "Woman, Life, Freedom" has become a global symbol of women's rights.
  • Songs written for the movement, such as Shervin Hajipour's "Baraye" ("For"), became anthems; Hajipour was later sentenced to prison.
08

Why does it matter?

The Woman, Life, Freedom movement was the largest challenge to the Islamic Republic in decades, and one of the most important women-led movements of the twenty-first century. It placed women's bodily freedom at the centre of a demand for wider political freedom.

  • It united people across ethnic groups, classes and generations, and gave a central place to Kurdish and Baluch grievances.
  • Its slogan has been taken up around the world.
  • The everyday defiance of the hijab law that continued afterwards has changed public life in Iran.

Questions to think about

  1. Why do you think the death of one young woman sparked such widespread protests?
  2. What does the slogan 'Woman, Life, Freedom' mean, and why did it become so powerful?
  3. How should we weigh the government's claims about violence against the evidence gathered by human rights organisations?
  4. Can a movement that is suppressed on the streets still change a society?
