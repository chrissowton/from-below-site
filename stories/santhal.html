The Santal Hul

Santal farmers who had cleared the forests were trapped by moneylenders and landlords backed by colonial courts. Led by four brothers and two sisters, they rose in their tens of thousands.

When
30 June 1855 – January 1856
Where
Bhognadih, Santal Parganas, Jharkhand, India
Facts checked
2026-11-16

See it on the map →

01

What is the background to this event?

From 1832, the East India Company encouraged Santal people to settle and clear the forests of the Damin-i-koh, in what is now Jharkhand.1

  • The Santal population there grew from 3,000 in 1838 to over 82,000 in 1851, and Company revenue rose 22-fold.1
  • Moneylenders (mahajans) and landlords (zamindars), backed by the Company's courts and police, came to dominate the area.1
  • Santals were lent money at huge rates of interest. When they could not pay, they lost their land and were forced into bonded labour.1
  • Their petitions were ignored.1

In their own words

03

What happened?

On 30 June 1855, thousands of Santals gathered at Bhognadih and began the Hul (rebellion), led by the Murmu siblings.1

  • They aimed to end the Company's revenue system, moneylending and the zamindari system, and to govern themselves.1
  • By July, tens of thousands of Santals were in arms, attacking moneylenders, landlords, police posts and railway buildings.1
  • Other local peoples, such as Kamars and Bagdis, joined them.1
  • The Company declared martial law in November 1855, and its armies crushed the rebellion by January 1856.1
04

What happened next?

  • Death toll: over 15,000 people were killed, and many villages were destroyed.1
  • Reform: the Santal Parganas Act of 1855 created a separate district with its own administration.1
  • Land: later laws restricted the sale of Santal land to outsiders.
05

Why does it matter?

The Santal Hul was the most significant armed resistance to Company rule before the rebellion of 1857.1

  • It was a rising against debt, land loss and colonial law.
  • Over 15,000 people were killed, and many villages were destroyed.1
  • It led to a separate district with its own administration, and later laws restricted the sale of Santal land to outsiders.1
  • 30 June is still commemorated as Hul Diwas in Jharkhand.1

What do others say?How it was seen at the time, and where historians disagree

At the time

The British press presented the Santals as savages; the Santals saw themselves as fighting for justice and self-rule.1

Debates since

  • Religion and politics: the rebellion combined millenarian beliefs with demands for land and justice.13
  • Violence on both sides: rebels killed moneylenders, landlords and others; Company troops destroyed villages and killed thousands of Santals.1

Reading it

This picture was made for readers in Britain, thousands of miles away. The soldiers are active; the Santals are hidden, hunted figures. It tells its readers nothing about why the Santals rose — debt, land loss and colonial law.
