The Accra Riots of 1948

Unarmed Gold Coast veterans of the Second World War marched to petition the governor for the pensions they had been promised. A British police officer shot three of them dead — and the road to Ghana's independence opened.

When
28 February 1948
Where
Christiansborg crossroads, Accra, Ghana
Facts checked
2026-11-15

See it on the map →

01

What is the background to this event?

Soldiers from the Gold Coast (now Ghana) had fought for Britain in the Second World War, including in Burma.1

  • They had been promised pensions and jobs, but when they returned there was little work and their pensions were not paid.1
  • In January 1948, a Ga chief, Nii Kwabena Bonne III, organised a boycott of European imports in protest at high prices.1
02

Who were the key people?

  • Sergeant Nii Adjetey, Corporal Patrick Attipoe and Private Odartey Lamptey

    Veterans shot dead on 28 February.

  • Nii Kwabena Bonne III

    Chief who led the boycott.

  • Kwame Nkrumah

    One of the 'Big Six' nationalist leaders arrested after the riots.

  • Colin Imray

    British police superintendent who opened fire.

In their own words

03

What happened?

On 28 February 1948, ex-servicemen marched peacefully towards the governor's residence at Christiansborg Castle to present a petition.1

  • Police stopped them. The superintendent, Colin Imray, ordered his men to shoot; when they did not, he fired himself, killing three veterans.1
  • People in Accra rioted; for five days, European- and Asian-owned shops were looted, and more people died.1
  • The governor declared a state of emergency and imposed press censorship.1
  • The "Big Six" leaders of the United Gold Coast Convention, including Kwame Nkrumah, were arrested.1
04

What happened next?

  • Inquiry: the Watson Commission investigated and recommended constitutional change.1
  • Independence: the Gold Coast became Ghana on 6 March 1957, the first sub-Saharan African colony to win independence.1
05

Why does it matter?

The killing of the veterans is seen as the turning point on the road to Ghana's independence.1

  • It showed the injustice of a colonial system that used Africans in war but abandoned them afterwards.
  • An official commission investigated and recommended constitutional change.1
  • The Gold Coast became Ghana in 1957, the first sub-Saharan African colony to win independence.1

What do others say?How it was seen at the time, and where historians disagree

At the time

The colonial government blamed nationalist agitators; Africans saw a massacre of loyal soldiers.1

Debates since

  • A turning point: historians see 1948 as the moment Britain began to accept the end of colonial rule in the Gold Coast.1
  • Violence on both sides: the veterans were unarmed; rioters later looted shops, and further deaths occurred during the unrest.1

Looting in Accra's commercial centre after a police superintendent fired on ex-servicemen marching to the governor's residence, killing three. Newspapers chose what to show of the riots — here, the looting rather than the shooting.

Reading it

Newspapers chose what to show of the riots, and this picture shows looting in Accra's commercial centre rather than the shooting of unarmed veterans that set it off. That choice shapes the story. For five days afterwards, European- and Asian-owned shops were looted — following a boycott of European imports earlier that year over high prices.

How is it remembered?Memorials, anniversaries and arguments over memory
