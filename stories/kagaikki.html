The Kaga Rebellion: 'A Province Held by Peasants'

Peasants, monks and local warriors united by their Buddhist faith overthrew the governor of Kaga and governed the province themselves for almost a century.

When
1488 – 1580
Where
Kaga Province (Ishikawa), Japan
Facts checked
2026-11-16

See it on the map →

01

What is the background to this event?

In fifteenth-century Japan, the authority of the Ashikaga shoguns was collapsing. Provinces were ruled by military governors (shugo), who fought each other for land and power, and the devastating Ōnin War (1467–1477) plunged the country into a century of civil war.1

  • Peasant leagues known as ikki ("of one mind") became increasingly common. Villagers swore oaths to act together, refused taxes and debts, and sometimes rose in arms. The first great rising, in 1428, marched on the capital, Kyoto.2
  • The Jōdo Shinshū ("True Pure Land") school of Buddhism, which taught that salvation came through faith in the Buddha Amida alone, spread rapidly among peasants, merchants and minor warriors. It was led by the abbots of the Hongan-ji temple.2
  • From 1471, the abbot Rennyo preached at Yoshizaki, on the border of Kaga and Echizen provinces, and won a huge following through his letters and network of temples.2

Leagues formed by Jōdo Shinshū believers became known as Ikkō-ikki. In 1473 they helped Togashi Masachika win control of Kaga in a family war — in return for promises that he would lift them out of poverty. When he failed to keep his promises, they turned against him.1

03

What happened?

Through the 1470s and 1480s, the Ikkō-ikki in Kaga grew bolder, refusing to pay taxes and seizing tax revenue and land, despite Rennyo's protests.1 An earlier rising in 1474 was defeated, and its participants fled to neighbouring Etchū.1

In 1487, Togashi Masachika left Kaga with a large army to support the shogun's campaign in Ōmi Province. In his absence, the Ikkō-ikki — led by three of Rennyo's sons — rose in revolt:1

  • Between one hundred thousand and two hundred thousand members took up arms.
  • Masachika hurried back and won several battles, but a number of his own vassal families, discontented with his rule, joined the rebels.
  • The rebels cut him off from reinforcements from the neighbouring provinces and besieged his castle at Takao. In 1488, trapped in his burning castle, Masachika took his own life.

The rebels installed Masachika's uncle, Togashi Yasutaka, as a figurehead governor, but real power passed to the league and its temples.1 It was the first time in Japanese history that a group of commoners had overthrown a provincial ruler and taken control of a province.2 A contemporary chronicle described Kaga as a province "held by peasants" (hyakushō no motaru kuni).3

Over the following decades the league tightened its hold. After a civil war among rival factions in 1531, the office of governor was abolished altogether. The Ikkō-ikki governed Kaga through assemblies of temples, local warriors and village leagues until 1580.1

04

What happened next?

  • Growing power: the Hongan-ji became one of the great powers of sixteenth-century Japan, with a fortified headquarters at Ishiyama Hongan-ji, where Osaka now stands, and leagues in many provinces.
  • War with Nobunaga: the warlord Oda Nobunaga, who was trying to unify Japan, saw the Ikkō-ikki as a major obstacle. His forces massacred tens of thousands of league members at Nagashima in 1574 and in Echizen in 1575.
  • The end: in 1580, after an eleven-year siege, the Ishiyama Hongan-ji surrendered, and in the same year Nobunaga's forces conquered Kaga. Under the unified regime that followed, peasants were disarmed and the independent power of religious institutions was broken.
05

What do others say?

At the time

Warrior chroniclers and rival Buddhist schools saw the Ikkō-ikki as dangerous fanatics who overturned the proper social order. Rennyo himself was compelled by the authorities to reprimand the Kaga league, and stepped down as abbot in 1489.

Historians' debates

  • A peasants' republic? Some historians, especially Japanese Marxist historians after 1945, celebrated Kaga as a commoners' republic. Others stress that real power lay with temple leaders and local warrior families, not ordinary peasants.
  • Religion or politics? Historians debate whether the Ikkō-ikki should be understood mainly as a religious movement or as a political and economic alliance that used religion.
  • Rennyo's role: Rennyo condemned the use of force, but his teaching and the temple network he built made the movement possible.
06

The evidence

  • Contemporary diaries and chronicles kept by courtiers, monks and warriors.
  • The letters (ofumi) of Rennyo, which were copied and read aloud to believers.
  • Temple records and documents issued by the league to govern the province.
07

How is it remembered?

  • The history of the Ikkō-ikki is commemorated at the 'Ikki no Sato' museum in Hakusan, Ishikawa Prefecture, near the site of the league's strongholds.
  • Jōdo Shinshū remains one of the largest schools of Buddhism in Japan.
  • The site of the Ishiyama Hongan-ji lies within the grounds of Osaka Castle.
08

Why does it matter?

For nearly a century, a province of Japan was governed not by a warrior lord but by a league of peasants, priests and local warriors bound together by faith and sworn oaths. It is one of the most remarkable examples of self-government by commoners in pre-modern Asia.

  • It shows that the world of the samurai was not the whole story of medieval Japan: villagers organised, negotiated and fought for their interests.
  • It shows how religion could provide the networks and solidarity that made collective action possible.
  • The violence with which the leagues were finally destroyed helped shape the strict social order of early modern Japan.

What other events are like this?

  • 1381The Peasants' Revolt — Tens of thousands of villagers and townspeople marched on London to demand an end to serfdom. For a few days, the government of England was at their mercy.
  • 1378The Revolt of the Ciompi — The wool workers of Florence, excluded from the guilds that ran the city, seized the government — and for a few weeks a wool-carder sat at the head of the republic.
  • 1894The Donghak Peasant Revolution — Korean peasants inspired by a new religion rose against corrupt officials, briefly governed their province through peasant councils, and then fought the Japanese army.
