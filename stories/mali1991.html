Mali's March Revolution

Students and democracy campaigners marched against 23 years of military rule. Soldiers fired on them — and then other soldiers refused, overthrew the dictator, and handed power to an elected government.

When
22 – 26 March 1991
Where
Bamako, Mali
Facts checked
2026-11-15

See it on the map →

01

What is the background to this event?

Moussa Traoré seized power in Mali in a military coup in 1968 and ruled for 23 years.1

  • His regime was accused of corruption and mismanagement, and economic hardship grew.1
  • IMF-backed austerity programmes caused increased hardship for all but the rich.1
  • Inspired by democracy movements across Africa and Eastern Europe, opposition groups formed the Alliance for Democracy in Mali (ADEMA) in October 1990.1
  • Students, teachers, lawyers and trade unionists demanded an end to the one-party state.1

03

What happened?

On 22 March 1991, tens of thousands of students and others marched through the streets of Bamako.1

  • Government soldiers fired on the peaceful demonstrators, killing 28 and setting off days of rioting.1
  • Estimates of the death toll vary: the opposition claimed 148 killed and hundreds wounded; Traoré said 27 had died.1
  • Traoré declared a state of emergency and offered concessions, but refused to step down.1
  • A general strike was called for 25 March. This time, the soldiers did nothing to stop it.1

On 26 March 1991, Lieutenant Colonel Amadou Toumani Touré led a coup and arrested Traoré.1 As news spread, at least another 59 people were killed in revenge attacks; a former minister and Traoré's brother-in-law were burned to death by crowds.1

04

What happened next?

  • Transition: a transitional committee chaired by Touré organised a constitutional referendum and elections, and civilian government was restored in 1992.1
  • Democracy: Alpha Oumar Konaré was elected president. Touré was praised for handing over power and later returned as an elected president in 2002.
  • Justice: Traoré was sentenced to death, but his sentence was commuted and he was pardoned in 2002.1
  • Fragility: Mali's democracy was overthrown by military coups in 2012, 2020 and 2021.
05

What do others say?

At the time

The regime blamed agitators for the unrest. The democracy movement saw itself as fighting for basic freedoms.

Debates since

  • Revolution or coup? Some describe 26 March as a coup; others as a revolution completed by soldiers. The political scientist Andrey Korotayev called it a "coupvolution".1
  • Violence: soldiers killed peaceful demonstrators; after the coup, crowds killed regime figures in revenge.1
  • Why did democracy fail later? Commentators debate whether the democracy built in 1991–92 was too fragile or too dependent on a narrow elite.

07

How is it remembered?

  • 26 March is a national holiday in Mali, Martyrs' Day, honouring those killed in March 1991.
  • A monument to the martyrs stands in Bamako.
08

Why does it matter?

Mali's March Revolution was part of the wave of democracy movements across Africa in the early 1990s. For two decades, Mali was seen as a model of African democracy.

  • It shows the power of students and civil society against military rule.
  • It raises questions about the role of the army in democratic change — and the risks when soldiers decide politics.

What other events are like this?

  • 1964Sudan's October Revolution — When police killed a student at a university debate about the war in the south, Sudanese professionals, workers and students launched a general strike that brought down a military dictatorship in days.
  • 1947The Dakar–Niger Railway Strike — African railway workers in French West Africa held out for more than five months to win equal treatment with their European colleagues.
  • 2006Nepal's People's Movement of 2006 — Defying curfews and shoot-on-sight orders, millions of Nepalis took to the streets for nineteen days and forced their king to give up absolute power — the beginning of the end of a 240-year-old monarchy.
