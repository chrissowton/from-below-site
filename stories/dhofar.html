The Dhofar Revolution

In one of the most isolated places on earth, mountain tribes rose against a sultan who banned radios, bicycles and girls' schools. The revolution opened schools for girls — and used terror against its opponents.

When
1965 – 1976
Where
Dhofar, southern Oman
Facts checked

01

What is the background to this event?

Dhofar is a mountainous region in the south of Oman, separated from the rest of the country by hundreds of miles of desert and more closely linked to Yemen.1

  • Oman was ruled by Sultan Said bin Taimur, who kept the country almost completely closed to the outside world. The scholar Fred Halliday called it "ultra-Tibet".1
  • The sultan banned many modern things, from radios to sunglasses. There were only a handful of schools and almost no health care. Girls' education was forbidden until 1970.1
  • Britain had long protected the sultanate and had military bases in the region.

In their own words

03

What happened?

On 9 June 1965, the Dhofar Liberation Front launched its first attack, ambushing an oil company truck.1 At first a nationalist and tribal movement, it moved to the left, and in 1968 adopted Marxism–Leninism, aiming to liberate the whole Gulf from British imperialism.1

  • Supported by South Yemen and China, the rebels controlled most of Dhofar by the late 1960s.1
  • In liberated areas, they opened schools for boys and girls, set up clinics, and gave women a place in political life and in the armed struggle.1
  • They tried to abolish the tribal system, and used terror against tribal leaders and opponents, including executions.1
  • The sultan's forces responded with search-and-destroy missions, burning villages and blowing up or concreting over wells.1

In 1970, Sultan Qaboos overthrew his father in a coup backed by Britain. He launched development programmes and offered amnesty, while British forces, including the SAS, ran a "hearts and minds" campaign alongside military operations. From 1973, thousands of Iranian troops sent by the Shah joined the war.1

04

What happened next?

  • Defeat: by 1975–76 the rebellion was defeated. Many fighters surrendered under amnesty and joined government-backed units.
  • Modernisation: Sultan Qaboos used oil revenues to build schools, hospitals and roads, transforming Oman. He ruled until his death in 2020.
  • Silence: the revolution is rarely discussed in Oman today.
05

What do others say?

At the time

The rebels saw themselves as part of a global anti-imperialist struggle. The sultan and Britain saw them as communists threatening the Gulf and its oil.

Historians' debates

  • "Hearts and minds": British accounts often present Dhofar as a model counter-insurgency; Abdel Razzaq Takriti and others emphasise the violence of the early campaign and the role of Iranian troops and air power.
  • Revolutionary violence: the front's attempt to destroy the tribal system involved executions and coercion, which alienated many Dhofaris and helped the government win them over.1
  • Women: historians note the real advances for women in liberated areas, but debate how far they went.

How is it remembered?

  • The Lebanese filmmaker Heiny Srour's documentary The Hour of Liberation Has Arrived (1974) filmed the liberated areas and their women fighters.
08

Why does it matter?

The Dhofar revolution was one of the most radical movements of the 1960s and 1970s, in one of the most isolated places in the world. It forced change on Oman, even in defeat.

  • It raises questions about the role of Britain in the Gulf after empire.
  • It shows how revolutions can bring education and women's rights, and also repression.
  • It is a key case in debates about counter-insurgency.

Questions to think about

  1. Why do you think Sultan Said bin Taimur kept Oman so closed?
  2. Why did the rebels open schools for girls?
  3. Why did the revolution eventually lose support?
  4. Should we judge the revolution by its reforms or by its violence?

  • 1952The Mau Mau Uprising — An armed rebellion over land and freedom was met with mass detention and torture. Decades later, the British government paid compensation to survivors.
  • 2006Nepal's People's Movement of 2006 — Defying curfews and shoot-on-sight orders, millions of Nepalis took to the streets for nineteen days and forced their king to give up absolute power — the beginning of the end of a 240-year-old monarchy.
