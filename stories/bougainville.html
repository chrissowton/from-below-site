Bougainville: A Mine, a War and the Women Who Made Peace

Landowners' anger at a giant copper mine became the Pacific's deadliest conflict since 1945. Women's groups were at the heart of the long road to peace.

When
1988 – 2001
Where
Panguna and Arawa, Bougainville, Papua New Guinea
Facts checked
2026-11-16

See it on the map →

01

What is the background to this event?

Bougainville is a large island east of mainland Papua New Guinea (PNG). Geographically and culturally it is part of the Solomon Islands chain, but colonial borders placed it under German, then Australian, rule, and in 1975 it became part of independent PNG.1

  • In the 1960s, vast copper deposits were found in the Crown Prince Range. The Australian company Conzinc Rio Tinto opened the Panguna mine, which began production in 1972.1
  • Panguna was then the largest open-cut mine in the world and produced more than 45 per cent of PNG's export revenue.1
  • Local landowners — in a society where land passes through the female line — received little of the wealth, while waste from the mine poisoned rivers. Thousands of migrant workers arrived from the mainland, causing resentment.1

03

What happened?

By late 1988, tensions over the mine led to violence. Militant landowners led by Francis Ona sabotaged the mine's power lines, and Panguna closed in 1989.1

  • PNG police and soldiers were sent in. Both sides were accused of abuses against civilians, including the burning of many villages, and the conflict became a separatist war.1
  • In May 1990, Ona declared independence. PNG imposed a blockade on the island, cutting off medicines and supplies until 1994.1
  • The war became multi-sided: the BRA fought PNG forces and also Bougainvillean "resistance" militias allied with PNG, and there were dozens of local conflicts.1
  • In 1997, the PNG government's attempt to hire foreign mercenaries from Sandline International caused a national crisis and collapsed.1

Estimates of the dead range from 1,000–2,000 to 15,000–20,000 Bougainvilleans, many from disease and lack of medicine during the blockade.1

Women played a central role in ending the war. Groups such as the Leitana Nehan Women's Development Agency, founded in 1992 by Helen Hakena, provided aid, ran peace education, and campaigned against violence.2 Women's organisations brought together mothers from opposing sides and urged their sons to lay down their weapons.

04

What happened next?

  • Peace talks: talks at Burnham, New Zealand, in 1997 produced a truce. The Lincoln Agreement of January 1998 led to a ceasefire monitored by an unarmed regional Peace Monitoring Group.1
  • Peace agreement: the Bougainville Peace Agreement of 2001 created the Autonomous Bougainville Government and promised a referendum on independence.1
  • Recognition: in 2000–01 the Leitana Nehan agency received the UN Millennium Peace Prize for Women.2 But Hakena later noted that very few women were included in constitutional and disarmament processes.2
  • Referendum: in 2019, Bougainvilleans voted overwhelmingly for independence in a non-binding referendum. Negotiations with PNG continue.1
05

What do others say?

At the time

The PNG government saw the rebellion as criminal secession threatening the nation's economy. The BRA saw it as a struggle for land, environment and self-determination.

Historians' and commentators' debates

  • Causes: some emphasise environmental destruction and unequal sharing of mine revenue; others emphasise long-standing Bougainvillean identity and the desire for independence.
  • Violence on all sides: PNG forces were accused of extrajudicial killings and village burnings; the BRA and other armed groups were also accused of killings, intimidation and abuses against civilians. The blockade caused many deaths from lack of medicine.
  • The role of the company and Australia: Australia supplied PNG with helicopters and military support; Rio Tinto's role has been the subject of legal claims, and in 2021 the company agreed to an independent assessment of the mine's environmental and human rights impacts.
  • Women's role: scholars note that women's peacebuilding was crucial but often unrecognised in formal agreements.

07

How is it remembered?

  • Local reconciliation ceremonies, often led by chiefs and women, continue to address the legacy of the war.
  • The documentary The Coconut Revolution (2001) told the story of the BRA and the blockade.
08

Why does it matter?

Bougainville shows how resource extraction can tear a society apart — and how communities, especially women, can rebuild peace from below.

  • It is a key case in debates about mining, land rights and the environment in the Pacific.
  • Its peace process, using unarmed monitors and local reconciliation ceremonies, is studied around the world.
  • The future of the Panguna mine and of Bougainville's independence remain open questions.
