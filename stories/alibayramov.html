The Ali Bayramov Women's Club

It began as a literacy and sewing circle. Within a few years, the women's club in Baku had moved into a millionaire's palace, trained women to teach in villages across Azerbaijan, and inspired more than a hundred clubs — before Stalin's government closed it.

01

What is the background to this event?

In 1918, the Azerbaijan Democratic Republic gave women the vote — the first Muslim-majority country to do so.2

  • In 1920, Azerbaijan was taken over by the Bolsheviks and became part of Soviet Russia.
  • Most Azerbaijani women could not read, and many were largely confined to the home.1
  • Jeyran Bayramova's brother-in-law and husband, the Bolshevik Ali Bayramov, had encouraged her education when her parents had not. He died in March 1920.1

In their own words

03

What happened?

Bayramova first proposed the club to her school friends at her husband's funeral.1

  • The club opened in 1920 as a literacy and sewing circle, under the People's Commissariat for Education. It was named after Ali Bayramov.1
  • It aimed to attract working-class women of all ethnic backgrounds, and it campaigned for literacy and for women to stop wearing the veil.1
  • It offered plays, music, films, chess, sport and dance, as well as training in skills such as midwifery and telephone work.1
  • In 1922 the club moved into the former mansion of the oil millionaire Mukhtarov.1
  • Women who passed its literacy exam were sent to set up clubs in the regions. By the early 1930s there were 103 such clubs.1

04

What happened next?

  • Hostility: many women needed their husbands' permission to attend, and male communists were often unenthusiastic. One meeting was reportedly attacked with boiling water and dogs.1
  • Closure: in 1930 Stalin declared that women's issues had been 'solved'. The Zhenotdel was closed that year, and the club was closed in 1937.1
  • Legacy: the sewing workshop, which began with seven workers, became a textile factory; the midwifery course became the School of Midwifery in Baku.1
05

What do others say?

At the time

Clara Zetkin praised the club when she visited in 1924; its opponents in Azerbaijani society attacked it as a threat to religion and family.1

Debates since

  • Liberation or Sovietisation? The unveiling campaign gained strength from Stalin's 'Hujum' campaigns, which also aimed to suppress Islam; veiling was banned in 1929. Historians debate how far women chose these changes and how far they were imposed.1
  • Who joined? The club excluded women from bourgeois backgrounds, which shows its close links to the Communist Party.1

How is it remembered?

  • The Mukhtarov Palace, which housed the club, is now known as the Palace of Happiness.1
08

Why does it matter?

The club shows women organising to change their own lives through education, work and culture. It also shows how such movements depended on the state's support — and could be shut down when that support was withdrawn.
