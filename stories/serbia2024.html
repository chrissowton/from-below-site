Serbia's Student Revolt after Novi Sad

When a newly renovated station canopy collapsed in Novi Sad and killed sixteen people, students blamed corruption. Their blockades and silent vigils grew into the largest protests in Serbia's history and, two years later, an election that removed the ruling party.

Ongoing situationLast reviewed 16 November 2026

The Student List is forming Serbia's first government without the Progressive Party since 2012. Because Vučić resigned, a presidential election must be held by the end of December 2026.1

Still unresolved:

  • Whether the new government can govern with little political experience
  • The result of the presidential election
  • Whether anyone is convicted over the canopy collapse

This is recent history. Accounts, casualty figures and outcomes may change as more evidence emerges.

01

What is the background to this event?

Aleksandar Vučić's Serbian Progressive Party (SNS) had dominated Serbian politics since 2012, and Vučić had been president since 2017.23

  • Critics accused the government of corruption, control of the media and pressure on independent institutions.2
  • The railway station in Novi Sad had been renovated by a Chinese-led consortium and reopened in July 2024.24
  • On 1 November 2024, its concrete canopy collapsed onto people below. Fourteen died that day and two more of their injuries later, bringing the death toll to sixteen.2
02

Who were the key people?

  • University and school students

    Who blockaded their faculties and organised through open assemblies (plenums) rather than leaders.

  • The victims of the canopy collapse

    Sixteen people, whose deaths were marked by daily silences of sixteen minutes.

  • Aleksandar Vučić

    President of Serbia from 2017 until his resignation in September 2026.

  • Miloš Vučević

    Prime Minister, who resigned in January 2025.

  • Teachers, farmers, lawyers and workers

    Who joined the students with strikes, marches and blockades.

In their own words

03

What happened?

At first, people gathered for quiet vigils. On 22 November 2024, students at the Faculty of Dramatic Arts in Belgrade were attacked during a silent tribute, and in response they blockaded their faculty. Other faculties and high schools soon followed.2

  • Protesters stopped traffic every day at 11.52, the time of the collapse, for fifteen minutes of silence, later sixteen.2
  • Students set out four demands: publication of all the documents on the station's reconstruction, the dropping of charges against arrested protesters, prosecution of those who had attacked students, and more funding for universities.2
  • Prosecutors charged thirteen people, including the former construction minister, but the minister was released from detention, which fuelled distrust.4
  • On 28 January 2025, after people linked to the ruling party attacked students in Novi Sad, Prime Minister Vučević resigned.6
  • On 15 March 2025, a huge rally filled central Belgrade. Police put the crowd at 107,000; independent counters estimated 275,000–325,000. It was regarded as the largest protest in Serbia's history.2

The protests continued through 2025 and 2026, with marches across the country, blockades of the state broadcaster, and clashes between protesters, police and supporters of the ruling party.2 The government accused foreign powers of organising a "colour revolution".2

04

What happened next?

  • Elections: Vučić called a snap parliamentary election for 25 October 2026, and resigned as president on 27 September to lead his party's list.3
  • Result: Vučić conceded defeat on election night. The final results gave the Student List more than 2.3 million votes and 140 of the 250 seats, an absolute majority. Vučić's list won 103 seats.78
  • The movement: the protests wound down after the election, as the students' candidates, mostly professors, teachers and other professionals with little political experience, prepared to form a government.28
05

Why does it matter?

The Serbian protests show how a disaster blamed on corruption can become a wider movement for accountability, and how students who refused to have leaders or join parties could, in the end, win power through an election.

  • It became the largest protest movement in Serbia's history: estimates of the Belgrade rally of 15 March 2025 ranged from 107,000, the police figure, to 275,000–325,000 from independent counters.2
  • Students organised without leaders, decided everything in assemblies, and turned silence into a form of protest, stopping traffic every day at 11.52, the time of the collapse.2
  • A movement that began outside party politics ended by winning an election: in October 2026 the Student List took 140 of the 250 seats, an absolute majority.78
  • Whether that was a victory for the movement or a transformation of it is still argued over.7

What do others say?How it was seen at the time, and where historians disagree

At the time

Protesters blamed corruption and a lack of accountability for the deaths. The government said it had met the students' demands and accused foreign-backed opponents of trying to overthrow it.2

Debates since

  • Crowd sizes: official and independent estimates differed widely; for 15 March 2025 they ranged from 107,000 to over 300,000.2
  • Violence: both sides accused the other of violence. Students and opposition reported attacks by ruling-party supporters and police; the government blamed protesters for clashes.2
  • Revolution or election? The movement began outside party politics but ended by contesting an election. Observers debate whether this was a victory or a transformation of the movement.7

Čačak, 26 January 2025

A protest in the town of Čačak. The banner reads, 'We don't have a plan B, but we are destroying plan A'. The movement spread far beyond Novi Sad and Belgrade to some 400 towns.

Reading it

The banner's joke — 'We don't have a plan B, but we are destroying plan A' — catches the movement's mood: no programme, but a flat refusal. The setting matters too. This is Čačak, not Belgrade; the protests reached some 400 towns, which is part of what made them so hard to dismiss.
