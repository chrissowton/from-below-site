The Lowell Mill Girls Turn Out

Young women from New England farms worked twelve to fourteen hours a day in America's first great textile mills. When the companies cut wages and raised rents, they walked out — and in 1845 founded the first union of working women in the United States.

When
1834 – 1845
Where
Lowell, Massachusetts, United States
Facts checked

01

What is the background to this event?

From the 1820s, the textile mills of Lowell recruited young women, mostly daughters of New England farmers aged 15 to 35, and housed them in company boarding houses.1

  • By 1840, the mills employed over 8,000 workers, nearly three-quarters of them women.1
  • The women earned three to four dollars a week — about half what men were paid — but for many it was their first economic independence. They formed book clubs and published their own journals, such as the Lowell Offering.1
  • They worked twelve to fourteen hours a day in dangerous conditions, and lived in crowded dormitories.1

In their own words

03

What happened?

In February 1834, the mill owners announced a wage cut of 12.5%. The women held meetings and organised a 'turn-out' — a strike.1

  • The strikers withdrew their savings, causing a run on two local banks. But the strike failed, and within days they had gone back to work at reduced pay or left town.1
  • In October 1836, the owners proposed raising the rent the workers paid for company housing. The women formed the Factory Girls' Association and turned out again.1
  • This time over 1,500 workers struck — nearly twice as many as in 1834 — and the mills ran far below capacity.1
  • After several weeks, the owners withdrew the rent rise.1
  • In January 1845, twelve workers founded the Lowell Female Labor Reform Association. Within six months it had 500 members. It petitioned the state legislature for a ten-hour day.1
04

What happened next?

  • The ten-hour day: the Massachusetts legislature held hearings in 1845 but decided it was not its job to regulate hours. In 1847, neighbouring New Hampshire passed the first state ten-hour law, though it was not enforced.1
  • A workers' press: the Association published in the Voice of Industry, where mill workers criticised factory life in their own words.1
05

What do others say?

At the time

The companies presented Lowell as a model of educated, well-cared-for workers; a worker writing in the Voice of Industry replied that "we who work in the factory know the sober reality to be quite another thing".1

Debates since

  • Freedom or exploitation? Historians debate how far mill work freed young women and how far it exploited them.1
06

The evidence

  • The workers' own journals and newspapers, the constitution of the Factory Girls' Association, and memoirs such as Harriet Hanson Robinson's.1

07

How is it remembered?

  • Lowell's mills and boarding houses are preserved today, and the mill girls are remembered as pioneers of women's labour organising in the United States.1
08

Why does it matter?

The Lowell women organised at a time when it was considered shocking for a woman to speak in public. As the labour historian Philip Foner put it, they raised serious questions about "woman's so-called 'place'".1

Open questions

  1. Why did living together in boarding houses help the women organise?

09

What other events are like this?

  • 1888The Matchwomen's Strike — About 1,400 women and girls walked out of a match factory, won their demands in three weeks, and founded the largest union of women in Britain.
  • 1844The Silesian Weavers' Uprising — Hand weavers starving on falling piece rates marched on the mansions of the merchants who paid them. Prussian troops shot eleven of them dead, and the rising became a symbol for poets, playwrights and Karl Marx.
  • 1856Eight Hours: The Stonemasons of Melbourne — Building workers in Sydney and Melbourne downed tools and marched on Parliament to demand an eight-hour day — and won it, decades before most workers anywhere in the world.
