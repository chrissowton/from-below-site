LGBTQ+ liberation

The Gay Liberation Front in London

A handful of people met in a basement at the LSE in 1970. Within weeks they marched through Highbury Fields — the UK's first public gay protest — and within two years they had organised Britain's first Pride march.

01

What is the background to this event?

In 1967, sex between men over 21 had been partly decriminalised in England and Wales, but gay people still faced arrest, prejudice and discrimination.

  • After the Stonewall riots of 1969 in New York, Gay Liberation Fronts were founded in the United States.1
  • Bob Mellors and Aubrey Walter had seen the GLF in America and set out to create a movement in Britain based on revolutionary politics.1

03

What happened?

The London Gay Liberation Front held its first meeting in the basement of the London School of Economics on 13 October 1970.1

  • On 27 November 1970, about 150 people marched through Highbury Fields in north London, protesting against the arrest of Louis Eaks for 'importuning'. It was the UK's first public gay protest.12
  • The GLF published a manifesto and its own newspaper, Come Together.1
  • It used 'zaps' and street theatre, most famously disrupting the launch of the Nationwide Festival of Light, a Christian morality campaign, in 1971.1
  • On 1 July 1972, about 200 people took part in the first official Pride march in London.2
04

What happened next?

  • New organisations: groups that grew out of the GLF include the London Lesbian and Gay Switchboard and the newspaper Gay News.1
  • Trans voices: in 1972, members including Rachel Pollack and Roz Kaveney wrote an essay that has been described as Britain's "first trans manifesto".1
  • Pride: the London Pride march grew from about 200 people in 1972 to the hosting of the first EuroPride in 1992, with some 100,000.2
05

Why does it matter?

The GLF changed the demand from tolerance to liberation. Its marches, zaps and newspapers launched the modern LGBTQ+ movement in Britain, and Pride.12

  • Groups that grew out of it include the London Lesbian and Gay Switchboard and the newspaper Gay News.1
  • London Pride grew from about 200 people in 1972 to some 100,000 at the first EuroPride in 1992.2
  • A plaque at Highbury Fields marks its first demonstration.1

What do others say?How it was seen at the time, and where historians disagree

At the time

The GLF argued that gay people should be proud and visible, not hidden; moral campaigners such as the Festival of Light argued for traditional Christian values.1

Debates since

  • Liberation or equality? Later campaigners debated whether to seek change through radical protest, like the GLF, or through lobbying for legal equality.1

The underground newspaper Ink gave a whole issue's cover to the Gay Liberation Front. Underground and alternative papers were one of the main ways the GLF's ideas spread in its first years.

Reading it

The underground newspaper Ink gave a whole cover to the Gay Liberation Front. The mainstream press either ignored the GLF or mocked it, so underground papers were how its ideas spread in its first years. The cover was aimed at the young, radical readers who might join.
