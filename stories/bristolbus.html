Civil rights & anti-racism

The Bristol Bus Boycott

West Indian activists in Bristol organised a boycott of the city's buses to end a colour bar that kept Black and Asian people from working as bus crews.

When
April – August 1963
Where
Bristol, England
Facts checked
2026-11-16

01

What is the background to this event?

After the Second World War, thousands of people from the Caribbean came to Britain, many invited to fill labour shortages in transport, health and industry. By the early 1960s several thousand people of West Indian origin lived in Bristol, many in the St Pauls area, where they faced discrimination in housing and employment.

One of the most visible grievances was the policy of the Bristol Omnibus Company, a nationalised company, which refused to employ Black or Asian people as drivers or conductors:

  • In 1955 the Passenger Group of the Transport and General Workers' Union (TGWU) in Bristol had passed a resolution that "coloured" workers should not be employed as bus crews.
  • Local union officials and the company publicly denied that any colour bar existed.
  • Some white bus workers feared that new recruits would reduce their overtime and earnings.

There was no law against racial discrimination in Britain at the time.

In their own words

03

What happened?

Four young West Indian men — Roy Hackett, Owen Henry, Audley Evans and Prince Brown — formed an action group, which became the West Indian Development Council. With Paul Stephenson as spokesman, they arranged for Guy Bailey to apply for a job as a bus conductor; he was refused an interview because he was Black.2

Inspired by the Montgomery bus boycott in Alabama, the group announced a boycott of the city's buses at a press conference on 29 April 1963.2 The campaign won support from many directions:23

  • Many West Indian residents stopped using the buses, walking or sharing lifts instead.
  • On 1 May, hundreds of students from the University of Bristol marched to the bus station in support.
  • Tony Benn MP and the Labour leader Harold Wilson spoke against the colour bar, as did Learie Constantine, the cricketer and High Commissioner for Trinidad and Tobago.

The campaign also faced opposition. Union officials and some local leaders claimed the West Indian Development Council was unrepresentative; an official at the Jamaican High Commission replied that it was "nonsense to describe a group of West Indians as unrepresentative when no representative West Indian body existed".2 One shop steward explained his members' fears that "an influx of people from elsewhere" would reduce "their earnings potential".2

After months of negotiations between the company and the union, a mass meeting of 500 bus workers voted on 27 August to end the colour bar. On 28 August 1963 — the same day as Martin Luther King Jr.'s "I Have a Dream" speech in Washington — the company announced that there would be no more discrimination in employing bus crews.2 On 17 September Raghbir Singh became Bristol's first non-white bus conductor.2

04

What happened next?

  • Legislation: the Race Relations Act 1965, passed by Harold Wilson's Labour government, made racial discrimination unlawful in public places. The Race Relations Act 1968 extended this to housing and employment. Many commentators credit the Bristol campaign with helping to build support for these laws.
  • Apology: in 2013 Unite, the successor to the TGWU, apologised for the union's role.
  • Recognition: Paul Stephenson and Roy Hackett were honoured for their work, and in 2020 Great Western Railway named trains after Stephenson and other figures of Bristol's Black history.
05

What do others say?

At the time

The bus company and union officials denied that there was a colour bar and accused the campaigners of stirring up trouble. Some members of the local West Indian community, and some church leaders, preferred quiet negotiation to a public boycott.

Historians' debates

  • How many boycotted? The extent of the boycott — how many people actually stopped using the buses — is uncertain; some historians suggest that its main power lay in publicity rather than economic pressure.
  • Who gets the credit? Paul Stephenson became the public face of the campaign, but in later years Roy Hackett and others emphasised the role of the four founders; historians have sought to give fuller recognition to all of them.
  • Its effect on the law: some historians caution against attributing the Race Relations Acts too directly to Bristol, pointing to wider campaigns and political pressures.

The evidence

  • Local newspapers, especially the Bristol Evening Post and Western Daily Press.
  • Union and company records, and correspondence with government departments.
  • Oral histories recorded with participants in later decades, including for Madge Dresser's research.
  • Memoirs, such as Paul Stephenson's.
07

How is it remembered?

  • A plaque at Bristol bus station commemorates the boycott, and an exhibition was held at the M Shed museum on its 50th anniversary in 2013.
  • The boycott is taught in many British schools as part of Black History Month.
  • A mural in St Pauls celebrates Roy Hackett, Paul Stephenson and other local figures.
08

Why does it matter?

The Bristol Bus Boycott is one of the most important episodes in the history of civil rights in Britain. It showed that racial discrimination was not only an American problem, and that a small group of local activists could expose and defeat it.

  • It contributed to Britain's first anti-discrimination laws.
  • It drew directly on the example of the American civil rights movement, showing how ideas and tactics travel between countries.
  • It revealed the uncomfortable role that a trade union — usually an organisation for workers' rights — had played in excluding Black workers.
