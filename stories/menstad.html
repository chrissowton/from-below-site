The Battle of Menstad

During a Depression-era lockout, 2,000 workers marched on a Norsk Hydro port where strikebreakers were working. They overwhelmed the police — and the defence minister, Vidkun Quisling, sent in the army.

When
8 June 1931
Where
Menstad, near Skien, Norway
Facts checked
2026-11-16

See it on the map →

01

What is the background to this event?

In 1931, the Great Depression hit Norway hard, and industrial tensions rose.1

  • The employers' confederation demanded pay cuts of 15–20%.1
  • When the unions refused and proposed shorter hours instead, employers locked out their workers.1
  • Norsk Hydro and another company used contract workers during the lockout, which unions saw as strikebreaking.1
02

Who were the key people?

  • Locked-out workers of Telemark

    About 2,000 of whom marched to Menstad.

  • Vidkun Quisling

    Minister of Defence, later leader of Norway's Nazi collaborationist government.

  • Einar Gerhardsen

    Labour Party secretary, later prime minister.

03

What happened?

On the afternoon of 8 June 1931, about 2,000 workers marched to Norsk Hydro's port and warehouse at Menstad.1

  • About 100 police officers were guarding the contract workers.1
  • The workers overwhelmed the police, throwing stones and iron pipes, then withdrew.1
  • The government, through Defence Minister Quisling, sent the army to Telemark.1
  • There was no further fighting, and order was restored.1

Sources

  1. Wikipedia, 'Menstad conflict', on the lockout, the strikebreakers, the march, the clash with police and the sending of troops. Link ↗
04

What happened next?

  • Criticism: the government's use of troops was heavily criticised.1
  • Labour's rise: the Labour Party came to power in 1935, and in the same year a "Basic Agreement" between unions and employers set the pattern for Norwegian industrial relations.
05

What do others say?

At the time

The Communist Party, which had led the demonstrations, condemned the use of troops; employers saw a threat to order.1

Debates since

  • Quisling: his role at Menstad is often seen as an early sign of his authoritarian politics.
  • Violence: the workers attacked the police with stones and pipes; no one was killed, and the army's arrival passed without further fighting.1

How is it remembered?

  • Songs such as "The Battle of Menstad" kept the event alive in the labour movement.
08

Why does it matter?

Menstad was one of the sharpest labour conflicts in interwar Norway, at a time of mass unemployment.

  • It came in the same years as the shootings at Ådalen in Sweden.

Questions to think about

  1. Why did employers use lockouts?
  2. Why did workers see contract labour as strikebreaking?
  3. Why was sending the army so controversial?
  4. How did Scandinavian countries move from conflict to compromise?
09

What other events are like this?

  • 1931The Ådalen Shootings — Thousands of striking sawmill and pulp workers marched on the quarters of strike-breakers. Soldiers opened fire, killing five people. Sweden never again used its army against its own workers.
  • 1919The Winnipeg General Strike — For six weeks, 30,000 workers brought Canada's third-largest city to a halt to win the right to collective bargaining. It ended with 'Bloody Saturday' — and transformed Canadian politics.
  • 1991The Swiss Women's Strike — 'If women want, everything stands still.' Ten years after equality was written into the constitution with little effect, half a million Swiss women stopped work, at home and in the workplace.
