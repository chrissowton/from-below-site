The Kongo-Wara Rebellion (War of the Hoe Handle)

A Gbaya healer preached non-violent resistance to forced labour, symbolised by a tiny hoe handle. When the French tried to crush the movement, it became one of the largest rural revolts in inter-war Africa.

When
1928 – 1931
Where
Upper Sangha, French Equatorial Africa (Central African Republic and Cameroon)

See it on the map →

01

What is the background to this event?

In the early twentieth century, France ruled vast areas of Central Africa as French Equatorial Africa. Much of the territory was handed to private concession companies, which forced Africans to gather rubber and other products. The French writer André Gide exposed the brutality of the system in his Voyage au Congo (1927).

  • From 1921, tens of thousands of men from across the region were forcibly recruited to build the Congo-Océan railway. Many thousands died from disease, malnutrition and accidents; the journalist Albert Londres described the railway as having been built at the cost of a life for every sleeper.
  • Among the Gbaya people of the Upper Sangha region, a healer called Barka Ngaïnoumbey, known as Karnou ("he who can change the world"), began from 1924 to preach non-violent resistance to recruitment for the railway and to rubber tapping.1
  • Karnou also preached against the Fula, who administered parts of Gbaya territory in Cameroon on France's behalf.1
02

Who were the key people?

  • Karnou (Barka Ngaïnoumbey)

    A Gbaya healer and prophet whose teaching inspired the movement. He was killed by a French patrol on 11 December 1928.

  • The Gbaya and their neighbours

    Gbaya, Pana, Karre, Yanghere and other peoples of the region, who joined the movement in large numbers.

  • The French colonial administration

    Which had only a limited presence in the region and responded with military force.

  • African chiefs and auxiliaries

    Who worked for the French administration, and were among those attacked by the rebels.

03

What happened?

Karnou's message was that the French and the Fula could be overthrown without violence, through traditional medicine symbolised by a small hooked stick that resembled a miniature hoe handle — kongo wara. Around him grew a movement that included a boycott of European goods and calls for solidarity among Africans. Such unity was unprecedented in a region known for its political fragmentation.1

The French administration paid little attention until 1927, when many of the movement's followers began to take up arms.1 Armed conflict broke out in mid-1928:1

  • Violence spread against French traders and government posts, and against African chiefs and soldiers who worked for the French. The town of Bouar was occupied and burned by Karnou's followers.
  • The French launched a counter-attack with reinforced troops in late 1928. On 11 December, Karnou was killed by a French patrol.
  • The rebellion nevertheless spread to neighbouring groups in Cameroon and the Lower Ubangi. French troops imprisoned the movement's followers and forcibly relocated people from the forest.

Many fighters believed that the hoe-handle medicine made them invulnerable to bullets; one was recorded dancing in front of a French commander, chanting that his gun would only fire water.1 The final stage of the conflict, the "war of the caves", came in 1931, when rebels who had taken refuge in caves were besieged and defeated.1

04

What happened next?

  • Repression: the rebellion was crushed, and many people were killed, imprisoned or displaced. The number of deaths is not known.
  • Reform: scandals over the Congo-Océan railway and the concession system, together with the revolt, contributed to reforms in French colonial labour policy, although forced labour was only abolished in 1946.
  • Independence: the Central African Republic became independent in 1960, and Karnou came to be honoured there as a hero of anti-colonial resistance.
05

What do others say?

At the time

French officials described the rebellion as the work of a fanatical "sorcerer" and barbarous tribes. Critics of the colonial system in France, such as Gide and Londres, pointed to the abuses that drove people to revolt.

Historians' debates

  • Non-violence or war? Karnou preached non-violence, but his movement became an armed revolt. Historians debate whether this was a betrayal of his teaching or an inevitable response to French repression.
  • Religion or politics? Thomas O'Toole asked whether it was a "messianic movement" or "village self-defence"; most historians see it as both.
  • Victims on all sides: the rebels killed traders, officials and African chiefs and soldiers who served the French; French repression killed and displaced far larger numbers of villagers. Records of the African dead are especially incomplete.
06

The evidence

  • French colonial military and administrative reports.
  • The writings of André Gide and Albert Londres on conditions in French Equatorial Africa.
  • Oral traditions collected among the Gbaya and their neighbours by historians such as Raphaël Nzabakomada-Yakoma.
07

How is it remembered?

  • Karnou is remembered in the Central African Republic as a hero of resistance to colonialism.
  • The memory of forced labour on the Congo-Océan railway remains strong in the region.
08

Why does it matter?

The Kongo-Wara rebellion was one of the largest anti-colonial revolts in inter-war Africa, yet it is little known outside Central Africa. It shows how people facing forced labour and exploitation used their own spiritual traditions to unite and resist.

  • It brought together peoples who had never before acted together.
  • It drew attention to the human cost of the Congo-Océan railway and the concession system.
  • It reminds us that colonial "pacification" in Africa continued long after the conquest.

Questions to think about

  1. Why do you think forced labour for the railway provoked such resistance?
  2. How could a small symbol like the hoe handle unite so many people?
  3. Why might a movement that began as non-violent turn to armed revolt?
  4. Why do you think this rebellion is so little known outside Central Africa?

What other events are like this?

  • 1905The Maji Maji War — Forced to grow cotton for German colonial rulers, peoples across southern Tanzania united behind a sacred water medicine and rose in one of Africa's largest anti-colonial wars.
  • 1915The Chilembwe Uprising — A Baptist minister educated in the United States led a short-lived rising against forced labour, racism and the recruitment of Africans to die in Britain's war.
  • 1959The Léopoldville Riots — When a nationalist meeting was banned, Kinshasa's unemployed young people rose in three days of rioting. Within eighteen months, the Congo was independent.
