The Tomb-Builders' Strike at Deir el-Medina

When the pharaoh's grain rations failed to arrive, the craftsmen who built the royal tombs laid down their tools and marched to the temples — the earliest strike in recorded history.

When
c. 1157 BCE (Year 29 of Ramesses III)
Where
Deir el-Medina, Thebes (near Luxor), Egypt

See it on the map →

01

What is the background to this event?

Deir el-Medina was a village on the west bank of the Nile, opposite the city of Thebes (modern Luxor). For around 450 years it was home to the skilled craftsmen who cut and decorated the royal tombs in the Valley of the Kings, together with their families.12 Its ancient name was Set Maat, the "Place of Truth", and the workmen were known as "Servants in the Place of Truth".2

  • The workmen were organised into two gangs, the "left" and the "right", each with a foreman, a deputy and scribes who kept detailed records of their work, absences and pay.12
  • They were state employees, paid in kind on the 28th day of each month for the month ahead: emmer wheat for bread, barley for beer, and also fish, vegetables, oil and clothing. Their rations were generous compared with those of farm labourers.12
  • They knew how important they were. When their wages were late, they could complain directly to the vizier, the most senior official below the pharaoh.1

By the later years of Ramesses III, Egypt was under strain. Wars against invaders known as the Sea Peoples had been costly, harvests seem to have been poor and grain prices were rising.1 In Year 28 of the king's reign, the workmen's pay was already late; the scribe Amennakht negotiated the release of 46 sacks of grain from a temple granary — only part of what they were owed.1

02

Who were the key people?

  • Amennakht

    The scribe of the tomb, who negotiated on the workers' behalf and wrote the papyrus that records the strike.

  • The workmen of the tomb

    Stone-cutters, plasterers, draughtsmen and painters — perhaps sixty men with their families — who built and decorated the royal tombs.

  • Khonsu

    A foreman of the workmen named in the records of the strike.

  • To

    The vizier of Upper Egypt, to whom the workers appealed.

  • Ramesses III

    The pharaoh (reigned c. 1186–1155 BCE) whose government failed to deliver the rations.

03

What happened?

The strike is recorded in a papyrus written by the scribe Amennakht, now in the Museo Egizio in Turin and known as the "Strike Papyrus".13 It began on the 21st day of the second month of winter in Year 29 of Ramesses III — around 1157 BCE. Rations were by then about eighteen days overdue.1

The workmen took collective action several times over the following months, sometimes by day and sometimes at night, carrying torches:1

  • They downed tools and walked out of the Valley of the Kings, past the guard posts, declaring that they were hungry.
  • They went to the mortuary temples on the west bank — including the temple of Thutmose III and Ramesses III's own temple at Medinet Habu — and sat down behind them, refusing to leave.
  • They marched to the Ramesseum, the temple of Ramesses II, loudly demanding their grain, meat, oil and vegetables.

The workers explained their action to the officials who came out to meet them:

"We are hungry, for eighteen days have already elapsed this month… The prospect of hunger and thirst has driven us to this; there is no clothing, there is no ointment, there is no fish, there are no vegetables. Send to Pharaoh, our good lord, about it, and send to the vizier, our superior, that we may be supplied with provisions."The workmen of the tomb, as recorded in the Strike Papyrus1

To placate them, officials released the rations owed for the previous month, and on several occasions grain was distributed from the temple stores. Each time, the workmen returned to work — only to strike again when supplies dried up. When officials gave them part of their rations, the workers agreed collectively to go back, even though most of them had still not been paid.1

04

What happened next?

  • More strikes: labour disputes continued at Deir el-Medina through the Twentieth Dynasty, as grain prices rose and the authority of the state weakened.
  • A murdered king: a year or two after the strikes, Ramesses III was the target of the "Harem Conspiracy", a plot by members of his household; scientific study of his mummy suggests that his throat was cut.
  • Tomb robbery: as the state weakened under the later Ramesside kings, tomb robbery increased, and some workmen themselves were later accused of robbing tombs.
  • Abandonment: the village was abandoned around 1100 BCE, under Ramesses XI, the last king to have a tomb cut in the Valley of the Kings.
05

What do others say?

Interpreting the evidence

Almost everything we know comes from the records of the workmen's own scribes, written on papyrus and on thousands of ostraca (inscribed fragments of pottery and limestone). These are unusually rich, but they record events from the point of view of the community's administrators.

Historians' debates

  • Was it really a "strike"? Some Egyptologists prefer to call it a protest or demonstration, since the workers were not seeking new rights but demanding rations already owed to them. Others argue that downing tools to force payment is exactly what a strike is.
  • Why did the rations fail? Some historians blame a general economic crisis — poor harvests and inflation; others point to corruption or mismanagement by officials, noting that the workers themselves accused some officials of stealing.
  • A sign of decline? The strikes are often seen as an early symptom of the weakening of the New Kingdom state, which fragmented a century later.
06

The evidence

  • The Strike Papyrus (Turin Papyrus Cat. 1880), written by the scribe Amennakht.
  • Thousands of ostraca found at Deir el-Medina, including some 5,000 discovered in a single well, which record work rotas, absences, rations, legal disputes and letters.
  • The remains of the village itself, excavated by Ernesto Schiaparelli (1905–1909) and Bernard Bruyère (from 1922), and studied for decades by Jaroslav Černý.
07

How is it remembered?

  • The Strike Papyrus is displayed in the Museo Egizio in Turin, which holds one of the most important collections from Deir el-Medina.
  • Trade unions and labour historians often cite Deir el-Medina as the beginning of the history of strikes.
  • The village and the workmen's own decorated tombs can be visited near Luxor.
08

Why does it matter?

The strike at Deir el-Medina is the earliest recorded collective labour action in history. More than three thousand years ago, a group of workers recognised that their labour gave them power, acted together, and forced the most powerful state of their time to listen to them.

  • It shows that strikes are not a modern invention: the basic tactics — downing tools, sitting in, marching and negotiating — are ancient.
  • It gives a rare glimpse of the voices of ordinary working people in the ancient world.
  • It reminds us that the great monuments of the past were built by people with families, grievances and demands.

Questions to think about

  1. Why do you think the tomb-builders felt able to take action against the pharaoh's government?
  2. What tactics did the workers use, and how do they compare with those of modern strikes?
  3. What are the strengths and weaknesses of evidence written by the community's own scribes?
  4. Is it useful to call this event a 'strike'? Why or why not?

What other events are like this?

  • 1888The Matchwomen's Strike — About 1,400 women and girls walked out of a match factory, won their demands in three weeks, and founded the largest union of women in Britain.
  • 1947The Dakar–Niger Railway Strike — African railway workers in French West Africa held out for more than five months to win equal treatment with their European colleagues.
  • 1907The Santa María School Massacre — Thousands of striking nitrate miners and their families were shot down in and around a school in Iquique — one of the worst massacres of workers in Latin American history.
