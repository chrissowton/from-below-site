The vote & democracy

The Peterloo Massacre

Tens of thousands of working people gathered peacefully to demand the vote. Within ten minutes, cavalry had cleared the field with sabres.

When
16 August 1819
Where
St Peter's Field, Manchester, England

See it on the map →

01

What is the background to this event?

The end of the Napoleonic Wars in 1815 brought little relief to the working people of Lancashire. Demobilised soldiers returned to find no work; the price of bread was kept high by the Corn Laws, which protected landowners from cheaper foreign grain; and the wages of handloom weavers, once a relatively prosperous trade, collapsed under competition from the new power looms.

Working people also had no political voice. The parliamentary system was a patchwork inherited from the Middle Ages:

  • Only a small minority of adult men could vote, and none of the women.
  • "Rotten boroughs" such as Old Sarum in Wiltshire returned two MPs with a handful of voters, while Manchester — by then a rapidly growing industrial town — had no MP of its own.
  • Voting was public, which exposed voters to pressure from landlords and employers.

Radical reformers organised Hampden Clubs, reading societies and, from 1818, Female Reform Societies. The government regarded all of this as dangerous. In March 1819 the Home Secretary, Lord Sidmouth, wrote to the Lancashire magistrates that the county "will not be tranquillised until blood shall have been shed, either by the law or the sword."

02

Who were the key people?

  • Henry 'Orator' Hunt

    A Wiltshire gentleman-farmer and the most celebrated radical speaker of his day, invited to address the meeting. He urged people to attend 'armed with no other weapon but that of a self-approving conscience'. Arrested on the hustings, he was later imprisoned for two and a half years.

  • Samuel Bamford

    A weaver and poet from Middleton who led his town's contingent and later wrote one of the most important eyewitness accounts, Passages in the Life of a Radical (1844).

  • Mary Fildes

    President of the Manchester Female Reform Society, who stood on the hustings with Hunt and was injured by a sabre during the charge.

  • Joseph Johnson

    Secretary of the Manchester Patriotic Union, which organised the meeting.

  • John Lees

    A young cotton spinner from Oldham who had fought at Waterloo. He died of his injuries on 9 September 1819.

  • William Fildes

    A two-year-old child, recorded as the first person killed, when he was ridden down by a Yeomanry trooper on the way to the field.

  • John Tyas

    A reporter for The Times, who was on the hustings, was arrested with the speakers, and whose account helped to turn national opinion against the authorities.

03

What happened?

Monday 16 August 1819 was a hot, clear day. The organisers had made great efforts to ensure a peaceful and orderly meeting. Samuel Bamford recalled that "it was deemed expedient that this meeting should be as morally effective as possible."1 Contingents marched in from the surrounding towns, many people wearing their Sunday best, with bands and banners reading "No Corn Laws", "Annual Parliaments", "Universal Suffrage" and "Vote By Ballot".2

Estimates of the crowd's size have always varied:

  • Contemporaries gave figures ranging from 30,000 to 150,000.2
  • Modern historians have generally placed it between about 40,000 and 80,000, with Robert Poole's recent analysis suggesting around 40,000.3

The magistrates, watching from a house overlooking the field, had substantial forces at their disposal: about 600 men of the 15th Hussars, several hundred infantry, artillery, 400 men of the Cheshire Yeomanry, around 400 special constables, and some 120 members of the Manchester and Salford Yeomanry, a volunteer cavalry force recruited largely from local shopkeepers and publicans.4

Shortly after Hunt began to speak, at around 1:40 pm, the magistrates issued a warrant for the arrest of the speakers and ordered the Manchester and Salford Yeomanry to escort the deputy constable through the crowd. The inexperienced Yeomanry quickly became entangled in the dense crowd. John Tyas of The Times reported that they began "cutting most indiscriminately to the right and to the left" to reach the banners, and that "from this point the Manchester and Salford Yeomanry lost all command of temper."5

At about 1:50 pm, the chairman of the magistrates, William Hulton, ordered the 15th Hussars into the field with the words: "Good God, Sir, don't you see they are attacking the Yeomanry; disperse the meeting!"5 An officer of the Hussars was heard trying to restrain the Yeomanry, shouting: "For shame! For shame! Gentlemen: forbear, forbear! The people cannot get away!"3

Within about ten minutes the field had been cleared.6 The casualties were severe:

  • Deaths: 18 people, according to the research of Michael Bush and Robert Poole.3
  • Injuries: between about 400 and 700, many from sabre cuts and many from being trampled. Of 654 recorded casualties, at least 168 were women.7

John Lees, the Waterloo veteran, is reported to have told a friend before his death:

"At Waterloo there was man to man but there it was downright murder."John Lees, as reported in Robert Reid, The Peterloo Massacre (1989), p. 2018

04

What happened next?

The government's response was to defend the magistrates and to tighten the law:

  • The Prince Regent conveyed his thanks to the magistrates and the troops.
  • Parliament passed the "Six Acts" at the end of 1819, restricting public meetings, increasing taxes on radical newspapers and widening the definition of seditious libel.
  • Hunt, Bamford and other organisers were tried and imprisoned.

Public opinion, however, moved decisively. The radical Manchester Observer coined the bitterly ironic name "Peterloo". In Italy, Percy Bysshe Shelley wrote The Masque of Anarchy, ending with the call "Ye are many — they are few." The local merchant John Edward Taylor, outraged by the events, founded the Manchester Guardian in 1821. The first Reform Act followed in 1832, giving Manchester its own MPs, although it would take until 1918 and 1928 for all adult men and women to win the vote.

For many years the massacre was barely commemorated. A blue plaque placed in 1972 did not mention any violence; it was replaced by a red plaque in 2007, and a permanent memorial by the artist Jeremy Deller was unveiled in 2019.

05

What do others say?

At the time

The magistrates and the government maintained that the meeting was a threat to public order. They pointed to reports that contingents had been drilling on the moors in military formation and argued that a crowd of tens of thousands could easily become an insurrection. The Home Secretary and the Prince Regent publicly thanked the magistrates and troops. Radical and many liberal newspapers, by contrast, described an unprovoked attack on a peaceful crowd.

Historians' debates

  • The size of the crowd: contemporary estimates ranged from 30,000 to 150,000. Robert Poole's recent work suggests around 40,000; others have argued for higher figures.
  • Who was to blame: some accounts blame the inexperienced Manchester Yeomanry above all; others, including Poole, emphasise that the regular Hussars also caused many injuries and that the magistrates' decisions were the root cause.
  • The drilling: radicals said it was to ensure discipline and good order; the authorities saw it as military training. Historians generally accept the organisers' peaceful intent, but debate how far some radicals contemplated physical force.
  • The death toll: figures range from 11 to 18 or more, depending on which deaths are attributed to the day's events.
06

The evidence

  • Eyewitness journalism: John Tyas's report in The Times, written by a reporter who was arrested on the hustings, was crucial in shaping national opinion.
  • Trial records: the trial of Hunt and others at York in 1820 and the inquest into John Lees's death produced extensive sworn testimony.
  • Casualty lists: relief committees compiled lists of the injured, recording names, addresses and injuries — an exceptional record of ordinary participants.
  • Memoirs: Samuel Bamford's Passages in the Life of a Radical is vivid but was written more than twenty years later.
  • Images: prints by Cruikshank and others were political propaganda as much as records.
07

How is it remembered?

  • For more than 150 years there was no public memorial. A blue plaque of 1972 referred only to the "dispersal by the military" of a meeting.
  • A red plaque naming the event as a massacre replaced it in 2007, following a campaign.
  • For the bicentenary in 2019, Jeremy Deller's memorial of stepped stone circles was unveiled. Disability campaigners criticised it because people using wheelchairs could not reach the top, and the council agreed to consider changes.
  • Mike Leigh's film Peterloo (2018) and the bicentenary events brought the story to a new audience.
08

Why does it matter?

Peterloo is one of the foundational moments of British democracy, and its lesson is that the vote was not simply granted from above: it was demanded, at great personal risk, by weavers, spinners and their families who walked for miles to stand in a field. It also left a lasting mark on how the British state polices protest, on the radical press, and on the idea that peaceful assembly is a right rather than a privilege.

  • It helped to build the momentum that led to the Reform Act of 1832 and, later, Chartism.
  • It shows how authorities can treat peaceful protest as a threat to public order — a debate that continues today.
  • The prominent role of women, both as organisers and as casualties, has been recovered by recent research.

Questions to think about

  1. Why do you think it took so long for Manchester to commemorate Peterloo properly?
  2. Is it ever legitimate for the authorities to break up a peaceful protest? Where should the line be drawn?
  3. How much should we trust a memoir written more than twenty years after the event?
  4. What connections can you draw between Peterloo and debates about the right to protest today?

What other events are like this?

  • 1965Bloody Sunday at the Edmund Pettus Bridge — Around six hundred marchers set out from Selma to demand the right to vote. On the Edmund Pettus Bridge, state troopers attacked them with clubs and tear gas.
  • 1976The Soweto Uprising — Thousands of Black schoolchildren marched against being taught in Afrikaans. Police opened fire, and the uprising spread across the country.
  • 1893Women Win the Vote in New Zealand — A nationwide petition campaign made New Zealand the first self-governing country in which all women could vote in parliamentary elections.
