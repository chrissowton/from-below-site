The German Peasants' War and the Twelve Articles

Tens of thousands of peasants rose against their lords, armed with the new ideas of the Reformation and a printed manifesto of rights. It was Europe's largest popular uprising before the French Revolution.

When
1524 – 1525
Where
Memmingen, Swabia, and across southern and central Germany

See it on the map →

01

What is the background to this event?

In the early sixteenth century, most people in the German lands of the Holy Roman Empire were peasants. They owed rents, labour services and tithes to lords, monasteries and bishops, and many were serfs, bound to the land.

  • Lords were tightening their grip: enclosing common woods and pastures, restricting hunting and fishing, and raising dues.
  • Earlier secret peasant leagues, such as the Bundschuh ("laced boot") conspiracies, had been crushed.1
  • From 1517, Martin Luther's challenge to the Catholic Church spread through printed pamphlets. Many peasants heard in the Reformation a message of Christian freedom and equality that applied to their own lives.1
  • Some radical preachers, most famously Thomas Müntzer, went further, calling for the overturning of the social order.1
02

Who were the key people?

  • Sebastian Lotzer and Christoph Schappeler

    A furrier's journeyman and a preacher in Memmingen, generally credited with drafting the Twelve Articles.

  • Thomas Müntzer

    A radical preacher who led the peasant army at Frankenhausen. He was captured, tortured and executed in May 1525.

  • Martin Luther

    The leader of the Reformation, who at first criticised both lords and peasants, then urged the princes to crush the rebellion.

  • Philip of Hesse and George of Saxony

    Princes whose armies destroyed the peasant forces at Frankenhausen.

  • The peasant bands (Haufen)

    Locally organised armies of farmers, labourers and townspeople, who elected their own leaders.

03

What happened?

From the summer of 1524, revolts broke out across south-western Germany. In February 1525, 25 villages around the city of Memmingen rebelled, demanding better economic and political conditions.1 In March, about 50 representatives of the peasant bands of Upper Swabia met in Memmingen and adopted the Twelve Articles.12

  • The Articles demanded that each community should be able to elect and dismiss its own pastor, and that tithes should be used for public purposes.
  • They called for the abolition of serfdom, the restoration of common lands, woods and fishing rights, and limits on labour services, rents and arbitrary justice.1
  • They were printed more than 25,000 times in two months — a huge number for the period — and spread throughout Germany.2

The uprising spread across Swabia, Franconia, Thuringia, Alsace and Austria. At its height, perhaps 300,000 people took part.1 There was violence on both sides:

  • At Weinsberg, at Easter 1525, a peasant band captured the castle and killed Count Ludwig von Helfenstein and other nobles by forcing them to run a gauntlet of pikes. The massacre horrified many, including Luther.1
  • Luther then published Against the Murderous, Thieving Hordes of Peasants, urging the princes to strike the rebels down like a mad dog.1
  • The princes' professional armies, with cavalry and artillery, defeated the peasant bands one by one. At Frankenhausen, on 15 May 1525, Müntzer's army of about 8,000 was routed, and thousands were killed.1

By the end of the war, up to 100,000 peasants had been killed.1

04

What happened next?

  • Repression: Müntzer was executed near Mühlhausen on 27 May 1525. Survivors were fined and many leaders executed; most of the peasants' demands were not met.
  • Some gains: in a few areas, such as parts of the south-west and the Tyrol, lords made concessions and peasants won the right to take disputes to courts.
  • The Reformation changes: Luther's support for the princes helped to tie Lutheranism to state power. Radical reformers, including some veterans of the war, went on to form Anabaptist communities.
05

What do others say?

At the time

The peasants argued that their demands were based on the Gospel and divine justice. The lords and Luther argued that rebellion against lawful authority was sinful, whatever the grievances.

Historians' debates

  • Class war or religious movement? Friedrich Engels, in The Peasant War in Germany (1850), saw it as an early class struggle, and Müntzer as a revolutionary hero. East Germany celebrated him. Other historians emphasise its religious motives and its roots in local disputes.
  • "Revolution of the common man": Peter Blickle argued that peasants and townspeople together sought a new political order based on communities, not lords.
  • Violence: historians acknowledge violence by the peasant bands, such as at Weinsberg and the plundering of castles and monasteries, but note that the killing by the princes' armies was on a far greater scale.
  • Müntzer: some see him as a social revolutionary, others as an apocalyptic preacher whose leadership led the peasants of Frankenhausen to disaster.
06

The evidence

  • The printed Twelve Articles and other peasant programmes, letters and pamphlets.
  • Luther's and Müntzer's writings.
  • Court records and chronicles written mostly by the victors.
07

How is it remembered?

  • The Panorama Museum at Bad Frankenhausen, built by East Germany, houses Werner Tübke's vast painting of the battle, completed in 1987.
  • The 500th anniversary in 2024–25 was marked by exhibitions across Germany, including in Memmingen and Mühlhausen.
08

Why does it matter?

The German Peasants' War was the largest popular uprising in Europe before 1789. The Twelve Articles have been described as one of the first statements of human rights and civil liberties in continental Europe.2

  • It shows how new technology — printing — and new ideas could give ordinary people a voice.
  • It raised questions about freedom, property and authority that would return in the English Revolution and the French Revolution.
  • It is a reminder of how brutally popular movements could be crushed.

Questions to think about

  1. Why do you think printing was so important to the uprising?
  2. Why did Luther turn against the peasants?
  3. Can the violence at Weinsberg be compared with the violence of the princes' armies?
  4. Were the Twelve Articles a statement of human rights?

What other events are like this?

  • 1381The Peasants' Revolt — Tens of thousands of villagers and townspeople marched on London to demand an end to serfdom. For a few days, the government of England was at their mercy.
  • 1649The Diggers of St George's Hill — In the year the king was executed, a small group of poor men and women began to dig and plant common land, declaring the earth 'a common treasury for all'.
  • 1773Pugachev's Rebellion — A Cossack deserter claiming to be the murdered Tsar Peter III proclaimed the end of serfdom. Cossacks, serfs, factory workers and Bashkirs rose in the largest peasant war in Russian history.
