The October Events in Cyprus

Greek Cypriots angry at new taxes and British rule burned the governor's residence in Nicosia. The revolt was crushed, and Britain ruled the island with an iron hand for a decade.

When
21 October – November 1931
Where
Nicosia, Cyprus
Facts checked
2026-11-16

See it on the map →

01

What is the background to this event?

Britain had administered Cyprus since 1878, annexed it in 1914 and made it a crown colony in 1925.1

  • Most Greek Cypriots wanted enosis — union with Greece.1
  • British officials controlled the Legislative Council, and petitions for enosis were rejected.1
  • In September 1931, Governor Ronald Storrs overruled the council to impose new taxes.1
02

Who were the key people?

  • Greek Cypriot students, priests and townspeople

    Who led the protests.

  • Bishop Nikodemos of Kition

    Member of the Legislative Council who called for resistance.

  • Sir Ronald Storrs

    British governor of Cyprus.

  • Alexis Kyrou

    Greek consul in Nicosia, accused by Britain of instigating the revolt.

03

What happened?

On 21 October 1931, about 5,000 Greek Cypriots marched through Nicosia chanting slogans for enosis.1

  • The crowd besieged Government House, and after hours of stone-throwing set it on fire.1
  • British flags were torn down from public buildings across the island and replaced with Greek ones.1
  • British troops were flown in from Cairo, and warships were sent.1
  • By early November, order had been restored.1
04

What happened next?

  • Punishment: seven protesters were killed and thirty injured; ten people were exiled for life, and 2,606 were punished with prison or fines.1
  • "Palmerocracy": under Governor Richmond Palmer, Britain abolished the Legislative Council and ruled autocratically until the Second World War.1
  • Independence: the struggle against British rule resumed in the 1950s; Cyprus became independent in 1960.
05

Why does it matter?

The October Events were the first major revolt against British rule in Cyprus.

  • They also show how anti-colonial movements can be tied to one community's national goal.
  • Seven protesters were killed, ten people were exiled for life, and 2,606 were punished with prison or fines.1
  • Britain abolished the Legislative Council and ruled autocratically until the Second World War.1

What do others say?How it was seen at the time, and where historians disagree

At the time

Britain blamed the Greek consul and nationalist clergy; the protesters saw themselves as fighting colonial rule.1

Debates since

  • Whose nation? The revolt was led by Greek Cypriots for enosis; Turkish Cypriots, about a fifth of the population, did not share that goal, and the issue later divided the island.
  • Violence: rioters burned Government House and attacked property; British forces killed seven protesters and punished thousands.1
How do we know?The evidence, and a source from the time
  • British colonial records and newspaper reports.1

Reading it

A demonstration for enosis — union with Greece. Petitions had already been rejected, and in October 1931 protest turned to revolt. It is worth remembering that a large part of the island's population did not share the demand on the banners.
