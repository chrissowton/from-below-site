The Haitian Revolution

The only revolution in history in which enslaved people overthrew their enslavers and founded an independent state.

01

What is the background to this event?

By the late 1780s the French colony of Saint-Domingue, on the western third of the island of Hispaniola, was the most profitable colony in the world. It produced a large share of the world's sugar and coffee, and its wealth flowed through the ports of Bordeaux and Nantes into the French economy. That wealth rested on a system of extraordinary brutality: roughly half a million enslaved Africans, worked so hard and fed so poorly that planters relied on a constant supply of new captives from Africa simply to maintain their numbers.

Colonial society was sharply divided:

  • Grands blancs — wealthy white planters and merchants, who held political power.
  • Petits blancs — poorer white artisans, overseers and clerks.
  • Gens de couleur libres — free people of colour, many of them property-owners, who were nonetheless denied equal civil rights.
  • The enslaved majority — outnumbering all free people by roughly ten to one.

When the French Revolution broke out in 1789, its language of the "Rights of Man" crossed the Atlantic. Free people of colour demanded equality, and in 1790 a rising led by Vincent Ogé was crushed and its leaders executed. The enslaved population had its own sources of solidarity, however: Vodou religious networks, communities of escaped "maroons" in the mountains, and a long memory of resistance. Even before the revolution, the French writer Guillaume Raynal had warned that the enslaved "only want a chief, sufficiently courageous, to lead them on to vengeance and slaughter."

02

Who were the key people?

  • Dutty Boukman

    A Vodou priest (houngan) and enslaved driver who, according to tradition, led the ceremony at Bois Caïman and the first stage of the rising. He was killed by colonial forces in November 1791.

  • Cécile Fatiman

    A Vodou priestess (mambo) remembered as officiating alongside Boukman at Bois Caïman.

  • Toussaint Louverture

    Born into slavery and freed in the 1770s, he became the revolution's outstanding general and statesman. Seized by the French under a flag of truce in 1802, he died in the Fort de Joux in the Jura mountains in April 1803.

  • Jean-Jacques Dessalines

    A formerly enslaved labourer who rose to command the revolutionary army in the final war of independence and proclaimed Haiti's independence in 1804.

  • Jean-Baptiste Belley

    Born in Africa and formerly enslaved, he was elected as a deputy for Saint-Domingue to the National Convention in Paris, where he argued for the abolition of slavery in 1794.

  • Sanité Bélair

    A free woman of colour who served as a sergeant and lieutenant in the revolutionary army and was executed by the French in 1802. She is one of the few women whose names survive from the fighting.

  • The rank and file

    Tens of thousands of field workers, domestic servants, craftsmen and maroons, nearly all of them unnamed in the surviving records, whose decision to rise and to keep fighting for twelve years made every leader possible.

03

What happened?

On the night of 14 August 1791, enslaved representatives from plantations across the Northern Plain met in a wooded area known as Bois Caïman, near Le Cap. According to the earliest accounts, the gathering combined a political meeting with a Vodou ceremony at which participants swore to fight for their freedom.2 One week later, on the night of 21–22 August, the rising began.

The scale and speed of the revolt astonished the colonists:

  • Within weeks, around 100,000 enslaved people in the north had joined the insurrection.3
  • Within two months, some 180 sugar plantations and hundreds of coffee and indigo plantations had been burned or destroyed.3
  • White militias retaliated with mass killings of Black people, and the conflict quickly became a war.

What followed was a twelve-year struggle in which the revolutionaries fought, at different times, against the French colonial authorities, Spanish forces from neighbouring Santo Domingo, and a British invasion force. In August 1793 the French commissioner Léger-Félicité Sonthonax, unable to hold the colony without the support of the insurgents, proclaimed the abolition of slavery in the north. On 4 February 1794 the National Convention in Paris extended this to all French colonies, decreeing that "slavery of the Blacks is abolished in all the colonies."4

By 1801 Toussaint Louverture governed the colony and had issued a constitution. In a letter of 1792 he had written:

"For too long we have borne your chains without thinking of shaking them off, but any authority which is not founded on virtue and humanity, and which only tends to subject one's fellow man to slavery, must come to an end."Toussaint Louverture, letter to the General Assembly, 17925

In 1802 Napoleon Bonaparte sent a large expedition under his brother-in-law, General Charles Leclerc, to restore French control — and, as soon became clear, slavery. Leclerc landed at Le Cap in February 1802 and declared Toussaint an outlaw.6 Toussaint was later seized under a flag of truce and deported to France. Resistance continued under Dessalines and Henri Christophe, using a deliberate scorched-earth strategy that Toussaint had described in a letter to Dessalines:

"Bear in mind that the soil bathed with our sweat must not furnish our enemies with the smallest aliment."Toussaint Louverture, letter to Dessalines, 18025

Yellow fever devastated the French army, and Leclerc himself died of it in November 1802. On 18 November 1803 the revolutionary army defeated the French at the Battle of Vertières, near Cap-Haïtien. On 1 January 1804, at Gonaïves, Dessalines declared the independence of the new state, giving it the Taíno name Haiti.7

04

What happened next?

Independence did not bring security. The great powers treated Haiti as a dangerous example:

  • The United States refused to recognise Haiti diplomatically until 1862.
  • In 1825, with warships off the coast, France demanded an indemnity of 150 million francs — later reduced to 90 million — to compensate former slaveholders for the loss of their "property" in exchange for recognition. Haiti borrowed heavily from French banks to pay it, and the debt and its interest burdened the country's finances for generations.
  • Internally, the new state was shaped by war, by the assassination of Dessalines in 1806, and by a division between a northern kingdom under Henri Christophe and a southern republic under Alexandre Pétion.

Haiti nonetheless offered shelter and material support to independence movements elsewhere. In 1815–16 Pétion supplied Simón Bolívar with arms and men, on condition that Bolívar abolish slavery in the lands he liberated.

05

Why does it matter?

For a long time the story of abolition was told chiefly as one of humane reformers in Europe and North America. The Haitian Revolution complicates that story profoundly: enslaved people freed themselves through organisation, sacrifice and armed struggle, and in doing so forced the French Republic to make its own promise of universal rights real. The anthropologist Michel-Rolph Trouillot argued that the revolution was long "silenced" in Western history precisely because it was so difficult for contemporaries to imagine.

  • It was the first successful revolution led by enslaved people and created the first Black-led republic in the Americas.
  • It shaped the politics of slavery throughout the Atlantic world, inspiring the enslaved and frightening slaveholders from Virginia to Brazil.
  • The 1825 indemnity has become central to contemporary debates about reparations and the long-term economic consequences of colonialism.

What do others say?How it was seen at the time, and where historians disagree

At the time

To planters and to most European and North American governments, the rising was an unprecedented catastrophe. Colonial accounts dwelt almost entirely on the killing of white colonists and the burning of property, and the revolution was widely presented as proof that emancipation would lead to chaos. Supporters of slavery in the United States and Britain used it as an argument against abolition for decades.

Historians' debates

  • How far was it a 'French' revolution? Older accounts treated events in Saint-Domingue as an offshoot of the French Revolution. Historians such as Carolyn Fick and Laurent Dubois have stressed instead the independent aims and political ideas of the enslaved, including African political traditions brought by people who had been born in Africa.
  • Bois Caïman: the ceremony is central to Haitian national memory, but the earliest detailed account was published in 1814 by a hostile colonist, Antoine Dalmas, and historians continue to debate its date, location and details.
  • Leadership: some historians argue that Toussaint Louverture's later policies — including forcing freed people back to work on plantations under strict labour codes — show the tensions between the leaders and the rank and file who wanted land of their own.

Uncomfortable questions

The revolution involved extreme violence on all sides, including the massacre of much of the remaining white population on Dessalines's orders in 1804. Historians differ on how to weigh this against the far greater and sustained violence of slavery itself, and against the French expedition's own atrocities in 1802–03.

How do we know?The evidence, and a source from the time

Most surviving written sources were produced by colonists, officials and soldiers — people hostile to the revolution. Few of the enslaved could write, so their voices survive mainly indirectly:

  • Letters and proclamations by leaders such as Toussaint Louverture, many dictated to secretaries.
  • Interrogation records of captured rebels and colonial correspondence held in French archives.
  • Later Haitian oral tradition, which preserved memories of Bois Caïman and of individual fighters.

Historians have increasingly read colonial documents "against the grain" to recover the actions and aims of ordinary participants.

Reading it

The artist was far from Saint-Domingue and worked from reports reaching Europe, so the picture says as much about European fears as about the revolution itself. Look at how the rebels are drawn, and think about who would have bought such an image. An enslaved person in Saint-Domingue would surely have chosen a very different scene.

How is it remembered?Memorials, anniversaries and arguments over memory
  • In Haiti, 1 January (Independence Day) and 18 November (the anniversary of Vertières) are national holidays.
  • The site of Bois Caïman is marked and visited, and the ceremony remains an important national symbol, though some evangelical preachers have attacked it as un-Christian.
  • In 1998 UNESCO chose 23 August — the night of the 1791 rising — as the International Day for the Remembrance of the Slave Trade and its Abolition.
  • Outside Haiti, the revolution was for long little taught; C.L.R. James's The Black Jacobins (1938) did much to change that.
