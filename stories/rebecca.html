The Rebecca Riots

At night, Welsh farmers with blackened faces and women's clothes smashed the toll-gates that taxed every journey to market. They called themselves 'Rebecca and her daughters'.

When
1839 – 1843
Where
Efailwen and south-west Wales, United Kingdom
Facts checked

01

What is the background to this event?

In the late 1830s and early 1840s, the farming communities of west Wales were in deep poverty.1

  • Bad harvests, then falling prices for grain and cattle, cut farmers' incomes.1
  • But rents, tithes, local rates and road tolls stayed high.1
  • Private turnpike trusts put up toll-gates on many roads, so farmers had to pay to take lime, crops and animals to market.

In their own words

03

What happened?

From 1839, groups of men, often dressed as women, destroyed toll-gates across west and mid Wales.1

  • The rioters called themselves Merched Beca — "Rebecca's daughters".1
  • Toll-gates were seen as a visible symbol of unfair taxes.1
  • In July 1843, about 200 men destroyed the Bolgoed toll-gate near Pontarddulais.1
  • That summer, letters signed by "Rebecca" began targeting landlords and calling for fair rents.1

04

What happened next?

  • The end: by late 1843, the riots had stopped, because of more troops, the protesters' wish to avoid violence, and criminals using Rebecca's name.1
  • Reform: in 1844, Parliament passed an act to reform the turnpike trusts in Wales.1
05

What do others say?

At the time

The authorities saw lawless mobs; the London Times sent a reporter who wrote sympathetically about the farmers' grievances.2

Debates since

  • The name: it is often said to come from a verse in Genesis about Rebekah's descendants possessing "the gate of those which hate them".2
  • Violence: the rioters mostly destroyed property, but at Hendy in September 1843 the toll-keeper Sarah Williams was killed; criminals also used the Rebecca name.1
06

The evidence

  • Newspaper reports, including the Illustrated London News and The Times.2
  • Court records and Rebecca's letters.1

07

How is it remembered?

  • Carvings and memorials in Carmarthenshire mark the riots, and "Rebecca" became a symbol of Welsh protest.
08

Why does it matter?

The Rebecca Riots were a leaderless rising of poor farmers against an unfair system, and won real reform.1

  • They drew on Welsh traditions of community justice.

Questions to think about

  1. Why did toll-gates become the target?

What other events are like this?

  • 1830The Swing Riots — Farm labourers across southern England smashed the threshing machines that took their winter work, and sent threatening letters signed by a mythical 'Captain Swing'. The state answered with hangings and transportation.
  • 1882The Crofters' War — Highland crofters, cleared from their ancestors' land to make way for sheep and deer, refused rent, occupied land and fought the police. Their land league won them the right not to be evicted.
  • 1549Kett's Rebellion — Villagers tearing down a landlord's fences asked a farmer to join them. He pulled down his own fences and led 16,000 people to camp outside Norwich, where they ran their own affairs for six weeks.
