The Tulip Revolution

After disputed elections, protesters took over towns in southern Kyrgyzstan and then marched on the capital. The president fled to Moscow — but the revolution was followed by looting, and its leader was himself overthrown five years later.

When
February – April 2005
Where
Jalal-Abad, Osh and Bishkek, Kyrgyzstan
Facts checked

01

What is the background to this event?

Askar Akayev, a physicist, had led Kyrgyzstan since independence from the Soviet Union.1

  • Opponents accused Akayev, his family and his supporters of corruption and authoritarian rule.1
  • In the parliamentary elections of 27 February and 13 March 2005, pro-Akayev candidates did well. Foreign observers, including the OSCE, criticised the vote.1
  • On 3 March, a bomb exploded in the flat of the opposition leader Roza Otunbayeva. The government denied responsibility.1

In their own words

03

What happened?

Protests began in the south and west, in Jalal-Abad, Osh and Uzgen.1

  • On 19 March, about 50,000 people protested in Jalal-Abad and 3,000 in Bishkek.1
  • On 20 March, protesters occupied government buildings and took control of the large cities of the south. They demanded Akayev's resignation.1
  • The opposition was divided and had no single leader, unlike the movements in Georgia and Ukraine. On 22 March, its leaders formed an interim government.1
  • On 24 March, protesters entered Bishkek and Akayev fled with his family, first to Kazakhstan and then to Russia. Police melted away or joined the protesters, and imprisoned opposition leaders were released.1
  • That night and over the following days, mobs looted shops and cash machines in Bishkek and set buildings on fire. Three people died in the unrest.1
  • Akayev signed his resignation in Moscow on 4 April.1

04

What happened next?

  • A new president: Bakiyev won about 90% of the vote in the presidential election of 10 July 2005.1
  • Another revolution: in April 2010, amid anger over corruption, cronyism and rising utility prices, Bakiyev was himself overthrown.2
05

What do others say?

At the time

It was Akayev himself who coined the name 'Tulip Revolution', in a speech warning that no 'colour revolution' should happen in Kyrgyzstan.1

Debates since

  • From below? According to The Wall Street Journal, US government agencies funded the only opposition print outlet; a Georgian politician advised the opposition on non-violent tactics. Others stress the role of local and regional networks in the south.1
  • Violence: the change of power was quick, but three people died in the unrest and looting.1
06

The evidence

  • Election observers' reports, press coverage, and Akayev's diaries, produced as evidence of corruption.1

07

How is it remembered?

  • The events of March 2005 are now often called the First Kyrgyz Revolution, followed by those of 2010.12
08

Why does it matter?

The Tulip Revolution was the first of the colour revolutions in Central Asia. Its messy aftermath — looting, a divided opposition, and a new president who also fell — fed debate about how much such revolutions really changed.12

Questions to think about

  1. Why did the revolution begin in the south rather than the capital?
  2. Why did police 'melt away' rather than fight?
  3. Was the Tulip Revolution a revolution, or a change of rulers?

What other events are like this?

  • 2004The Orange Revolution — When a presidential run-off was rigged, hundreds of thousands of Ukrainians camped in the snow in central Kyiv for weeks. The Supreme Court annulled the result, and the election was held again.
  • 2003The Rose Revolution — After a rigged election, Georgians filled the streets for three weeks. Protesters carrying roses entered parliament, and the president resigned without a shot being fired.
  • 2005The Andijan Massacre — Thousands gathered in a city square to protest against poverty and corruption, after armed men freed prisoners from the local jail. Government troops sealed off the square and opened fire.
