The Salt March

A 24-day march to the sea to make salt in defiance of British law set off a campaign of civil disobedience across India.

When
12 March – 6 April 1930
Where
Sabarmati Ashram to Dandi, Gujarat, India
Facts checked

01

What is the background to this event?

Under the Salt Act of 1882, the British government in India held a monopoly on the manufacture and sale of salt and levied a tax on it. Making salt from seawater without a licence was a criminal offence. Because salt is a necessity, especially for labourers in a hot climate, the tax fell heaviest on the poor.

On 26 January 1930 the Indian National Congress publicly adopted the goal of Purna Swaraj — complete self-rule — and entrusted Mohandas Gandhi with launching a campaign of civil disobedience. His choice of the salt tax as the target was greeted with scepticism:

  • The newspaper The Statesman wrote: "It is difficult not to laugh, and we imagine that will be the mood of most thinking Indians."
  • The Viceroy, Lord Irwin, wrote to London: "At present, the prospect of a salt campaign does not keep me awake at night."

Gandhi's reasoning was that salt united every Indian, regardless of religion, caste or region. As he put it: "Next to air and water, salt is perhaps the greatest necessity of life."

In their own words

03

What happened?

On 12 March 1930 Gandhi set out from his ashram at Sabarmati, near Ahmedabad, with 78 selected followers. Over 24 days they walked about 240 miles (385 km) to the coastal village of Dandi, stopping in villages along the way where Gandhi spoke to large crowds. Numbers grew as people joined them.1

The march was followed closely by the Indian and international press. Gandhi spoke repeatedly of the struggle's moral stakes: "I want world sympathy in this battle of right against might."1 On the morning of 6 April, after prayers, he picked up a lump of salty mud from the shore and declared:

"With this, I am shaking the foundations of the British Empire."M.K. Gandhi at Dandi, 6 April 19301

The act was symbolic, but what followed was not. Across India, people began to make and sell salt illegally, boycott British cloth and liquor, and resign from government posts. Jawaharlal Nehru remarked that "it seemed as though a spring had been suddenly released."1 Women took part on a scale the authorities had not anticipated; a government report noted that "thousands of them emerged ... from the seclusion of their homes" to join demonstrations and picketing.1

Gandhi was arrested on the night of 4–5 May 1930. On 21 May, Sarojini Naidu led hundreds of volunteers to the Dharasana Salt Works. Column after column advanced towards the police cordon without resisting and were beaten down with steel-tipped lathis.2 The American journalist Webb Miller, whose report was initially censored, wrote that at the makeshift hospital he:

"counted 320 injured, many still insensible with fractured skulls ... Scores of the injured had received no treatment for hours and two had died."Webb Miller, United Press report, May 19303

The former Speaker of the Legislative Assembly, Vithalbhai Patel, who watched the beatings, said: "All hope of reconciling India with the British Empire is lost forever."1 By the end of 1930, around 60,000 people had been jailed.2

04

What happened next?

  • Repression and resistance: the civil disobedience campaign continued into 1931 and, in a second phase, until 1934. In Peshawar on 23 April 1930, troops fired on unarmed followers of Abdul Ghaffar Khan in the Qissa Khwani Bazaar.
  • International attention: Time magazine named Gandhi its "Man of the Year" for 1930, comparing the march to the American colonists' defiance of the British tea tax.
  • Negotiation: in March 1931 the Gandhi–Irwin Pact released political prisoners and allowed coastal villagers to make salt for their own use; Gandhi attended the Second Round Table Conference in London later that year.

Independence did not come until 1947, accompanied by the partition of British India and terrible communal violence. The salt tax itself was finally abolished on the eve of independence.

05

What do others say?

At the time

The British authorities initially dismissed the march as theatrical and expected it to fail. Some Indian critics, including within the Congress, doubted that salt was a serious enough issue. Others, such as B.R. Ambedkar, were sceptical of Gandhi's leadership more generally, arguing that the independence movement was not adequately addressing caste oppression.

Historians' debates

  • How effective was it? The campaign did not win independence directly, and the Gandhi–Irwin Pact of 1931 was seen by some contemporaries as a disappointing compromise.
  • Who participated? Historians such as Sumit Sarkar have shown that participation varied greatly by region and class, and that some local movements went beyond Gandhi's strict non-violence.
  • Non-violence and its limits: some argue that non-violence succeeded because of the publicity it generated in Britain and the United States; others stress the importance of mass economic pressure such as the boycott of British goods.
  • Gandhi's attitude to women: Gandhi initially excluded women from the march itself, which activists such as Kamaladevi Chattopadhyay challenged.

How is it remembered?

  • The National Salt Satyagraha Memorial at Dandi opened in 2019, with statues of Gandhi and the 80 marchers.
  • The march has been re-enacted on major anniversaries, including the 75th anniversary in 2005.
  • Richard Attenborough's film Gandhi (1982) depicted the march and the beatings at Dharasana.
08

Why does it matter?

The Salt March showed how a carefully chosen, simple, symbolic act could unite millions of people across deep divisions. It made non-violent mass civil disobedience a recognised political strategy on the world stage, and directly influenced Martin Luther King Jr. and the American civil rights movement.

  • It transformed the independence movement into a genuinely mass movement, with unprecedented participation by women.
  • The events at Dharasana demonstrated, before an international audience, the moral cost of colonial rule.
  • Gandhi is the central figure in the story, but it was the millions who made salt themselves who gave the march its force.

Questions to think about

  2. How important was the international press to the success of the campaign?
  3. Can non-violent resistance work against every kind of government?
