Vanuatu's Road to Independence

Ruled jointly by Britain and France, the islanders of the New Hebrides built their own movement for independence. A former Anglican priest led them to freedom in 1980 — after a rebellion on Santo backed by foreign interests.

When
1971 – 1980
Where
Port Vila and Espiritu Santo, New Hebrides (now Vanuatu)

See it on the map →

01

What is the background to this event?

From 1906, the New Hebrides were ruled jointly by Britain and France as a "condominium", with two governments, two police forces and two school systems.

  • Much of the land had been taken by European planters and speculators.
  • In 1966, chiefs on Espiritu Santo founded the Nagriamel movement, which demanded the return of unused land held by Europeans.2
02

Who were the key people?

  • Walter Lini

    An Anglican priest who founded the New Hebrides National Party and became the first prime minister.

  • Jimmy Stevens

    Leader of the Nagriamel movement on Santo.

  • Chief Buluk

    A Santo chief who convened the founding meeting of Nagriamel.

  • The Phoenix Foundation

    A US libertarian group that backed the Santo secession.

03

What happened?

On 17 August 1971, Walter Lini founded the New Hebridean Cultural Association, soon renamed the New Hebrides National Party.1

  • The party campaigned for independence and for land to be returned to its customary owners.
  • It won the 1975 elections, and in 1977 renamed itself the Vanua'aku Pati — "Party of Our Land".1
  • The party boycotted bodies it saw as unfair and organised a "People's Provisional Government" in 1977.
  • It won the 1979 elections, and Lini became chief minister.1
  • On the eve of independence in 1980, Nagriamel, backed by French settlers and the Phoenix Foundation, declared the "Republic of Vemerana" on Santo.2
  • Vanuatu became independent on 30 July 1980. Papua New Guinean soldiers and Vanua'aku supporters put down the Santo rebellion weeks later; reprisals against Nagriamel supporters followed.2

04

What happened next?

  • Land: the constitution returned land to its customary owners.
  • Lini led Vanuatu until 1991, following a non-aligned foreign policy.1
  • Stevens was jailed for his part in the rebellion.
05

What do others say?

At the time

Britain largely supported early independence; French officials and settlers were more hostile, and some backed the Santo rebels.2

Debates since

  • Nagriamel: some see it as a genuine movement for land and custom, manipulated by outsiders; others as a front for foreign interests.2
  • Violence: the rebellion and its suppression were relatively small in scale, but reprisals against Nagriamel supporters caused lasting bitterness.2
06

The evidence

  • Records of the condominium and the independence talks.
  • Party newspapers and oral histories.
07

How is it remembered?

  • 30 July is Vanuatu's Independence Day.
08

Why does it matter?

Vanuatu's independence movement united islanders divided by two colonial powers and many languages, and put land back in the hands of its people.

  • The Santo rebellion shows how outside interests can exploit local grievances.

Questions to think about

  1. What problems did rule by two colonial powers create?
  2. Why was land so central to the independence movement?
  3. Why did Nagriamel oppose independence under Lini?
  4. Was the use of force against the Santo rebellion justified?

  • 1947Maasina Ruru — the Rule of Brotherhood — Islanders who had worked alongside American troops in the war came home determined to govern themselves. They refused to pay taxes or work on plantations, built their own villages and courts — and filled the colonial jails.
