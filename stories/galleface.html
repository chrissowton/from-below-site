The Galle Face Satyagraha and the 'Sinhala Only' Act

When Ceylon's parliament made Sinhala the only official language, Tamil leaders sat in peaceful protest outside. A mob attacked them — and the violence that followed was the first step on the road to civil war.

When
5 June 1956
Where
Galle Face Green, Colombo, Sri Lanka

See it on the map →

01

What is the background to this event?

Ceylon (now Sri Lanka) became independent from Britain in 1948. Its population was mainly Sinhalese, mostly Sinhala-speaking Buddhists, with a large Tamil-speaking minority — Sri Lankan Tamils in the north and east, Muslims, and the "Indian" Tamils of the tea plantations.

  • Under British rule, English was the language of government, and English-educated people — Sinhalese and Tamil — dominated public service. Tamils, partly because of mission schools in Jaffna, held a large share of government jobs.1
  • Many Sinhalese felt that their language, religion and culture had been neglected under colonial rule. Populist Sinhalese politicians promised to raise the status of the majority.1
  • In the April 1956 election, S. W. R. D. Bandaranaike's Sri Lanka Freedom Party won a landslide on a promise to make Sinhala the sole official language — "Sinhala Only" — within 24 hours.1
  • Bandaranaike intended to allow the "reasonable use" of Tamil in the north and east, but under pressure from Sinhalese nationalists, including a fast unto death outside parliament, he dropped these provisions.1
02

Who were the key people?

  • S. J. V. Chelvanayakam

    Leader of the Federal Party, the main Tamil party, which campaigned for a federal state and for equal status for Tamil.

  • The Federal Party satyagrahis

    Tamil MPs and supporters who staged a peaceful protest on Galle Face Green.

  • S. W. R. D. Bandaranaike

    Prime Minister of Ceylon, whose government passed the Official Language Act. He was assassinated in 1959 by a Buddhist monk.

  • The victims

    Tamils killed, injured or driven from their homes in the riots that followed, and Sinhalese victims of reprisals in the east.

03

What happened?

The Federal Party's working committee met in Jaffna and decided to oppose the Language Bill with a satyagraha — a peaceful sit-in protest in the tradition of Gandhi.1 On 5 June 1956, as Parliament debated the Official Language Bill, Chelvanayakam and around two hundred Federal Party volunteers sat down on Galle Face Green, opposite the Parliament building in Colombo.

  • A crowd of Sinhalese counter-demonstrators attacked the satyagrahis while the police looked on. Several protesters were beaten, and some were thrown into the nearby Beira Lake.
  • The violence spread into Colombo, where Tamil-owned shops and Tamils in the streets were attacked.1
  • The Official Language Act — the "Sinhala Only" Act — was passed by Parliament the same month.

The violence then spread to the east of the island. In Batticaloa, there was anti-Sinhalese rioting; in the Gal Oya valley, where the government had settled Sinhalese colonists on land Tamils considered part of their homeland, Sinhalese settlers and workers attacked Tamils. Over 150 people, the vast majority of them Tamils, were killed before the police and army brought the situation under control.1

04

What happened next?

  • A broken pact: in 1957, Bandaranaike and Chelvanayakam signed a pact promising regional councils and the use of Tamil in the north and east. Under pressure from Sinhalese nationalists and Buddhist monks, Bandaranaike abandoned it in 1958.
  • 1958: anti-Tamil riots in 1958 killed hundreds of people. A Tamil Language (Special Provisions) Act was passed later that year, but was not fully implemented for many years.
  • Escalation: discrimination in jobs and university admissions, and further violence, especially the anti-Tamil pogrom of 1983, led many young Tamils to turn to armed militancy. A civil war between the government and the Liberation Tigers of Tamil Eelam lasted from 1983 to 2009, with atrocities on both sides and tens of thousands of civilian deaths.
  • Recognition: in 1987, Tamil was made an official language alongside Sinhala, with English as a link language.
05

What do others say?

At the time

Sinhalese nationalists argued that Sinhala Only would correct the injustices of colonial rule and give the majority its rightful place. Tamil leaders argued that it would make Tamils second-class citizens in their own country, and asked for parity of status for Tamil.

Historians' debates

  • Colonial legacy or majoritarianism? Historians debate how far the conflict grew out of inequalities created under British rule, and how far it was driven by politicians exploiting ethnic fears for votes.
  • Violence on all sides: the 1956 riots involved anti-Tamil violence in Colombo and Gal Oya and anti-Sinhalese violence in Batticaloa. The later civil war saw grave abuses by both the Sri Lankan armed forces and the LTTE, including attacks on civilians, which historians and the United Nations have documented.
  • Could it have been different? Many historians see the abandonment of the 1957 pact as a lost opportunity for a peaceful settlement.

07

How is it remembered?

  • The Galle Face satyagraha is remembered by Tamils as a moment when peaceful protest was met with violence.
  • Galle Face Green was again the site of mass protest in 2022, when the "Aragalaya" movement forced President Gotabaya Rajapaksa from office.
  • Chelvanayakam, known as "Thanthai Chelva" (Father Chelva), is commemorated in Jaffna.
08

Why does it matter?

The Sinhala Only Act and the attack on the Galle Face satyagraha were turning points in Sri Lanka's history. A law about language, and the violence that met peaceful protest against it, began the breakdown of trust between communities that led, a generation later, to civil war.

  • It shows how language policy can become a matter of life and death.
  • It shows the importance of protecting peaceful protest by minorities.
  • It is a warning of how majoritarian nationalism can damage a democracy.

Questions to think about

  1. Why do you think language became such an explosive political issue in Ceylon?
  2. Why did the Federal Party choose a satyagraha as its form of protest?
  3. How can a democracy protect the rights of minorities when the majority wants a different policy?
  4. Could the civil war have been avoided? What might have made a difference?

What other events are like this?

  • 1965The Anti-Hindi Agitation in Tamil Nadu — When Hindi was due to become India's sole official language, Tamil students rose in protest. Months of unrest, police shootings and self-immolations forced the government to keep English — and changed Indian politics.
  • 1952The Bengali Language Movement — Students in Dhaka defied a ban on protest to demand that Bengali be a national language of Pakistan. Police opened fire. The date is now International Mother Language Day.
  • 1976The Soweto Uprising — Thousands of Black schoolchildren marched against being taught in Afrikaans. Police opened fire, and the uprising spread across the country.
