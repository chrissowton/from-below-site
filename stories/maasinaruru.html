Maasina Ruru — the Rule of Brotherhood

Islanders who had worked alongside American troops in the war came home determined to govern themselves. They refused to pay taxes or work on plantations, built their own villages and courts — and filled the colonial jails.

When
1944 – 1952
Where
Malaita, British Solomon Islands
Facts checked
2026-11-16

See it on the map →

01

What is the background to this event?

The Solomon Islands were a British protectorate. Islanders, especially from Malaita, worked for very low pay on plantations owned by Europeans.

  • During the Second World War, thousands of Solomon Islanders joined the Solomon Islands Labour Corps, working for the US forces.1
  • They were struck by the friendliness and generosity of American soldiers, including African Americans, compared with the plantation owners.1
  • Earlier movements, and sympathetic missionaries such as Richard Fallowes, had already called for islanders to have a say.1

03

What happened?

Men who had worked together in the Labour Corps founded Maasina Ruru in about 1944. The name, from the 'Are'are language, means the rule of the relationship between brothers and sisters.1

  • The movement spread across Malaita and to other islands. People moved into large new villages, appointed their own chiefs, wrote down customary law and held their own courts.
  • They refused to work on plantations for the old wages and stopped co-operating with the government.1
  • Meetings drew thousands of people.1
  • In 1947, the British launched "Operation De-Louse" and arrested the leaders. The nine head chiefs were charged with sedition, and most were sentenced to six and a half years' hard labour.1
  • The civil disobedience continued. Villages refused to pay tax, and some were barricaded. In "Operation Jericho", about 2,000 people were arrested on Malaita alone.1

04

What happened next?

  • A settlement: the British released the leaders in the early 1950s and agreed to an elected Malaita Council, which first met in 1953.1
  • Independence: the movement is credited with starting the path to self-government. Solomon Islands became independent in 1978.1
05

What do others say?

At the time

British officials called it "Marching Rule" and dismissed it as a "cargo cult" or a Communist plot. Its leaders said it was about dignity, fair wages and self-government.13

Debates since

  • Cargo cult? Historians such as David Akin argue that the "cargo cult" label was used to deny that it was a rational political movement.3
  • Pressure: the movement was non-violent towards the British, but some people who refused to join faced fines and pressure from its chiefs.3
06

The evidence

  • Colonial reports and trial records.
  • Jonathan Fifi'i's memoir and oral histories.2
07

How is it remembered?

  • Maasina Ruru is remembered with pride on Malaita, and its leaders are honoured as founders of the nation.
08

Why does it matter?

Maasina Ruru was one of the most successful campaigns of non-violent non-co-operation in the Pacific.

  • It united people who had been divided by language and religion.
  • It defended custom (kastom) as a basis for self-rule.
