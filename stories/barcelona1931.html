The Barcelona Rent Strike of 1931

With unemployment soaring after the 1929 Exposition, 100,000 working-class families in Barcelona stopped paying rent. When bailiffs carried their furniture into the street, neighbours carried it back in.

When
July – December 1931
Where
Bon Pastor and the 'cheap houses', Barcelona, Spain
Facts checked
2026-11-16

01

What is the background to this event?

In the 1920s, building works for the 1929 International Exposition — including the metro and the development of Montjuïc — drew hundreds of thousands of workers to Barcelona. The city's population grew from 600,000 to over a million.1

  • No housing plan was made for them, and rents soared. Many lived in self-built slums; some were moved into privately owned "cheap houses" (casas baratas) in neighbourhoods such as Bon Pastor and Baró de Viver.1
  • After the Exposition ended and the Great Depression struck, unemployment rose sharply.1
  • In April 1931, Spain became a republic, raising hopes of social change.
  • The anarcho-syndicalist union, the CNT, was the most powerful workers' organisation in Barcelona.

Who were the key people?

  • The Committee for Economic Defence

    Set up by the CNT's construction workers' union to organise the strike.

  • Victoria Ruiz Rodríguez and Dolores Maldonado Ruiz

    Women who led neighbourhood resistance to evictions in Ferrer i Guàrdia.

  • Tenants of the casas baratas

    The families at the heart of the strike.

  • The Chamber of Urban Property

    The landlords' organisation, which opposed the strike.

03

What happened?

When negotiations with landlords and the city council failed, the Committee for Economic Defence called on tenants to stop paying rent.1 Their demands included a 40 per cent cut in rents and no rent for those without income.1

  • The strike was called on 5 July 1931. By August, 100,000 families were taking part, especially in the casas baratas.1
  • Landlords responded with a huge wave of evictions. Tenants created a resistance fund to support those without income.1
  • When bailiffs carried a family's furniture into the street, neighbours carried it back into the house.1
  • Women led the neighbourhood organisation, especially in Ferrer i Guàrdia, and managed to stop evictions.1

The strike overlapped with a telephone strike and a CNT general strike in September. It was fiercely repressed, with 18 deaths and dozens injured and arrested.1 It ended in December 1931 after the strike committee was imprisoned, though it continued on and off the following year.1

04

What happened next?

  • Small gains: some tenants reached agreements with small landlords for lower rents.1
  • Revolution and war: in 1936, when the Spanish Civil War began, Barcelona's workers took over much of the city; rents were cut and housing collectivised. After Franco's victory in 1939, the CNT was crushed.
  • Today: the casas baratas of Bon Pastor were demolished in the 2000s and 2010s. Barcelona has again seen tenants' unions and campaigns against evictions.
05

What do others say?

At the time

Landlords and the authorities saw the strike as anarchist subversion. Tenants saw it as defending their homes against unaffordable rents.

Historians' debates

  • Neighbourhood or union? Historians such as Chris Ealham show how the strike was rooted in neighbourhood solidarity as well as in the CNT.
  • The Republic: the strike revealed tensions between the new Republic's government and the anarchist movement, which would grow in the following years.
  • Violence: the deaths came mostly from police repression of the strike and the general strike.

How is it remembered?

  • A museum house in Bon Pastor preserves one of the casas baratas and tells the neighbourhood's history.
08

Why does it matter?

The Barcelona rent strike was one of the largest rent strikes in history. It showed how neighbourhoods — and especially women — could organise as powerfully as workplaces.

  • It connects to struggles over housing in cities around the world.
  • It shows the power of direct action and mutual aid.

Questions to think about

  1. Why did rents rise so steeply in Barcelona?
  2. How did neighbours resist evictions?
  3. Why were women so important in the strike?

What other events are like this?

  • 1907The Tenants' Strike and the March of the Brooms — When rents soared, tenants in Buenos Aires's crowded tenements refused to pay. Women and children marched with brooms to 'sweep away the landlords'.
  • 1915The Glasgow Rent Strikes — Working-class women in wartime Glasgow refused to pay rent rises and physically blocked evictions — forcing the government to introduce rent controls.
  • 1978Crossroads: The Squatters Who Defied Apartheid — Under apartheid, Black families were not allowed to live together in Cape Town. At Crossroads, thousands built homes anyway — and women led the fight to stop the bulldozers.
