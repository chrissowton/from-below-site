Mexico 1968 and the Tlatelolco Massacre

Ten days before the Olympic Games, soldiers and secret agents opened fire on thousands of students gathered peacefully in a Mexico City square. For decades, the government denied what had happened.

When
July – 2 October 1968
Where
Plaza de las Tres Culturas, Tlatelolco, Mexico City

01

What is the background to this event?

Since 1929, Mexico had been ruled by one party, the Institutional Revolutionary Party (PRI). It presented itself as the heir of the Mexican Revolution, but repressed independent unions, peasant leaders and opponents.1

  • In 1958–59, the railway workers' leader Demetrio Vallejo was arrested; in 1962, the peasant leader Rubén Jaramillo was murdered with his family.1
  • Mexico City was to host the Olympic Games in October 1968, and President Gustavo Díaz Ordaz was determined to show a modern, orderly country.
  • In July 1968, police violently broke up a fight between student groups, and students formed a National Strike Council (CNH) to organise protests and present demands.1

03

What happened?

Over the summer, the protests grew. Students demanded the release of political prisoners, the abolition of the riot police, and the repeal of a law on "social dissolution" used against opponents. Huge marches filled the capital, and in September the army occupied the National University.

On 2 October 1968, around 10,000 students and others gathered in the Plaza de las Tres Culturas in Tlatelolco to listen to speeches.1

  • Flares were fired from a helicopter as 5,000 soldiers and armoured vehicles surrounded the square.1
  • The Olympia Battalion, a secret force of soldiers, police and agents, moved in to arrest the student leaders.1
  • Gunmen in surrounding buildings opened fire on the crowd. Government documents released since 2000 suggest that government snipers fired on both the crowd and the troops.1
  • Some 1,345 people were arrested.1

The government claimed the soldiers had been provoked by student gunmen. The number of dead is still disputed: researcher Kate Doyle documented 44 deaths, but estimates range from 300 to 400.1 The Olympic Games opened ten days later as planned.1

04

What happened next?

  • Silence: the massacre was covered up for decades, and the press largely repeated the government's version.
  • The "Dirty War": repression continued, including the Corpus Christi massacre of 1971 and a campaign against guerrillas and activists in the 1970s.
  • Investigations: after the PRI lost power in 2000, a special prosecutor investigated. Former President Luis Echeverría was charged with genocide, but the case was dismissed in 2009.
  • Democracy: many 1968 activists went on to help build Mexico's democratic opposition.
05

Why does it matter?

Tlatelolco is the defining wound of modern Mexican history. It exposed the violence behind the one-party state and inspired generations of activists.

  • It shows how governments may use violence to protect their international image.
  • The slogan "2 de octubre no se olvida" ("2 October is not forgotten") is chanted at protests every year.
  • After the governing party lost power in 2000, a special prosecutor investigated, and former President Echeverría was charged with genocide.

What do others say?How it was seen at the time, and where historians disagree

At the time

The government claimed that armed students and foreign agitators started the shooting. Students and witnesses insisted that the army fired on a peaceful crowd.

Debates since

  • Who fired first? Declassified documents and video show that government snipers in nearby buildings fired on the crowd and on soldiers, provoking the troops.1
  • The death toll: 44 deaths are documented with names; many believe the true number was far higher.1
  • Responsibility: historians debate the roles of Díaz Ordaz, Echeverría and the army.

How is it remembered?Memorials, anniversaries and arguments over memory
  • A memorial stands in the Plaza de las Tres Culturas, and a museum of the 1968 movement is at the nearby Tlatelolco University Cultural Centre.
  • Thousands march every 2 October in Mexico City.
