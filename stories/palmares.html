Palmares: The Free Kingdom in the Hills

For almost a century, people who had escaped slavery built a free society in the hills of north-east Brazil and resisted repeated attempts to destroy it.

When
c. 1605 – 1694
Where
Serra da Barriga, Alagoas, Brazil

See it on the map →

01

What is the background to this event?

From the sixteenth century onwards, Portuguese colonists in north-eastern Brazil built a sugar economy on the labour of enslaved Africans, most of them taken from West-Central Africa (present-day Angola and the Congo region). Conditions on the plantations were brutal, and life expectancy was short.

Enslaved people resisted in many ways, and one of the most important was flight. Those who escaped formed settlements in the interior known as mocambos or quilombos. The largest grew up in the forested hills of the Serra da Barriga, inland from the captaincy of Pernambuco, in what is now the state of Alagoas. It became known as Palmares, after the palm trees that grew there.

  • The community grew rapidly during the 1630s and 1640s, when the Dutch occupation of north-eastern Brazil and the war between the Dutch and the Portuguese disrupted the plantations and allowed many more people to escape.
  • Palmares was not only African: it also included Indigenous people and some poor people of European descent.
02

Who were the key people?

  • Ganga Zumba

    King of Palmares in the 1670s, described in Portuguese sources as ruling over a confederation of settlements with members of his family. He agreed a peace treaty with the Portuguese in 1678 and died soon after.

  • Zumbi

    Born free in Palmares around 1655, captured as a child and raised by a Portuguese priest, he escaped at about fifteen and returned. He rejected the 1678 treaty, became the kingdom's leader and resisted until he was killed in 1695.

  • Dandara

    Remembered in Brazilian tradition as a warrior and Zumbi's partner. Little documentary evidence about her survives, but she has become an important symbol for Black women in Brazil.

  • Domingos Jorge Velho

    A bandeirante (frontier slave-raider and soldier) from São Paulo, hired by the colonial authorities to destroy Palmares.

  • The palmaristas

    The thousands of people — escaped Africans, people born in Palmares, Indigenous people and others — who farmed, traded, worshipped and fought there.

03

What happened?

Palmares developed from around 1605 into the largest community of escaped enslaved people ever to exist in Brazil. The historian Stuart Schwartz estimates its population at around 11,000.1 Accounts written in the 1670s describe a large, consolidated state with nine major settlements, ruled by a king whom the Portuguese called Ganga Zumba. The kingdom was known as Angola Janga, which Portuguese writers translated as "Little Angola".1

Evidence about daily life comes mainly from the reports of its enemies:

  • When the Dutch officer Johan Blaer reached one of the larger, recently abandoned settlements in 1645, he found 220 buildings, a church, four smithies and a council house.1
  • Palmares grew crops, including maize, beans and manioc, and traded with neighbouring settlers. Archaeological excavations at the Serra da Barriga have found evidence of Indigenous as well as African influence.13

The Dutch and then the Portuguese sent repeated military expeditions against Palmares. After a devastating attack in 1676–77, in which Ganga Zumba was wounded and members of his family were captured, he agreed a treaty with the governor of Pernambuco in 1678: his followers would be pardoned if they moved closer to the Portuguese settlements and returned enslaved people who had not been born in Palmares.1

Zumbi, one of Palmares' most powerful leaders, refused to accept the terms. Ganga Zumba died soon afterwards — possibly poisoned, although this is not proven — and the planters re-enslaved many of those who had moved closer to the coast.1 From 1680 to 1694, under Zumbi's leadership, Palmares waged an almost constant war against the Portuguese.12

Finally the colonial government hired the bandeirantes Domingos Jorge Velho and Bernardo Vieira de Melo. In January and February 1694, with artillery and local allies, they besieged and destroyed Cerca do Macaco, the kingdom's main settlement.12 According to some accounts, around 200 inhabitants chose to kill themselves rather than be re-enslaved.1 Zumbi escaped, but on 20 November 1695 he was betrayed, killed and beheaded, and his head was displayed in Recife to disprove legends of his immortality.2

04

What happened next?

  • Land for the conquerors: Domingos Jorge Velho and his followers were granted land in the territory of Palmares, partly to prevent the kingdom from being rebuilt.
  • Continued flight: smaller quilombos continued to form in the region and across Brazil until slavery was finally abolished in 1888 — Brazil was the last country in the Americas to do so.
  • Quilombo communities today: Brazil's 1988 constitution recognised the right of descendants of quilombo communities to the land they occupy. Thousands of communities have since been officially recognised, although many still wait for land titles.
05

What do others say?

Sources written by enemies

Almost everything written about Palmares at the time was written by people trying to destroy it: Dutch and Portuguese soldiers, officials and priests. Historians therefore read these sources carefully for what they reveal, and what they distort.

Historians' debates

  • An African kingdom in Brazil? Some historians stress the kingdom's roots in the political traditions of West-Central Africa, such as the Mbundu kilombo; others emphasise that it was a new, mixed society created in Brazil.
  • Who lived there? Archaeologist Pedro Paulo Funari and historian Alida Metcalf have argued, using archaeological evidence, that Palmares included many Indigenous people as well as Africans.
  • Slavery within Palmares? Some colonial accounts claim that Palmares itself held captives taken in raids. Historians disagree about how reliable these accounts are and how this "slavery" should be understood.
  • Zumbi or Ganga Zumba? Zumbi is now celebrated as a hero, but some historians argue that Ganga Zumba's treaty was a rational attempt to protect his people, and that the planters' betrayal of it proved Zumbi right.
06

The evidence

  • Portuguese accounts, especially the anonymous Relação das Guerras de Palmares (1678), and letters between the governor of Pernambuco and Ganga Zumba.
  • Dutch records, including Johan Blaer's journal of 1645.
  • Archaeological excavations at the Serra da Barriga since the 1990s, which have uncovered pottery and other objects.
  • Oral traditions and popular memory in north-eastern Brazil.
07

How is it remembered?

  • 20 November, the anniversary of Zumbi's death, is celebrated as the National Day of Zumbi and Black Consciousness. It became a national public holiday in 2024.
  • The Serra da Barriga is a protected national heritage site, with a memorial park (Parque Memorial Quilombo dos Palmares).
  • Zumbi and Palmares appear in Brazilian music, films (such as Carlos Diegues's Quilombo, 1984) and literature, and in Marcelo D'Salete's graphic novel Angola Janga (2017).
08

Why does it matter?

Palmares shows that enslaved people did not simply endure slavery: they fled it, and built a lasting alternative society on their own terms. For almost a century, a community made up largely of formerly enslaved Africans governed itself, farmed, traded and defended itself against one of the most powerful colonial empires of the day.

  • It is one of the longest-lasting examples of self-liberation by enslaved people anywhere in the Americas.
  • Zumbi has become a central figure of the Black movement in Brazil, and the date of his death is now a national day of remembrance.
  • The word quilombo has become a symbol of Black resistance and community in Brazil today.

Questions to think about

  1. Why might Palmares have been seen as such a threat by the colonial authorities?
  2. How can historians write about a community when almost all the written sources were produced by its enemies?
  3. Was Ganga Zumba right to make a treaty with the Portuguese? Was Zumbi right to refuse?
  4. Why do you think Zumbi became such an important symbol in twentieth-century Brazil?

What other events are like this?

  • 1791The Haitian Revolution — The only revolution in history in which enslaved people overthrew their enslavers and founded an independent state.
  • 1835The Malê Revolt — During Ramadan, enslaved and freed African Muslims rose up in Salvador — the most significant urban revolt by enslaved people in the history of the Americas.
  • 1780The Rebellion of Túpac Amaru II — An Indigenous leader and his wife, Micaela Bastidas, led the largest rebellion in Spain's American empire, demanding an end to forced labour and abuse.
