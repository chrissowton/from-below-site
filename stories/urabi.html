The ʻUrabi Revolution

An army colonel from a peasant family demanded an elected government and an end to European control of Egypt. His movement was crushed by a British invasion that began seventy years of occupation.

When
1879 – 1882
Where
Cairo and Alexandria, Egypt

See it on the map →

01

What is the background to this event?

In the 1870s, Egypt was bankrupt. Its finances were controlled by representatives of Britain and France on behalf of European banks.1

  • When the khedive (ruler) Ismaʻil resisted, he was deposed and replaced by his son Tewfik.1
  • Europeans held top jobs and paid lower taxes; heavily taxed peasants saw their money go to foreigners.1
  • Native Egyptian officers were held back by a Turco-Circassian elite.1
  • The satirical magazine Abu Naddara, written in everyday Egyptian Arabic, spread the slogan "Egypt for the Egyptians".1
02

Who were the key people?

  • Ahmed ʻUrabi

    Army colonel of peasant origin who led the movement; exiled to Ceylon.

  • Yaʿqūb Ṣanūʿ

    Editor of Abu Naddara.

  • Khedive Tewfik

    Egypt's ruler, who sided with Britain.

03

What happened?

In September 1881, ʻUrabi refused an order to move his regiment from Cairo, and demanded the dismissal of Turco-Circassian generals and an elected government.1

  • The khedive gave way, and a new chamber of deputies and government were formed.1
  • On 11 June 1882, riots in Alexandria killed about 50 Europeans and 250 Egyptians; who started them is unknown.1
  • The British fleet bombarded Alexandria and captured the city.1
  • On 13 September 1882, British forces defeated ʻUrabi's army at Tel el-Kebir.1

04

What happened next?

  • Exile: ʻUrabi was captured and exiled to Ceylon (Sri Lanka).1
  • Occupation: British forces stayed in Egypt until 1956.1
  • Memory: the Free Officers saw ʻUrabi as a forerunner of their 1952 revolution.1
05

What do others say?

At the time

Britain and the khedive presented ʻUrabi as a traitor and a military dictator.1

Debates since

  • Social revolution: Juan Cole argues the movement had deep roots among peasants, artisans and the middle classes, not only the army.2
  • Violence: the Alexandria riots killed Europeans and Egyptians alike; the British bombardment, invasion and battles killed many more Egyptians.1
06

The evidence

  • ʻUrabi's own memoirs and Egyptian newspapers.2
07

How is it remembered?

  • Under Nasser, the revolution was celebrated as a national struggle, and streets and a square in Cairo are named after ʻUrabi.1
08

Why does it matter?

The ʻUrabi revolution was the first great expression of Egyptian anti-colonial nationalism.1

  • It shows how debt was used as a tool of empire.

Questions to think about

  1. How did debt lead to foreign control of Egypt?
  2. Why did peasants see ʻUrabi as their voice?
  3. How did a satirical magazine spread political ideas?
  4. Was the British invasion a response to the riots, or the result of wider aims?
09

What other events are like this?

  • 1919Egypt's 1919 Revolution — When Britain exiled the nationalist leader Saad Zaghlul, Egyptians of every class and religion rose up in strikes and demonstrations — and women marched in public for the first time.
  • 831The Bashmurian Revolts — In the reed marshes of the Nile Delta, Coptic Egyptians rose again and again against heavy taxes and the rule of the caliphs. They held out for decades, until a caliph came in person to crush them.
  • 1905Iran's Constitutional Revolution — Merchants, clerics, craftsmen and radicals forced the Shah to grant Iran a constitution and an elected parliament — the first in the Middle East.
