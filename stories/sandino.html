Sandino's War against the US Marines

When Nicaragua's generals made peace under US supervision, one refused. Augusto César Sandino led a peasant army in a six-year guerrilla war against the US occupation.

When
1927 – 1934
Where
Las Segovias, northern Nicaragua
Facts checked
2026-11-16

See it on the map →

01

What is the background to this event?

From 1912, the United States kept Marines in Nicaragua to protect governments friendly to American business and its interest in a possible canal route.

  • In 1926, Liberal soldiers revolted against the Conservative president Adolfo Díaz, who had been installed with US support.1
  • Augusto César Sandino, a mechanic who had worked in Mexico and returned to work at an American-owned mine, joined the Liberal side, raising a force among miners and peasants in the mountainous north, Las Segovias.1
  • In May 1927, the Liberal general José María Moncada agreed to a US-brokered peace, the Espino Negro accord, and his army disarmed.1

In their own words

03

What happened?

Sandino refused to accept the agreement. In July 1927 he issued a manifesto denouncing Moncada as a "country-seller" and declared war on the US occupation.1

  • On 16 July 1927, Sandino's followers attacked the US Marines and Nicaraguan National Guard at Ocotal. They were driven back with the help of one of the first dive-bombing attacks in history, which killed many of the attackers.1
  • Sandino turned to guerrilla warfare, using his knowledge of the mountains. Despite repeated efforts, the Marines failed to capture him.1
  • At its height, Sandino claimed 3,000 soldiers; US officials later estimated about 300.1
  • The war became a cause célèbre across Latin America and among anti-imperialists worldwide.

The war was brutal on both sides. US Marines and the National Guard bombed villages and burned homes suspected of supporting Sandino. Some of Sandino's units killed prisoners and civilians accused of collaborating, and attacked foreign-owned property.

In January 1933, US troops withdrew, after supervising the election of President Juan Bautista Sacasa.1 Sandino agreed to a peace and to disarm most of his army.

04

What happened next?

  • Assassination: on 21 February 1934, after dining with President Sacasa, Sandino was seized and executed by National Guard troops on the orders of Anastasio Somoza García.1
  • Wiwilí: the National Guard then attacked the agricultural cooperative Sandino's followers had set up at Wiwilí, killing many of its members.
  • Dictatorship: Somoza took power in 1936, and his family ruled Nicaragua for more than 40 years.1
  • The Sandinistas: the Sandinista National Liberation Front (FSLN), named after Sandino, overthrew the Somoza dictatorship in 1979.1
05

What do others say?

At the time

US officials and the press called Sandino a bandit. Supporters across Latin America saw him as a patriot fighting imperialism.

Historians' debates

  • Hero or bandit? Historians now largely see Sandino as a nationalist rebel with deep peasant support, though his forces also used terror against opponents.
  • His ideas: Sandino combined nationalism, anti-imperialism, spiritualism and support for cooperatives; historians debate how far he was a socialist. He broke with the communists, including Martí.
  • Violence: Michael Schroeder's research documents atrocities by both the Marines and National Guard and by Sandinista units, including mutilation of bodies.
  • Who owns his legacy? The FSLN claimed Sandino; critics argue that the later FSLN government of Daniel Ortega has betrayed his ideals.

07

How is it remembered?

  • A giant silhouette of Sandino stands above Managua.
  • His image is seen throughout Nicaragua and in murals across Latin America.
08

Why does it matter?

Sandino became a symbol of resistance to US intervention across Latin America. A small army of peasants and miners forced the world's rising superpower to withdraw.

  • His war shaped US policy, contributing to the "Good Neighbor" policy of non-intervention in the 1930s.
  • His legacy has been claimed by very different political movements.

Questions to think about

  1. Why do you think Sandino refused to accept the 1927 peace agreement?
  2. Why were US officials so keen to call him a bandit?
  3. How did support from peasants help Sandino's army survive?
  4. Can a movement's legacy be betrayed by its heirs?
