The Persian Tobacco Protest

When the shah sold the whole of Iran's tobacco trade to a British company, merchants and clerics organised a nationwide boycott. Even the women of the shah's household stopped smoking.

When
1890 – 1892
Where
Tehran, Iran
Facts checked
2026-11-16

See it on the map →

01

What is the background to this event?

In the nineteenth century, Qajar Iran was under heavy pressure from Russia and Britain, and granted many concessions to foreigners.1

  • Iranian merchants (bazaaris) could not compete with the privileges given to European merchants.1
  • In 1872, a huge concession to Paul Reuter caused such outrage that the shah cancelled it.1
  • On 20 March 1890, Naser al-Din Shah granted a British subject, Major G. F. Talbot, a 50-year monopoly over the production, sale and export of all Iranian tobacco.1

In their own words

03

What happened?

Merchants in major cities protested against the concession, supported by the clergy.1

  • In December 1891, a fatwa (ruling) attributed to Mirza Shirazi declared the use of tobacco forbidden.1
  • The boycott was widely obeyed; it was said that even the women of the shah's household stopped smoking.1
  • In January 1892, the shah cancelled the concession, and the fatwa was lifted.1
04

What happened next?

  • Compensation: Iran had to pay the company £500,000.1
  • Revolution: the protest is seen as one of the roots of the Constitutional Revolution of 1905–11.1
05

What do others say?

At the time

The shah and the company saw an attack on lawful agreements; the protesters saw the sale of the country to foreigners.1

Debates since

  • Religion and nation: historians such as Nikki Keddie stress the alliance of merchants and clerics, and the role of Russian rivalry with Britain.3
  • Violence: the protest was mainly a boycott; there were clashes in Tehran in which some protesters were killed.3
06

The evidence

  • British diplomatic reports and Persian accounts.3

Primary source: Religious ruling (fatwa)

What is it?
Religious ruling (fatwa)
Made by, and when
Attributed to Mirza Hassan Shirazi, early 1890s

The ruling that declared the use of tobacco forbidden while the concession lasted. It is attributed to Shirazi, and it was obeyed so widely that the Shah cancelled the concession.

Things worth asking

  • Why might a religious ruling be more powerful than a political protest?
  • Why does it matter whether Shirazi wrote it himself?
  • Who gained and who lost when the concession was cancelled?
07

How is it remembered?

  • The boycott is remembered in Iran as an early victory against foreign economic domination.
08

Why does it matter?

The Tobacco Protest showed how a mass boycott could defeat a foreign monopoly and a powerful ruler.

  • It brought merchants, clergy and ordinary consumers together.

Open questions

  1. Why did one concession cause such anger?
  2. Why did a religious ruling work so well?
  3. What role did ordinary consumers play?
  4. How did the protest prepare the way for the Constitutional Revolution?
