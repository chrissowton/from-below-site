The February 28 Incident

A dispute over contraband cigarettes sparked an island-wide uprising against a corrupt new government. The crackdown that followed killed tens of thousands and was not spoken of publicly for decades.

When
27 February – May 1947
Where
Taipei and across Taiwan
Facts checked
2026-11-16

See it on the map →

01

What is the background to this event?

Taiwan was a Japanese colony from 1895 until 1945. After Japan's defeat, the island was handed to the Republic of China, ruled by the Kuomintang (KMT) of Chiang Kai-shek.1

  • Many Taiwanese at first welcomed the end of Japanese rule. But the new governor, Chen Yi, and his officials from mainland China were seen as high-handed and corrupt.1
  • The government took over Japanese monopolies on tobacco, sugar, camphor and other goods, and seized private property. Taiwanese were excluded from political life.1
  • Inflation, unemployment and food shortages added to the anger.
02

Who were the key people?

  • Lin Jiang-mai

    A 40-year-old widow selling cigarettes, whose beating by monopoly agents began the incident.

  • The Settlement Committees

    Committees of Taiwanese leaders who negotiated with the government and demanded reforms.

  • Chen Yi

    The governor, who called in troops from the mainland. He was later executed for other reasons in 1950.

  • The victims

    Taiwanese students, lawyers, doctors, teachers and many others killed or disappeared; and mainland civilians killed by Taiwanese crowds.

In their own words

03

What happened?

On the evening of 27 February 1947, agents of the tobacco monopoly in Taipei confiscated contraband cigarettes from a widow, Lin Jiang-mai, and struck her. An officer fired into an angry crowd, killing a bystander.1

  • The next day, soldiers fired on demonstrators. Protesters seized a radio station and broadcast news of the revolt across the island.1
  • The early riots were spontaneous and sometimes violent. Crowds beat and killed people from mainland China; over 1,000 mainlanders are reported to have been killed.1
  • Taiwanese leaders formed Settlement Committees, which demanded greater autonomy, free elections and an end to corruption.1

The governor stalled for time while he waited for troops from the mainland.1 When they arrived in March, a brutal crackdown followed. Soldiers killed civilians in the streets, and Taiwanese leaders — lawyers, teachers, doctors, students and local politicians — were arrested and executed or disappeared.1

The number of deaths is estimated at between 18,000 and 28,000.1

04

What happened next?

  • The White Terror: two years later, Taiwan was placed under martial law, which lasted 38 years. The KMT persecuted perceived dissidents, and the incident could not be discussed.1
  • Silence broken: after martial law ended in 1987, families and activists demanded the truth. In 1995, President Lee Teng-hui became the first president to discuss the incident publicly on its anniversary, and apologised.1
  • Commemoration: 28 February is now Peace Memorial Day, a public holiday in Taiwan.1
05

What do others say?

At the time

The government blamed communists and Japanese-influenced agitators. Taiwanese saw it as a revolt against misrule.

Historians' debates

  • Violence on both sides: the initial riots included attacks on innocent mainland civilians; the crackdown by government troops killed far more people, most of them Taiwanese.1
  • Who was responsible? Debate continues about the role of Chiang Kai-shek in ordering the crackdown.
  • Communist involvement: some research shows that communists took part in the uprising, though they did not start it.1
  • Identity: the memory of 228 remains politically sensitive, and is interpreted differently by those who favour Taiwanese independence and those who favour closer ties with China.

07

How is it remembered?

  • Taipei's New Park was renamed the 228 Peace Memorial Park, and the National 228 Memorial Museum opened in 1997.1
  • Hou Hsiao-hsien's film A City of Sadness (1989) was the first to depict the incident.
08

Why does it matter?

The February 28 incident is one of the most important events in Taiwan's modern history and a key reason for the growth of a distinct Taiwanese identity and the independence movement.1

  • It shows how a small incident can spark an uprising when anger has built up.
  • It shows how silence imposed by the state can last for generations — and how democracy can open the way to remembrance.
