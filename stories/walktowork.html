Uganda's Walk to Work Protests

With fuel and food prices soaring, Ugandans were asked to protest in the simplest way possible: by walking to work. The government answered with tear gas, arrests and live bullets.

When
April – May 2011
Where
Kampala, Gulu, Masaka and other towns, Uganda
Facts checked
2026-11-16

See it on the map →

01

What is the background to this event?

Yoweri Museveni had ruled Uganda since 1986. In February 2011 he won another presidential election, which the opposition said was unfair.1

  • Inflation was running at 11 per cent in February 2011, and fuel prices rose by about 50 per cent between January and April.1
  • Asked about fuel prices, Museveni advised the public to use fuel sparingly and not to drive to bars.1
  • Public gatherings were tightly controlled, so activists looked for a form of protest that would be hard to ban.

In their own words

03

What happened?

On 11 April 2011, Activists for Change called on people to walk to work, to highlight the cost of transport.1

  • Police broke up the first walk with tear gas and arrested Kizza Besigye and Norbert Mao.1
  • On 14 April, Besigye was hit in the arm by a rubber bullet. Protests spread to Gulu, Masaka and other towns.1
  • By 15 April, five people had been shot dead by the security forces, with dozens injured and hundreds arrested.1
  • Further protests on 21 and 29 April led to four more deaths, about 100 injuries and 600 arrests.1
  • In some places protesters burned tyres, blocked roads and threw stones at police.2

According to Human Rights Watch, none of the nine people killed had been actively taking part in the protests.12

04

What happened next?

  • Banned: the government banned Activists for Change in 2012.
  • New laws: the Public Order Management Act of 2013 gave police wide powers over public gatherings.
  • Museveni remained in power; the constitutional age limit for presidents was removed in 2017.
05

What do others say?

At the time

The government said the protests were an attempt by defeated politicians to overthrow it, and blamed protesters for the violence. Organisers said they were protesting peacefully about prices.1

Debates since

  • Economic or political? Critics noted that the leaders were opposition politicians; supporters replied that high prices and bad government were linked.
  • Violence: some protesters rioted, but those killed were shot by the security forces, and no one was held to account.2
06

The evidence

  • Television footage, including of Besigye's arrests.
  • Human Rights Watch's interviews with witnesses.2
07

How is it remembered?

  • "Walk to work" remains a well-known phrase in Ugandan politics.
08

Why does it matter?

Walk to Work showed how an everyday act could become a protest that was difficult to outlaw — and how harshly a government might still respond.

  • It linked the cost of living to democracy.
  • It became a model for creative protest in other African countries.

Questions to think about

  1. Why did the organisers choose walking as a form of protest?
  2. Why did the government treat walking to work as a threat?
  3. Who was responsible for the deaths?
  4. Did the protests achieve anything?
