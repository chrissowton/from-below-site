The Herero and Nama Uprisings and Genocide

The Herero and Nama rose against German settlers who had taken their land and cattle. Germany's response was the first genocide of the twentieth century.

When
1904 – 1908
Where
Okahandja, Waterberg and across central and southern Namibia

See it on the map →

01

What is the background to this event?

Germany established the colony of German South West Africa (now Namibia) in 1884. Settlers began to arrive in significant numbers in the mid-1890s.1

  • The Herero, a cattle-herding people, and the Nama lost land and cattle through treaties, debt and force. The rinderpest epidemic of 1897 killed most of their herds.
  • Settlers could kill Africans with near impunity; murder and rape by settlers were widespread.1
  • Germany aimed to turn Africans into a labour force for settlers.1
02

Who were the key people?

  • Samuel Maharero

    Paramount chief of the Herero, who launched the uprising in January 1904. He escaped to British Bechuanaland and died in exile.

  • Hendrik Witbooi

    Nama leader who rose against Germany in 1904, aged about 70. He was killed in 1905.

  • Jacob Marengo and Cornelius Fredericks

    Nama leaders who continued the war.

  • General Lothar von Trotha

    The German commander who issued the extermination order.

03

What happened?

On 12 January 1904, Samuel Maharero launched a surprise uprising.1 Herero fighters attacked farms and settlements, killing around 120 Germans, mostly men; according to many accounts, Maharero ordered that women, children, missionaries and other Europeans be spared. The uprising was initially a success.1

After reinforcements arrived from Germany, the Herero — around 60,000 people with their cattle — were surrounded at the Waterberg in August 1904 and routed.1

  • The survivors fled into the Omaheke desert, where most died of thirst and starvation, or in German massacres.1
  • General von Trotha ordered that every Herero within German territory should be shot; in practice, women and children were also killed.1
  • After the Kaiser overruled the extermination order, surviving Herero were imprisoned in concentration camps, where around half died of disease, hunger and forced labour.1

The Nama, who had at first fought alongside the Germans, rose in October 1904 under Hendrik Witbooi, and fought a guerrilla war until 1908.1 Thousands of Nama died in camps, including the notorious camp on Shark Island.

Around 40,000 to 80,000 Herero (perhaps 80 per cent of the people) and 10,000 Nama (half of the people) died.1

04

What happened next?

  • Dispossession: the survivors lost almost all their land and cattle and were forced into labour.
  • Independence: Namibia was ruled by South Africa after 1915 and became independent in 1990.
  • Recognition: in 2015, Germany acknowledged that a genocide had been committed. In 2021, it agreed to pay €1.1 billion in development aid, while rejecting legal responsibility. Many Herero and Nama leaders rejected the deal because they were not properly consulted.1
  • Remains: skulls of victims taken to Germany for "racial science" have been returned to Namibia in several ceremonies since 2011.
05

What do others say?

At the time

German settlers portrayed themselves as victims and demanded harsh punishment of the Herero.1 Some Germans, including socialist members of the Reichstag, criticised the killings.

Historians' debates

  • Genocide: there is broad agreement among historians that the killings constituted genocide; Germany officially accepted this in 2015.1
  • The uprising: historians debate its causes — Jan-Bart Gewald argues that it was provoked by a German officer.1 The Herero did kill settlers at the start, but the German response was out of all proportion.
  • Links to the Holocaust: some historians see connections between colonial genocide and the Nazi Holocaust; others argue the comparison is overstated.
  • Reparations: the 2021 agreement remains controversial in Namibia.1
06

The evidence

  • Von Trotha's extermination order and German military records.
  • The British "Blue Book" of 1918, which collected testimonies of survivors.
  • Photographs and the remains of victims taken to Germany.
07

How is it remembered?

  • Herero Day is commemorated each August at Okahandja, where Samuel Maharero is buried.
  • Hendrik Witbooi appears on Namibian banknotes.
08

Why does it matter?

The Herero and Nama genocide was the first genocide of the twentieth century. Its methods — extermination orders, concentration camps and racial science — foreshadowed later atrocities.

  • It shows how resistance to colonial rule could be met with annihilation.
  • It raises questions about reparations and how former colonial powers should take responsibility.

Questions to think about

  2. Why do historians describe the German response as genocide?
  3. How should Germany make amends for the genocide?
  4. Why was this genocide forgotten for so long in Europe?
