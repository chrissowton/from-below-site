The Butler Riots: Trinidad's Oilfield Strike

Oil workers led by the preacher 'Buzz' Butler struck against low wages and racism. The violence that followed killed police and strikers alike — and the island's labour movement was born.

When
19 June – 6 July 1937
Where
Fyzabad and the oilfields of southern Trinidad

See it on the map →

01

What is the background to this event?

In the 1930s, Trinidad was a British colony with one of the most important oil industries in the British Empire. The oilfields of the south were owned by British companies, and the island's sugar estates by planters. Most workers — African-Trinidadians in the oilfields, and Indo-Trinidadians on the sugar estates — had no vote.

  • During the Depression, wages fell and unemployment rose. Workers complained of low pay, poor housing and racism from white managers, many of them from South Africa.1
  • Across the British Caribbean, from St Kitts (1935) to Barbados (1937) and Jamaica (1938), a wave of labour unrest was under way.
  • Tubal Uriah "Buzz" Butler, a Grenadian-born oilfield worker, First World War veteran and Spiritual Baptist preacher, became a powerful champion of the workers. In 1935 he led a "hunger march" from the oilfields to Port of Spain.1
02

Who were the key people?

  • Tubal Uriah 'Buzz' Butler

    A preacher and labour leader whose speeches drew huge crowds of oil workers. He was imprisoned from 1937 to 1939 and again during the Second World War.

  • Adrian Cola Rienzi

    A lawyer and trade unionist of Indian descent, who helped found the Oilfields Workers' Trade Union and the All Trinidad Sugar Estates and Factory Workers' Union.

  • Corporal Charlie King

    A police officer killed by the crowd at Fyzabad on 19 June 1937.

  • The oil and sugar workers

    African- and Indo-Trinidadian workers who joined the strike across the south of the island.

03

What happened?

On 19 June 1937, a strike over working conditions, wages, racism and exploitation began in the oilfields of southern Trinidad.1 When police tried to arrest Butler as he addressed a meeting at Fyzabad, his supporters prevented them. In the struggle, a police officer, Corporal Charlie King, was killed by the crowd.1

The unrest spread rapidly:12

  • Strikes and riots spread through the oilfields and then to the sugar estates and factories, and to Port of Spain.
  • The colonial government issued a warrant for Butler's arrest; he went into hiding.
  • British marines were landed from warships to support the police. In clashes over the following two weeks, police and soldiers shot dead a number of strikers and bystanders; more than a dozen people were killed in total, including police officers, and many more were injured.

Butler stayed in contact with the authorities through Adrian Cola Rienzi. He was promised safe passage to testify before a commission of enquiry, but was arrested; he was imprisoned from September 1937 to May 1939, and detained again for the whole of the Second World War.1

04

What happened next?

  • Unions: the Oilfields Workers' Trade Union (OWTU) was founded in July 1937, and unions were formed among sugar workers and others. They won recognition and collective bargaining.
  • Commissions: the Forster Commission (1938) accepted that workers had genuine grievances. The Moyne Commission (1938–39) investigated conditions across the British West Indies; its damning findings were kept secret until after the war.
  • Politics: universal adult suffrage came to Trinidad in 1946. Butler's party won the largest bloc of seats in 1950, but he was kept out of government. Trinidad and Tobago became independent in 1962.
05

What do others say?

At the time

The colonial government and the oil companies described Butler as a dangerous agitator and a religious fanatic, and the unrest as riots incited by him. Many workers saw Butler as a prophet and a hero.

Historians' debates

  • The violence: the killing of Charlie King, a Black police officer, is remembered very differently: some see it as a tragedy that shows how quickly confrontation became deadly; others emphasise that most of those killed in the following days were shot by police and soldiers. A junction in Fyzabad is now named after him.
  • Butler's role: historians debate whether Butler was a labour leader with a clear programme, a charismatic religious figure, or both; the colonial authorities and later politicians, including Eric Williams, regarded him as too unpredictable.
  • A regional revolt: O. Nigel Bolland places the Trinidad strike within a wider Caribbean labour rebellion of 1934–39.

07

How is it remembered?

  • Labour Day, 19 June, has been a public holiday since 1973.
  • A statue of Butler stands outside the OWTU building at Fyzabad, and the main highway of Trinidad is named after him.
  • Butler was awarded the Trinity Cross, the country's highest honour, in 1970.
08

Why does it matter?

The 1937 strike was the founding moment of the labour movement in Trinidad and Tobago, and part of a Caribbean-wide revolt that changed British colonial policy. Workers who had no vote forced the colonial state to recognise their unions and their grievances.

  • It brought together African- and Indo-Trinidadian workers in a common struggle.
  • It led directly to the trade unions and political parties that shaped the independent nation.
  • 19 June is now celebrated as Labour Day, a public holiday in Trinidad and Tobago.

Questions to think about

  1. Why do you think the strike spread so quickly from the oilfields to the sugar estates?
  2. How should the death of Corporal Charlie King be remembered alongside the strikers who were killed?
  3. Why did the colonial authorities fear Butler so much?
  4. How did the labour movement shape the politics of independent Trinidad and Tobago?

What other events are like this?

  • 1919The Winnipeg General Strike — For six weeks, 30,000 workers brought Canada's third-largest city to a halt to win the right to collective bargaining. It ended with 'Bloody Saturday' — and transformed Canadian politics.
  • 1816Bussa's Rebellion — On Easter Sunday 1816, enslaved people across south-east Barbados rose up — the largest slave revolt in the island's history, and the first of three great risings that shook British slavery.
  • 1947The Dakar–Niger Railway Strike — African railway workers in French West Africa held out for more than five months to win equal treatment with their European colleagues.
