Kimpa Vita and the Antonian Movement

A young noblewoman in a kingdom torn apart by civil war and the slave trade declared that Jesus was born in Kongo. Thousands followed her to reoccupy the ruined capital — until she was burned at the stake.

When
1704 – 1706
Where
São Salvador (Mbanza Kongo), Kingdom of Kongo (Angola)
Facts checked
2026-11-16

See it on the map →

01

What is the background to this event?

The Kingdom of Kongo, in what is now northern Angola and the western Democratic Republic of the Congo, had been Christian since the late fifteenth century.

  • After the Battle of Mbwila in 1665, in which the Portuguese defeated and killed King António I, Kongo collapsed into civil war between rival claimants to the throne.1
  • The capital, São Salvador, was abandoned in 1678.1
  • The wars fed the Atlantic slave trade: prisoners were sold to European traders and shipped to the Americas.
  • Many people, exhausted by war, longed for peace and a reunited kingdom. An elderly prophetess, Apolonia Mafuta, had already attracted followers.1
02

Who were the key people?

  • Dona Beatriz Kimpa Vita

    A noblewoman, born around 1684, who led the Antonian movement. She was burned in 1706, aged about 22.

  • King Pedro IV

    One of the rival kings, who ordered her execution.

  • Bernardo da Gallo and Lorenzo da Lucca

    Italian Capuchin missionaries whose writings are the main sources for her life.

  • The Antonians

    Thousands of followers, many of them peasants and war refugees.

03

What happened?

In 1704, during an illness, Kimpa Vita said she had died and been possessed by Saint Anthony, who had sent her to restore the kingdom.1

  • She preached that Jesus and other holy figures had been born in Kongo, and that Kongo must reunite under a single king, because the civil wars had angered Christ.1
  • She challenged the European missionaries and called for a Kongolese church.
  • She destroyed both traditional charms (minkisi) and Catholic crosses that she believed had become objects of superstition.1
  • According to some accounts, she warned that trusting the Portuguese would lead to enslavement.1

Thousands of followers, the Antonians, joined her, and many resettled the ruined capital of São Salvador, which became her base.1

In 1706, she was captured by King Pedro IV. After consulting the Capuchin missionaries, he had her tried for heresy, and on 2 July 1706 she was burned at the stake.1

04

What happened next?

  • Reunification: Pedro IV reoccupied São Salvador in 1709 and reunited the kingdom, partly by winning over Antonians.
  • The slave trade: many Antonians captured in the wars were sold into slavery. Historians such as John Thornton suggest that some of them may have taken part in the Stono Rebellion in South Carolina in 1739.
  • Later movements: Kimpa Vita's ideas foreshadowed later African-led churches, such as the Kimbanguist church founded in the Belgian Congo in 1921.
05

Why does it matter?

Kimpa Vita is one of the most remarkable figures in African history: a young woman who challenged kings and missionaries and offered her people a vision of peace and a Christianity of their own.

  • She is seen by some as an early opponent of the slave trade and of European domination.
  • Her story shows how Africans adapted Christianity to their own needs.
  • Her ideas foreshadowed later African-led churches, such as the Kimbanguist church founded in the Belgian Congo in 1921.

What do others say?How it was seen at the time, and where historians disagree

At the time

The missionaries saw her as a heretic and a witch. Her followers saw her as a saint and prophet.

Historians' debates

  • Religious or political? John Thornton shows that her movement was both a religious reform and a political project to restore the kingdom.
  • Anti-slavery? Historians debate how far she opposed the slave trade; later writers have made her a symbol of resistance to it.
  • Sources: almost everything we know comes from the missionaries who opposed her, so historians must read their accounts critically.
How do we know?The evidence, and a source from the time

Reading it

Almost everything we know about Kimpa Vita comes from her enemies, including this drawing by Bernardo da Gallo, one of the missionaries who opposed her. He saw her as a heretic, and drew her accordingly. What her followers would have wanted to record — her visions, her message of peace — has to be read between the lines.

How is it remembered?Memorials, anniversaries and arguments over memory
  • Kimpa Vita is honoured in Angola and the Democratic Republic of the Congo; a statue stands in Uíge, and universities and organisations bear her name.
  • She has been the subject of plays, including Bernard Dadié's Béatrice du Congo (1970).
