Disability rights

The Disabled People's Direct Action Network

Disabled activists chained their wheelchairs to buses, blocked traffic and picketed charity telethons, demanding 'rights not charity'. Their direct action helped force Britain to pass its first disability discrimination law.

When
1990 – 1995
Where
Westminster, Oxford Street and towns across Britain

See it on the map →

01

What is the background to this event?

In the 1980s, most British buses, trains and public buildings were inaccessible to wheelchair users, and there was no law against disability discrimination.

  • Disabled people were often portrayed as objects of pity in charity fundraising, such as television telethons.1
  • The "social model of disability", developed by British disabled activists, argued that people are disabled by barriers in society, not by their impairments.
  • Activists learned from ADAPT, a US direct action group, which led a workshop in Manchester in 1991.1
02

Who were the key people?

  • Barbara Lisicki

    A comedian and activist, co-founder of the Block Telethon campaign and of DAN.

  • Alan Holdsworth

    Also known as the singer Johnny Crescendo; co-founder of DAN.

  • Sue Elsegood

    A co-founder of DAN.

  • Ken Davis

    A leading member of the Derbyshire Coalition of Disabled People.

03

What happened?

In 1989, disabled people in Derbyshire protested against the pedestrianisation of Chesterfield. Arrested protesters refused to plead guilty.1

  • Between 1990 and 1993, the Campaign for Accessible Transport held street protests, including on Oxford Street.1
  • In November 1991, 150 disabled people picketed the BBC's Children in Need broadcast in Leeds under the banner "Rights Not Charity"; eight people were arrested.1
  • On 18 July 1992, more than a thousand disabled people protested outside ITV's Telethon in London. ITV later abandoned the Telethon.1
  • In 1993, Lisicki, Holdsworth and Elsegood founded the Disabled People's Direct Action Network (DAN).1
  • DAN held more than 100 actions between 1993 and 1998. Activists handcuffed or chained themselves to buses, or put their wheelchairs in the path of traffic.1
  • In 1994 and 1995, while parliament debated disability law, activists chained themselves to a bus outside the Palace of Westminster.1

04

What happened next?

  • The law: the Disability Discrimination Act was passed in 1995. Activists criticised it as weak, and continued to campaign.
  • Accessible buses: over the following decades, low-floor accessible buses became standard in Britain.
  • Equality Act: disability rights were later included in the Equality Act 2010.
05

What do others say?

At the time

Some charities and members of the public were shocked by protests against fundraising; activists argued that pity undermined their rights.1

Debates since

  • Direct action or lobbying? historians debate how far protest, rather than lobbying, won the 1995 Act.2
  • Non-violence: the actions were non-violent civil disobedience, though they disrupted traffic and led to many arrests.1

07

How is it remembered?

  • Disabled activists in Britain continue to use direct action, for example against cuts to welfare benefits.
08

Why does it matter?

DAN brought the disability rights movement onto the streets and changed how disabled people were seen — as citizens demanding rights, not objects of pity.

  • It showed the power of direct action by people often excluded from public life.
  • It linked British activists to a global disability rights movement.

09

What other events are like this?

  • 1977The Section 504 Sit-in — More than a hundred disabled people occupied a federal building for 26 days until the government signed the first major civil rights regulations for disabled Americans.
  • 1920The Blind Marchers of 1920 — Blind workers organised in their own trade union marched on London from three directions, demanding 'rights not charity'. Their march won the first disability law in Britain — and inspired the Jarrow March.
  • 1977Aoi Shiba no Kai and the Kawasaki Bus Struggle — A radical group of people with cerebral palsy refused to be pitied. They protested against the killing of disabled children — and occupied buses that refused to carry wheelchair users.
