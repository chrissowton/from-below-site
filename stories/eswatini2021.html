Eswatini's Pro-Democracy Uprising

In Africa's last absolute monarchy, young people marched to deliver petitions demanding an elected prime minister. When the petitions were banned, protests turned to burning and looting, and security forces opened fire.

When
June – July 2021
Where
Manzini, Mbabane and across Eswatini

See it on the map →

01

What is the background to this event?

Eswatini (formerly Swaziland) is Africa's only absolute monarchy. Political parties have been banned from elections since 1973, and King Mswati III has ruled since 1986.1

  • The king appoints the prime minister and controls much of the economy, while most people live in poverty.
  • There had been protests in 2018 and 2019 against the king's lavish lifestyle.1
  • In May 2021, the death of a law student, Thabani Nkomonye, led to suspicions of police involvement and to protests.1
  • Three pro-democracy MPs called for an elected prime minister.1
02

Who were the key people?

  • Young people in Manzini and the countryside

    Who began the protests.

  • Mduduzi Bacede Mabuza and Mthandeni Dube

    Pro-democracy MPs, arrested in July 2021 and later jailed.

  • Maxwell Dlamini

    A student and pro-democracy activist.

  • Thulani Maseko

    A human rights lawyer murdered at his home in January 2023.

  • King Mswati III

    King of Eswatini since 1986.

03

What happened?

On 20 June 2021, young people in Manzini region marched through villages calling for a democratically chosen prime minister.1

  • Thousands tried to deliver petitions to local authorities, but the government banned their delivery.1
  • From 25 June, the protests escalated. Shops and businesses, including some linked to the king, were looted and burned.1
  • Police used tear gas and gunfire. A curfew was imposed, the internet was restricted and the army was deployed.1
  • The army and police used deadly force against demonstrators.1
  • The Southern African Development Community (SADC) sent ministers and later a team to meet the king.1

Human rights groups reported that dozens of people were killed by the security forces; estimates range from about 30 to more than 70. Many more were injured.2

04

What happened next?

  • Unrest continued: protests and strikes continued into 2022.
  • Maseko: on 21 January 2023, the human rights lawyer Thulani Maseko was shot dead at his home, causing international outrage.1
  • MPs jailed: the two arrested MPs were convicted under anti-terrorism and other laws and given long sentences.
  • Dialogue: the king promised a national dialogue, which critics said had not taken place.
05

What do others say?

At the time

The government blamed criminals and "terrorists" for the violence. Human Rights Watch and Amnesty International condemned the use of lethal force against protesters.12

Debates since

  • Violence: the destruction of property, including businesses that employed ordinary people, was criticised; most of the deaths were caused by the security forces.12
  • Reform: some favour a constitutional monarchy, others a republic.
06

The evidence

  • Videos and photographs shared on social media despite internet restrictions.
  • Reports by human rights organisations.2
07

How is it remembered?

  • Pro-democracy groups mark the anniversary of the protests each June.
08

Why does it matter?

The uprising was the largest challenge to the monarchy in decades, and shows how demands for democracy can grow from economic hardship.

  • It drew international attention to Africa's last absolute monarchy.

Questions to think about

  1. Why did the ban on petitions make things worse?
  2. Why did protests turn to looting and burning?
  3. Can an absolute monarchy reform itself?
  4. What role should neighbouring countries play?

What other events are like this?

  • 2006Nepal's People's Movement of 2006 — Defying curfews and shoot-on-sight orders, millions of Nepalis took to the streets for nineteen days and forced their king to give up absolute power — the beginning of the end of a 240-year-old monarchy.
  • 2014The Burkinabè Uprising — When Blaise Compaoré tried to change the constitution to stay in power after 27 years, hundreds of thousands took to the streets. Within three days, the parliament was on fire and the president had fled.
  • 1976The Soweto Uprising — Thousands of Black schoolchildren marched against being taught in Afrikaans. Police opened fire, and the uprising spread across the country.
