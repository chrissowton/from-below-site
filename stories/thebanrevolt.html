The Great Theban Revolt

Egyptians rose against the Greek-speaking Ptolemaic kings who ruled from Alexandria. For twenty years, two native pharaohs ruled from Thebes — the longest revolt against foreign rule in ancient Egypt.

When
206 – 186 BCE
Where
Thebes (Luxor) and Upper Egypt
Facts checked
2026-11-16

See it on the map →

01

What is the background to this event?

After Alexander the Great's death in 323 BCE, his general Ptolemy took Egypt. The Ptolemaic dynasty ruled from Alexandria, with a Greek-speaking elite.1

  • Native Egyptians paid heavy taxes, and the state was in financial crisis after the Fourth Syrian War.1
  • Thebes, the old capital and centre of the god Amun, had a long tradition of independence.1
  • Ptolemy IV's attempts to tighten control over the Theban region provoked resistance.1
02

Who were the key people?

  • Haronnophris (Hugronaphor)

    Crowned pharaoh at Thebes in 205 BCE.

  • Ankhmakis (Chaonnophris)

    His successor as rebel pharaoh, defeated in 186 BCE.

  • The people of Upper Egypt

    Farmers, priests and soldiers who supported the revolt.

  • Ptolemy IV and Ptolemy V

    The Greek kings of Egypt.

03

What happened?

Unrest broke out in Upper Egypt in 207–206 BCE. In autumn 206 BCE, rebels seized the great temple of Edfu, a symbol of Ptolemaic power.1

  • They marched on Thebes, took the city and drove out the Greeks.1
  • In 205 BCE, the rebel leader was crowned pharaoh at Thebes as Haronnophris, beloved of Isis and Amun-Ra.1
  • The revolt spread across much of Upper Egypt, and was not quickly crushed.1
  • Revolts also broke out in the Nile Delta; the Rosetta Stone (196 BCE) records Ptolemy V's siege of rebels at Lycopolis.
  • In 186 BCE, the second rebel pharaoh, Ankhmakis, was defeated, and Ptolemaic rule was restored.

04

What happened next?

  • Amnesty: after the defeat of the rebels, a royal decree of 186 BCE offered an amnesty, while the defeated leader was executed.
  • Unrest continued: further revolts broke out in Upper Egypt in the second century BCE.
  • Rome: Egypt became a Roman province in 30 BCE.
05

What do others say?

At the time

The Greek historian Polybius blamed Egyptian soldiers returning from war; this explanation is now widely rejected.1

Debates since

  • Nationalism or economics? Historians debate whether the revolt was driven mainly by Egyptian identity or by taxes and economic crisis.2
  • Violence: both rebels and royal forces killed opponents, and temples and towns were damaged.2
06

The evidence

  • Papyri, temple inscriptions and the Rosetta Stone.
  • Building records from the temple of Edfu, whose construction was interrupted.
07

How is it remembered?

  • The revolt is little known outside specialist circles, but is increasingly studied as a case of resistance to foreign rule.
08

Why does it matter?

The Great Theban Revolt was a long struggle by Egyptians against foreign rulers, centuries before modern anti-colonial movements.

  • It shows how religion and the memory of former independence could inspire resistance.

Questions to think about

09

What other events are like this?

  • 1157 BCEThe Tomb-Builders' Strike at Deir el-Medina — When the pharaoh's grain rations failed to arrive, the craftsmen who built the royal tombs laid down their tools and marched to the temples — the earliest strike in recorded history.
  • 1919Egypt's 1919 Revolution — When Britain exiled the nationalist leader Saad Zaghlul, Egyptians of every class and religion rose up in strikes and demonstrations — and women marched in public for the first time.
  • 40 CEThe Trưng Sisters' Rebellion — Two sisters led the peoples of the Red River Delta in a revolt against Chinese rule, drove out the Han governor and ruled as queens for three years.
