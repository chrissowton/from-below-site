The vote & democracy

Bahrain's National Union Committee

After clashes between Sunni and Shia Bahrainis, reformers from both communities came together to demand an elected assembly, trade unions and an end to British control. Their leaders were exiled to a remote Atlantic island.

When
1954 – 1956
Where
Manama, Bahrain

See it on the map →

01

What is the background to this event?

In the 1950s, Bahrain was ruled by the Al Khalifa family under British protection. A British adviser, Charles Belgrave, had run much of the administration since 1926.

  • Oil had been discovered in 1932, and thousands of Bahrainis worked for the oil company, but they had no unions and no political voice.
  • Arab nationalism, inspired by Gamal Abdel Nasser in Egypt, was spreading across the Gulf.
  • In 1953–54, clashes broke out between Sunni and Shia Bahrainis. Reformers from both communities feared that sectarianism would be used to divide them.1
02

Who were the key people?

  • Abdulrahman Al Bakir

    A Sunni journalist and the committee's secretary, exiled to St Helena.

  • Abdulaziz Al Shamlan and Abdali Al Alaiwat

    Committee leaders — one Sunni, one Shia — also imprisoned on St Helena.

  • Four Sunni and four Shia members

    The committee was deliberately made up of equal numbers from both communities.

  • Oil workers and students

    Who joined the strikes and demonstrations.

03

What happened?

In 1954, reformers formed the Higher Executive Committee, later the National Union Committee (NUC), with four Sunni and four Shia members.1 Its ideas were spread through the journal Sawt al-Bahrain.1

The committee demanded:1

  • an elected popular assembly;
  • codified civil and criminal law and a court of appeal;
  • the right to form trade unions;
  • an end to British influence, through the removal of Charles Belgrave;
  • an end to sectarianism.

The NUC organised a number of general strikes and demonstrations.1 In March 1956, crowds protesting against British influence threw sand and stones at the entourage of the visiting British Foreign Secretary, Selwyn Lloyd, injuring several people.1

In October 1956, during the British, French and Israeli attack on Egypt (the Suez crisis), the NUC called strikes and demonstrations, which led to days of violence.1 In November, the ruler ordered the leaders' arrest, accusing three of them of plotting to kill him. A special court of three judges, all members of the ruling family, sentenced them to 14 years in prison — on the British island of St Helena, in the South Atlantic.1

04

What happened next?

  • Release: in June 1961, the three prisoners were freed after a successful legal challenge (habeas corpus) in the British courts, and Britain later paid them compensation.1
  • Belgrave: Charles Belgrave left Bahrain in 1957.
  • Further protest: the March Intifada of 1965, led by oil workers and students, continued the struggle.1
  • Parliament: after independence in 1971, Bahrain elected a parliament in 1973, but the ruler dissolved it in 1975. Demands for democracy and an end to sectarian division continued, including the mass protests of 2011.
05

What do others say?

At the time

The ruler and the British saw the committee as a dangerous nationalist movement linked to Nasser. The committee saw itself as a peaceful reform movement seeking constitutional government.

Historians' debates

  • Fair trial? Historians note that the trial was conducted by members of the ruling family, and that the British courts later found the imprisonment unlawful.1
  • Violence: some protests, such as the attack on Selwyn Lloyd's entourage and the Suez riots, involved violence; the committee's leaders denied plotting to kill the ruler.
  • Unity: historians debate how deep the cross-sectarian unity was, and how far it was undermined by later politics.

07

How is it remembered?

  • The committee is remembered by Bahraini opposition movements as a model of national unity.
08

Why does it matter?

The National Union Committee was a rare moment when Bahrainis of both Sunni and Shia communities united around demands for democracy, workers' rights and independence.

  • It shows how movements can overcome sectarian divisions.
  • It reveals how colonial powers used legal tools — and remote islands — to deal with opponents.
  • Its demands remain relevant in the Gulf today.
