The Yellow Turban Rebellion

Inspired by a Daoist healer who promised a new age of 'Great Peace', hundreds of thousands of peasants rose against the Han dynasty — a revolt that helped to bring the empire down.

When
184 CE – 205
Where
North China Plain (Julu, Hebei)
Facts checked
2026-11-16

See it on the map →

01

What is the background to this event?

In the second century AD, the Han dynasty ruled one of the largest empires in the world. But by the 180s its central government was weak:1

  • The court was dominated by struggles between palace eunuchs and officials. A group of powerful eunuchs, known as the "Ten Attendants", controlled access to Emperor Ling, who once said of two of them that "Zhang Rang is my father and Zhao Zhong is my mother".
  • Offices were bought and sold, and local officials and landlords exploited the peasants through heavy taxes and forced labour.
  • Floods along the Yellow River, epidemics and famine drove farmers from their land, and many fell into debt or became tenants of great landowners.

In this time of suffering, a Daoist religious movement called the Way of Supreme Peace (Taiping Dao) spread rapidly across eastern China. Its leader, Zhang Jue, won followers through healing: the sick were treated with charmed water and encouraged to confess their wrongdoing.1

In their own words

03

What happened?

Over more than a decade, Zhang Jue and his brothers sent disciples across eight provinces of northern and eastern China. Their followers were organised into thirty-six regional units, and plans were made for a coordinated rising across the empire, timed for the beginning of a new sixty-year cycle in the Chinese calendar — the jiazi year, 184.1 The movement spread a sixteen-character prophecy:

"The Azure Sky is already dead; the Yellow Sky will soon rise. When the year is jiazi, there will be great fortune under heaven."Slogan of the Yellow Turbans, 184 CE1

The plan was betrayed by one of the movement's own members, Tang Zhou. Ma Yuanyi, who had been preparing the rising in the capital, Luoyang, was arrested and executed, and hundreds of sympathisers in the city were killed.1 Forced to act early, in March 184 Zhang Jue launched the rebellion with some 360,000 followers wearing yellow headscarves:1

  • Government offices were plundered and burned across eastern China, and many officials were killed or fled.
  • The imperial armies were immediately forced onto the defensive.
  • The emperor appointed his brother-in-law He Jin as commander-in-chief and sent three generals — Lu Zhi, Huangfu Song and Zhu Jun — against the rebels with around 40,000 troops.

By the end of 184 the main rebel armies had been defeated. Zhang Jue died of illness, and his brothers were killed in battle; the government armies massacred tens of thousands of rebels. But other uprisings continued, and Yellow Turban groups remained active in different parts of China for twenty years; resistance was finally subdued only in 205.1

04

What happened next?

  • The rise of the warlords: to fight the rebels, the court gave regional governors and generals new military powers. These commanders — including Dong Zhuo and Cao Cao — became warlords who competed for power.
  • The fall of the Han: in 189 the court collapsed into violence, and in 220 the last Han emperor abdicated. China was divided into the Three Kingdoms of Wei, Shu and Wu.
  • Religious movements: the related Way of the Celestial Masters established a semi-independent religious state in Hanzhong in western China, and Daoism developed into an organised religion.
05

What do others say?

At the time and afterwards

The official histories, written by scholars loyal to the imperial order, presented the Yellow Turbans as bandits and heretics led by a sorcerer. The famous fourteenth-century novel Romance of the Three Kingdoms opens with the heroes Liu Bei, Guan Yu and Zhang Fei fighting the Yellow Turbans, and this is how most people have known the story.

Historians' debates

  • Peasant revolution? Historians in the People's Republic of China long presented the rebellion as a great class struggle of peasants against the feudal ruling class. Others emphasise its religious character and its millenarian hopes.
  • What did they believe? Few records of the Way of Supreme Peace survive; some of its ideas may be preserved in the Daoist scripture Taipingjing (Scripture of Great Peace), but historians debate how closely they are linked.
  • How large? Figures such as 360,000 come from official sources and may be estimates or exaggerations.
06

The evidence

  • The Book of the Later Han and the Records of the Three Kingdoms, written in the third to fifth centuries.
  • The Daoist scripture Taipingjing, partly preserved in the Daoist canon.
  • Archaeological evidence of Han-period religious practices, such as tomb texts.
07

How is it remembered?

  • The Yellow Turbans are best known through Romance of the Three Kingdoms and the many films, television series and computer games based on it — almost always from the point of view of their enemies.
  • In the People's Republic, Zhang Jue was presented in school textbooks as the leader of a progressive peasant uprising.
08

Why does it matter?

The Yellow Turban Rebellion was one of the largest peasant uprisings of the ancient world. It showed how economic hardship, government corruption and religious hope could combine to mobilise vast numbers of ordinary people against an empire.

  • It began the collapse of the Han dynasty, one of the most important dynasties in Chinese history.
  • It was the first of a series of great peasant revolts in Chinese history, many inspired by religious visions of a new age.
  • It shows how official histories, and later fiction, can shape the memory of rebels.

Questions to think about

  3. Why did suppressing the rebellion end up weakening the Han dynasty even further?
