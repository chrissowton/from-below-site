The 1916 Central Asian Revolt (Ürkün)

When the Tsar ordered Central Asian men to be conscripted for labour behind the front lines, the region rose in revolt. The repression, and the flight across the mountains to China, cost well over a hundred thousand lives.

Where

01

What is the background to this event?

During the nineteenth century the Russian Empire conquered Central Asia. The region's peoples — Kazakhs, Kyrgyz, Uzbeks, Turkmen and others — were colonial subjects, without the rights of Russian citizens, and Muslims were exempt from military service.

The conquest was followed by large-scale settlement:1

  • Between 1906 and 1912, as part of the Stolypin land reforms, up to 500,000 peasant households from European Russia were resettled in Kazakhstan and the rest of Central Asia.
  • Much of the best land and water, especially the pastures used by nomadic herders, was taken for settlers and Cossacks. Local families were limited to small plots.
  • During the First World War, the region was required to supply cotton, livestock and taxes, and prices rose sharply.

On 25 June 1916, with Russia's armies short of manpower, Tsar Nicholas II decreed that Central Asian men aged 19 to 43 should be conscripted into labour battalions to dig trenches and build fortifications behind the front lines.1 The decree was announced at the height of the harvest, without explanation, and lists of conscripts were often compiled corruptly.1

03

What happened?

Resistance began in early July 1916 in the towns of the Fergana Valley and around Samarkand, where crowds protested against the lists of conscripts. On 12 July (old style), Tashkent rose in protest. The protesters demanded that the lists be made transparent, that the draft be postponed until after the harvest, and that one man from each family be allowed to stay at home.1 In Jizzakh, rebels killed Russian officials, and a punitive force retook the town, causing many civilian casualties.1

On 17 July, martial law was declared across the Turkestan military district.1 In August the revolt spread to the Semirechye region, in present-day Kyrgyzstan and south-eastern Kazakhstan:1

  • Kyrgyz, Kazakh and Dungan rebels attacked Russian settlements, especially around Lake Issyk-Kul and the town of Przhevalsk (now Karakol). About 2,300 Russian settlers were killed and more than 1,300 went missing, according to later figures.
  • Punitive expeditions of soldiers and armed settlers responded with mass killings of Kyrgyz and Kazakh civilians and the destruction of villages and herds.
  • In the Turgai region of Kazakhstan, rebels led by Amangeldy Imanov besieged the town of Turgai and held out until the revolution of 1917.

Tens of thousands of Kyrgyz and Kazakhs fled towards China, crossing the Tian Shan mountains in autumn and winter. Many died of cold and hunger in passes over 3,000 metres high.1 Estimates of the total number of Central Asians who died — through violence, famine and disease — range from around 100,000 to 500,000. In Kyrgyz historiography, it is often said that as much as 40 per cent of the Kyrgyz population died during the revolt and its aftermath.1

04

What happened next?

  • Revolution: the Tsar fell in February 1917. Some refugees returned from China, but the region was soon engulfed by civil war, famine and the Basmachi resistance to Soviet rule.
  • Soviet silence: in the Soviet period, the revolt was at first celebrated as an anti-colonial uprising against Tsarism, but discussion of the violence between settlers and local people was later restricted. Its full history was little studied until after independence in 1991.
  • Centenary: in 2016, the centenary was marked in Kyrgyzstan with commemorations, research and public debate about whether the killings should be called a genocide.
05

What do others say?

At the time

Russian officials blamed the revolt on Islamic agitators and foreign (Turkish and German) influence, and some Russian settlers saw it as a massacre of Christians. Local leaders pointed to the injustice of the decree and to the seizure of land by settlers.

Historians' debates

  • How many died? Estimates vary enormously. The Kyrgyz historian Shayyrkul Batyrbaeva estimated about 40,000 deaths among the Kyrgyz; Edward Sokol estimated around 270,000 Central Asians; other estimates are higher still.
  • Genocide? Some historians and Kyrgyz politicians call the repression a genocide; others prefer terms such as massacre or colonial violence, and stress that settlers were also killed. The issue remains politically sensitive between Kyrgyzstan and Russia.
  • Anti-colonial revolt or local conflict? Soviet historians presented it as a national liberation struggle; recent historians emphasise its diversity, with different causes and aims in different regions.

  • Oral testimonies of survivors and their descendants, collected in Kyrgyzstan and Kazakhstan, and among Kyrgyz communities in China.

07

How is it remembered?

  • Kyrgyzstan marks the anniversary of Ürkün each year, and memorials stand in the Issyk-Kul region and elsewhere.
  • The Kyrgyz writer Chingiz Aitmatov and others wrote about the tragedy, and it features in Kyrgyz and Kazakh poetry and song.
  • Amangeldy Imanov is honoured in Kazakhstan, where a town and many streets bear his name.
08

Why does it matter?

The revolt of 1916 was one of the largest anti-colonial uprisings of the First World War, and one of the least known outside Central Asia. It showed how the burdens of a world war fell on colonised peoples who had no say in it, and how settler colonialism produced conflicts over land that turned murderous.

  • For Kyrgyzstan and Kazakhstan, it is a defining national tragedy, known in Kyrgyz as Ürkün — the "exodus" or "flight".
  • It belongs to a wider wave of wartime resistance across colonial empires, from Ireland in 1916 to Africa.
  • The struggle over its memory shows how history can become a matter of politics between neighbouring states.
