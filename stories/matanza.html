The 1932 Uprising and La Matanza

Indigenous peasants and communists rose against a military government in coffee country. The army's revenge killed thousands and nearly wiped out a language.

When
22 January – February 1932
Where
Izalco, Juayúa and western El Salvador

See it on the map →

01

What is the background to this event?

In the early twentieth century, El Salvador's economy depended on coffee. Communal Indigenous lands had been abolished in the 1880s and taken over by coffee planters.

  • In western El Salvador, many Nahua-Pipil people worked as labourers on coffee estates for very low wages.
  • The Great Depression caused coffee prices to collapse. Wages were cut and many workers lost their jobs.
  • In 1930, Farabundo Martí and Miguel Mármol founded the Communist Party of El Salvador. Martí had fought alongside Augusto César Sandino against the US occupation of Nicaragua.1
  • In December 1931, a military coup brought General Maximiliano Hernández Martínez to power. In January 1932, the results of local elections in which communists had done well were cancelled.1
02

Who were the key people?

  • Farabundo Martí

    A communist leader who helped plan the uprising. He was arrested before it began and executed.

  • Feliciano Ama

    An Indigenous leader (cacique) in Izalco, who led Pipil rebels. He was publicly hanged.

  • Francisco Sánchez

    A rebel leader in Juayúa.

  • General Maximiliano Hernández Martínez

    The military ruler who ordered the repression and governed El Salvador until 1944.

03

What happened?

On the night of 22 January 1932, communist and Indigenous rebels launched an uprising in western El Salvador.1

  • Armed mostly with machetes, rebels captured several towns, including Juayúa, Nahuizalco, Tacuba and Izalco.1
  • Rebels killed a number of officials, soldiers and civilians, including the mayor of Izalco and a military commander, and some civilians were tortured and murdered.1 Estimates of the number killed by rebels vary widely: some accounts give figures as high as 2,000, while many historians put it far lower.1
  • The uprising was quickly crushed by the army within a few days.

What followed was a massacre. Over the following weeks, the army, police and civilian militias killed between 10,000 and 40,000 people.1

  • Most of the victims were Pipil peasants and non-combatants, killed simply for being Indigenous.1
  • Martí, Ama and other leaders were executed.1

04

What happened next?

  • Ethnocide: the massacre has been described as an ethnocide. Afraid of being identified as Indigenous, many Pipil stopped speaking their language and wearing traditional dress. Nawat almost disappeared.1
  • Military rule: Hernández Martínez ruled until 1944, and the military dominated El Salvador for decades.
  • Civil war: the guerrilla movement in the civil war of 1980–92 took the name of Farabundo Martí (FMLN). The war killed about 75,000 people, most of them civilians killed by government forces and death squads.
05

What do others say?

At the time

The government portrayed the uprising as a communist plot of savage violence, justifying the repression. Survivors remembered it as a massacre of innocent Indigenous people.

Historians' debates

  • Communist or Indigenous revolt? Historians debate how far the rising was led by the Communist Party and how far it grew from Indigenous communities' own grievances. Gould and Lauria-Santiago emphasise a broad rural movement involving both.
  • Rebel violence: the government exaggerated the rebels' violence; historians agree that rebels did kill officials and some civilians, but that the scale of killing by the state was vastly greater.
  • The death toll: estimates range from 10,000 to 40,000; the lack of records makes it impossible to be sure.1
06

The evidence

  • Oral histories collected from survivors decades later.
  • Government reports, newspapers and photographs, often heavily biased.
  • Diplomatic reports, such as those of British and US officials.
07

How is it remembered?

  • The massacre was rarely discussed publicly for decades. Today, Indigenous organisations commemorate it each January in Izalco.
  • In 2010, President Mauricio Funes apologised to Indigenous peoples for the massacre and other abuses.
  • Efforts are under way to revive the Nawat language.
08

Why does it matter?

La Matanza was one of the worst massacres in the history of the Americas, and a turning point for El Salvador's Indigenous peoples. For decades it was a silenced memory.

  • It shows how states can use a revolt as a pretext for mass killing.
  • It shows how violence can destroy a language and culture.
  • Its memory shaped the politics of El Salvador for the rest of the century.

Questions to think about

  1. Why do you think coffee workers in western El Salvador rose up in 1932?
  2. Why was the state's response so much more violent than the uprising?
  3. How can violence destroy a language?
  4. Why did the memory of 1932 remain silent for so long?
