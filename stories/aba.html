The Women's War (Ogu Umunwanyi)

Tens of thousands of Igbo and Ibibio women challenged British colonial rule, using their own traditions of collective protest.

When
November – December 1929
Where
Owerri and Calabar provinces, south-eastern Nigeria
Facts checked
2026-11-15

See it on the map →

01

What is the background to this event?

In south-eastern Nigeria the British practised what they called "indirect rule", governing through local intermediaries. Where no centralised chiefs existed, as in much of Igboland, they created "Warrant Chiefs" — men appointed by colonial officers who often had no traditional standing and who frequently abused their power, imposing fines, forced labour and bribes.

Igbo women had long held considerable economic and political influence. They dominated local trade, particularly in palm oil, and had their own associations and assemblies with recognised means of disciplining men who behaved badly. Colonial rule largely ignored these institutions. Several pressures came together in 1929:

  • Direct taxation had been imposed on men in 1928.
  • In late 1929 a colonial officer ordered a recount of households, livestock and property in the Bende district, and women feared that they too would soon be taxed.
  • Falling prices for palm produce, worsened by the world economic crisis, had already squeezed women's incomes.
02

Who were the key people?

  • Nwanyeruwa

    A woman in the town of Oloko whose confrontation with a census-taker on 18 November 1929 sparked the protests. She urged non-violent methods in the early stages.

  • Ikonnia, Mary Okezie and Nwugo

    Women leaders of the Oloko protests who later gave evidence to the colonial commissions of inquiry.

  • Warrant Chief Okugo

    The Oloko Warrant Chief whose agent carried out the count, and against whom the first protest was directed.

  • The market women

    Traders whose networks carried the call to protest across a vast area, faster than the colonial administration could react.

In their own words

03

What happened?

On 18 November 1929, a man working for Warrant Chief Okugo of Oloko came to count the property of a woman named Nwanyeruwa. An argument followed, and Nwanyeruwa raised the alarm.3 The women of Oloko summoned others by sending palm-oil leaves to neighbouring districts, and nearly 10,000 women gathered at Okugo's office, demanding his resignation and trial.3

The protesters used a long-established Igbo practice sometimes called "sitting on a man": gathering at the home or compound of a man who had offended, singing, dancing, mocking and refusing to leave until he changed his behaviour.4 When women sought written statements of their demands, Nwanyeruwa summarised them as:

"Women will not pay tax till the world ends [and] Chiefs were not to exist any more."Nwanyeruwa's summary of the women's demands, as recorded in the evidence to the colonial inquiry3

Over the following weeks the protests spread across Owerri and Calabar provinces, involving tens of thousands of women. As the movement grew, it became more confrontational:3

  • Ten native courts were destroyed and others damaged.
  • The houses of court officials and Warrant Chiefs were attacked.
  • European trading stores at Imo River, Aba, Mbawsi and Amata were looted.

The colonial government sent police and troops. On several occasions in December, troops opened fire on crowds of women, and more than fifty women are generally reported to have been killed.5 The last soldiers left Owerri on 27 December 1929, and more than thirty "collective punishment" inquiries were held in the following weeks.3

04

What happened next?

The colonial government established an Aba Commission of Inquiry in March 1930 and conducted wide-ranging investigations into local government. The outcome was a significant, if limited, victory:

  • Women were not taxed.
  • The Warrant Chief system was abolished and native courts were reorganised to reflect local institutions more closely.
  • Some women were appointed to the native court system.

Colonial officials called the events the "Aba riots". Igbo and Ibibio communities called them Ogu Umunwanyi and Ekong Iban — both meaning "the women's war".

05

Why does it matter?

The Women's War is one of the most significant episodes of anti-colonial resistance in West Africa, and it was organised and led by women using their own political traditions. It shows that forms of protest that colonial officials dismissed as disorderly or "primitive" were in fact sophisticated mechanisms of accountability.

  • It forced a colonial government to reform its system of local administration.
  • It anticipated later women's protests in Nigeria, such as the Abeokuta Women's Union's campaign against taxation in the 1940s.
  • The debate over its name — "riot" or "war" — is itself a lesson in how the language of history reflects whose perspective is being told.

What do others say?How it was seen at the time, and where historians disagree

At the time

British officials described the events as "riots" and blamed agitators, rumour and the excitability of the women. The two commissions of inquiry did, however, acknowledge widespread abuses by Warrant Chiefs and failures in colonial administration.

Historians' debates

  • Riot or war? Many historians now prefer the term used by the women themselves, "Women's War", arguing that "Aba riots" reflects the colonial view and hides the political organisation involved.
  • Causes: historians weigh the fear of taxation, the corruption of Warrant Chiefs, and the economic pressure of falling palm-oil prices differently.
  • Women's political institutions: Judith Van Allen argued that colonial rule destroyed women's traditional political power; others, such as Misty Bastian, have explored how the women creatively reinterpreted those traditions in 1929.
  • Violence: the movement began with non-violent sanctions but later included the destruction of property. Accounts differ on how far this was planned.
How do we know?The evidence, and a source from the time

The main sources are the colonial commissions of inquiry, which took evidence from hundreds of witnesses, including many women. These are invaluable, but they were produced by the colonial state and in English, through interpreters. Historians have supplemented them with:

  • Oral histories collected in later decades.
  • Colonial correspondence and military reports.
  • Songs and local traditions that preserved memories of the events.
How is it remembered?Memorials, anniversaries and arguments over memory
  • The Women's War is widely taught in Nigeria as an early anti-colonial movement.
  • Memorials have been erected in Aba and elsewhere, including a sculpture in Aba.
  • The events are often cited in discussions of women's political leadership in Africa.
