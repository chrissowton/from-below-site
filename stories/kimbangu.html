Simon Kimbangu and the Prophet Movement

For five months in 1921, a Kongo catechist drew huge crowds to his village with healing and preaching. Belgian authorities saw a threat to colonial rule, sentenced him to death, and kept him in prison for thirty years.

When
April – October 1921
Where
Nkamba, Lower Congo (now Kongo Central), Belgian Congo
Facts checked
2026-11-16

See it on the map →

01

What is the background to this event?

By 1921, the Belgian Congo had passed from King Leopold II's personal rule — notorious for forced labour and atrocities — to the Belgian state. Forced labour, taxes and missionary control of education continued.1

  • Simon Kimbangu was born in 1887 in Nkamba, in the Kongo region near the border with Angola.1
  • He was baptised by British Baptist missionaries in 1915 and worked as a catechist.1
  • The Kongo region had a long tradition of prophetic movements, including Kimpa Vita in the early 1700s.2

03

What happened?

In April 1921, Kimbangu began to preach and heal at Nkamba. News spread quickly, and thousands of people came from across the region.1

  • Crowds left mission stations, hospitals and workplaces to visit Nkamba, which became known as a "New Jerusalem".1
  • Kimbangu preached against fetishes, polygamy and dancing, and urged people to follow the Bible. He did not call for armed revolt.1
  • Some of his followers, however, spoke openly against white rule and taxation, and colonial officials and some missionaries feared a rising.2
  • Kimbangu was ordered arrested on 6 June 1921. He escaped and preached in hiding, then gave himself up in September.1
  • A military court tried him without a defence lawyer and sentenced him to death on 3 October 1921 for sedition and hostility to whites.1
  • The sentence was commuted to life imprisonment — according to most accounts, by King Albert I. Kimbangu was taken to Elisabethville (now Lubumbashi), more than 2,000 km away.1

Thousands of his followers were deported to labour camps and remote areas; estimates of the number vary widely.2

04

What happened next?

  • Prison: Kimbangu died in prison in Elisabethville on 12 October 1951, after 30 years.1
  • An underground church: his followers kept the movement alive in secret, despite bans and deportations.3
  • Recognition: the Belgian authorities recognised the Kimbanguist Church in December 1959, months before independence.3
  • Return: in 1960 his remains were reburied at Nkamba.1
05

What do others say?

At the time

Colonial officials described the movement as a dangerous anti-European uprising. Catholic missionaries were especially hostile; some Protestant missionaries were more sympathetic.2

Debates since

  • Political or religious? Some historians see Kimbanguism as an early nationalist movement; others stress that Kimbangu himself preached obedience and non-violence.2
  • Prophet: for the Kimbanguist Church, Kimbangu is a prophet and the Holy Spirit's envoy, which other Christian churches dispute.3

07

How is it remembered?

  • 6 April, the day of his first healing, is a public holiday in the Democratic Republic of the Congo.
  • Nkamba is a major site of pilgrimage.3
08

Why does it matter?

Kimbangu's movement showed how religion could become a form of resistance — an African-led church outside missionary and colonial control.

  • It became one of the largest independent churches in Africa.
  • It shows how colonial governments treated even non-violent religious movements as political threats.

Questions to think about

  1. Why did the Belgian authorities fear a healer who preached non-violence?
  2. Was Kimbangu's movement religious, political, or both?
  3. Why did so many people leave mission churches to follow him?

  • 1915The Chilembwe Uprising — A Baptist minister educated in the United States led a short-lived rising against forced labour, racism and the recruitment of Africans to die in Britain's war.
