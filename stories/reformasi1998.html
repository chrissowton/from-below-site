Reformasi: The Fall of Suharto

When Asia's financial crisis wrecked Indonesia's economy, students rose against a president who had ruled for 32 years. Four were shot dead at Trisakti University, riots killed more than a thousand, and Suharto resigned.

When
January – 21 May 1998
Where
Jakarta and cities across Indonesia
Facts checked
2026-11-17

See it on the map →

01

What is the background to this event?

Suharto had ruled Indonesia since the 1960s under his 'New Order', which severely restricted civil liberties and shared power between his Golkar organisation and the military.1

  • Only two political parties and Golkar were allowed to take part in elections. Suharto was re-elected unopposed every five years from 1973.1
  • Student protest had been banned since the 1970s, and critics such as the signatories of the 1980 'Petition of Fifty' were restricted or jailed.1
  • In 1997, Indonesia was the country hardest hit by the Asian financial crisis. The rupiah fell from about Rp 2,600 to the US dollar in August 1997 to over Rp 14,800 by January 1998, and many companies went bankrupt.1
  • In March 1998, Suharto was re-elected for a seventh term.1
02

Who were the key people?

  • University students

    Who organised demonstrations across the country from January 1998 and occupied the parliament building in May.

  • Elang Mulia Lesmana, Heri Hertanto, Hafidin Royan and Hendriawan Sie

    The four Trisakti University students shot dead on 12 May 1998.

  • Amien Rais

    Leader of the Muslim organisation Muhammadiyah, a prominent critic of Suharto.

  • Suharto

    President of Indonesia for 32 years, who resigned on 21 May 1998.

  • B. J. Habibie

    Vice-president, who succeeded Suharto and began the reforms.

03

What happened?

From January 1998, university students organised demonstrations across Indonesia.1

  • In May, the government raised fuel prices by 70%, which set off rioting in Medan.1
  • On 12 May, students at Trisakti University in Jakarta tried to march to parliament. Security forces kept them on campus, and men in police Mobile Brigade uniforms fired on them from a flyover, killing four students and injuring dozens.1
  • The killings set off riots across Jakarta and other cities. Many targeted Chinese Indonesians, who had long faced discrimination. More than 1,000 people are estimated to have died, many of them looters trapped in burning shopping centres, and at least 168 cases of rape were reported.2
  • Students occupied the parliament building. On 18 May, the speaker of parliament, a Suharto loyalist, called on him to resign.1
  • On 20 May, the army filled Jakarta's streets. Amien Rais called off a planned mass rally after warnings of bloodshed.1
  • When fourteen ministers refused to serve in a new cabinet, Suharto resigned on the morning of 21 May.1
04

What happened next?

  • Reformasi: Habibie's government allowed new parties, and 48 competed in the 1999 legislative election, the first free election since 1955.3
  • East Timor: Habibie called a referendum, and on 30 August 1999 the people of East Timor voted for independence.3
  • Justice: a report by human rights organisations concluded that officials consistently failed to deliver truth, accountability and reparations for the worst crimes of the period.1
05

Why does it matter?

Reformasi transformed Indonesia's political institutions, bringing free elections, a freer press and decentralisation. Indonesia's transition is often cited as a model of democratic change from below in Southeast Asia.1

  • 48 parties competed in the 1999 election, the first free election since 1955.3
  • On 30 August 1999 the people of East Timor voted for independence in a referendum.3
  • Human rights organisations found that officials consistently failed to deliver truth, accountability and reparations for the worst crimes.3

What do others say?How it was seen at the time, and where historians disagree

At the time

Students demanded reform and Suharto's resignation. In the end, even the speaker of parliament, a Suharto loyalist, joined the call.1

Debates since

  • From below? Students and protesters forced the issue, but Suharto finally went when his speaker, ministers and army commanders withdrew their support.1
  • Violence: the riots of May 1998, with more than 1,000 estimated deaths and attacks on Chinese Indonesians, are remembered as a national tragedy alongside the story of democratic change.2

Reading it

Suharto reads his resignation after 32 years in power. It is an official photograph, calm and formal. Nothing in it shows the students in the streets or the allies who had deserted him — the forces that made the moment happen.
