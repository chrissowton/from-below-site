The Arpilleristas of Chile

Under Pinochet's dictatorship, poor women stitched scenes of their lives — disappeared relatives, soup kitchens, protests — onto scraps of sacking. Smuggled abroad, their patchworks told the world what was happening.

When
1974 – 1990
Where
Santiago, Chile
Facts checked
2026-11-16

See it on the map →

01

What is the background to this event?

In September 1973, General Augusto Pinochet seized power in Chile in a military coup.

  • Thousands were killed or disappeared, and censorship was strict.1
  • Many poor women lost husbands and sons, and their families' income.1
  • The Catholic Church organised workshops where women could earn money by making arpilleras — appliqué pictures on burlap.1
02

Who were the key people?

  • The arpilleristas

    Women, mostly from poor neighbourhoods, who made the patchworks.

  • The Vicariate of Solidarity

    Catholic human rights organisation that distributed them abroad.

In their own words

03

What happened?

From the mid-1970s, women made arpilleras in church-organised workshops.1

  • The pictures showed disappearances, poverty, soup kitchens, protests and daily life under the dictatorship.
  • The Vicariate of Solidarity secretly sent them abroad, where they were sold to support the women.1
  • The police confiscated arpilleras, and the military called them "tapestries of infamy".1
  • Because of the danger, most arpilleras were made anonymously.1
04

What happened next?

  • Democracy: Chile returned to democracy in 1990.
  • Museums: arpilleras are now held in museums around the world, and the art form has spread to other countries.
05

Why does it matter?

The arpilleras turned women's sewing into testimony and resistance, at a time when speaking out was dangerous.

  • Women whose relatives had disappeared stitched their stories when speaking out was dangerous.
  • Arpilleras are now held in museums around the world, and the form has spread to other countries.
  • They are displayed at Chile's Museum of Memory and Human Rights.

What do others say?How it was seen at the time, and where historians disagree

At the time

The regime called the arpilleras defamatory; supporters abroad saw them as evidence of repression.1

Debates since

  • Art and survival: the workshops were both a source of income and a form of protest; writers such as Marjorie Agosín stress their role as women's testimony.3
  • Violence: the arpilleristas were non-violent; their work recorded the state's violence.1
