The San Fight to Return to the Central Kalahari

The Botswana government moved the San people out of their ancestral lands in the Central Kalahari. Led by Roy Sesana, they took the government to court — and won the right to go home.

When
1997 – 2011
Where
Central Kalahari Game Reserve, Botswana
Facts checked

01

What is the background to this event?

The San (also called Bushmen or Basarwa) have lived in the Kalahari for thousands of years as hunters and gatherers.1

  • The Central Kalahari Game Reserve was created in 1961, partly to protect the land of the San who lived there.
  • From the mid-1990s, the government sought to move the San out, saying it could not provide services inside the reserve and that their presence conflicted with conservation.12
  • In 1997, most of the San in the reserve were resettled in new settlements such as New Xade.12

In their own words

03

What happened?

Many of the San resettled outside the reserve faced poverty, unemployment, alcoholism and disease in the new settlements.2

  • In 2002, the government cut off services in the reserve, including capping the borehole that supplied water, to persuade the remaining residents to leave.
  • In 2005, the government resumed relocations, leaving only about 250 people in the reserve.1
  • Roy Sesana and other San took the government to the High Court. It was one of the longest and most expensive cases in Botswana's history.
  • In December 2006, the High Court ruled that the evictions had been unlawful and that the San had the right to live in the reserve.1
  • In 2011, the Court of Appeal ruled that the San had the right to use and drill for water in the reserve.

04

What happened next?

  • Limited return: the government interpreted the ruling narrowly, allowing only the named applicants and their families to return without permits.
  • Hunting: a ban on hunting, introduced in 2014, made traditional life harder.
  • Diamonds: in 2014, the Ghaghoo diamond mine opened in the reserve, on San ancestral land.1
  • Recognition: Roy Sesana received the Right Livelihood Award in 2005.
05

What do others say?

At the time

The government said resettlement brought the San schools, clinics and development, and denied that diamonds were the motive. San campaigners and their supporters, including Survival International, said the San were being forced off their land.12

Debates since

  • Diamonds: campaigners point to diamond deposits in the reserve; the government says relocation was about development and conservation.1
  • Outside campaigns: some in Botswana criticised the role of foreign campaign groups; San leaders defended the alliance.
  • Non-violence: the campaign used the courts and publicity, not force.

How is it remembered?

  • The case is studied around the world as an example of indigenous peoples using the law to defend their land.
08

Why does it matter?

The San case became a landmark for indigenous land rights in Africa, showing that a small, marginalised people could defeat a government in court.

  • It raised questions about conservation that excludes the people who live on the land.
