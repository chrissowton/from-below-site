Zambia's Return to Multi-Party Democracy

Riots over rising prices, a failed coup and a trade-union-led movement ended seventeen years of one-party rule. When Kenneth Kaunda lost the election, he handed over power peacefully.

When
June 1990 – November 1991
Where
Lusaka and the Copperbelt, Zambia
Facts checked
2026-11-17

See it on the map →

01

What is the background to this event?

Kenneth Kaunda had led Zambia since independence in 1964.2

  • In 1972, all parties except Kaunda's United National Independence Party (UNIP) were banned, and a new constitution in 1973 made Zambia a one-party state.1
  • Kaunda was the only candidate in the presidential elections of 1978, 1983 and 1988, each time officially approved by over 80% of voters.2
  • Zambia's economy, dependent on copper, was in crisis. Austerity measures agreed with the IMF, including the removal of price controls and subsidies, caused prices to soar.3

03

What happened?

In June 1990, rising prices set off three days of rioting in Lusaka and other towns. About 27 people died and more than 100 were wounded.3

  • Amid the riots, Kaunda announced a referendum on whether to allow other parties, though he argued that only a one-party state could prevent tribalism and violence.2
  • Hours later, on 1 July 1990, an army officer, Lieutenant Mwamba Luchembe, announced on the radio that the military had taken over, citing the riots. The coup attempt collapsed within hours, but it weakened Kaunda further.3
  • In July 1990, critics of UNIP formed the Movement for Multi-Party Democracy, led by the trade union leader Frederick Chiluba.4
  • Strikes spread, including among postal and bank workers.3 By September 1990, opposition demands forced Kaunda to cancel the referendum and accept constitutional changes ending UNIP's monopoly. He signed them into law in December.2
  • On 31 October 1991, Zambia held its first multi-party elections since 1968. Chiluba won about 75% of the presidential vote, and the MMD won 125 of 150 elected seats.15
  • On 2 November 1991, Kaunda handed power to Chiluba.2

04

What happened next?

  • A peaceful handover: Kaunda became the second mainland African head of state to allow free multi-party elections and give up power peacefully after losing; the first was Mathieu Kérékou of Benin earlier that year.2
  • Disappointment: by the end of Chiluba's first term, the MMD's commitment to reform had faded. In 1996, constitutional amendments shut Kaunda and other opposition leaders out of the election.4
05

What do others say?

At the time

Kaunda argued that only a one-party state could prevent tribalism and violence; the opposition argued that one-party rule had brought economic decline and repression.2

Debates since

  • From below or from outside? The IMF's austerity measures set off the riots, while the movement was built by Zambian trade unions and civil society.34
  • Violence: about 27 people died in the riots of June 1990; the change of government itself was peaceful.32

07

How is it remembered?

  • Kaunda lived to see many more elections; he died in 2021, aged 97.2
08

Why does it matter?

Zambia's transition was part of the wave that ended one-party rule across Africa in the early 1990s. A movement built on trade unions and popular anger at rising prices replaced the founding president of the nation at the ballot box.24

09

What other events are like this?

  • 1990Benin's National Conference — Strikes by unpaid teachers, students and civil servants forced Benin's military ruler to call a national conference. The delegates declared themselves sovereign, took power peacefully — and inspired democratic movements across Africa.
  • 1993Malawi's Vote for Democracy — After 30 years under a 'President for Life', Malawi's Catholic bishops broke the silence with a pastoral letter. Strikes, protests and an exile's return followed, and in 1993 Malawians voted to end one-party rule.
  • 1935The Copperbelt Strike of 1935 — When the colonial government raised the tax on African mineworkers, they walked out at three of the Copperbelt's great mines. Police shot six people dead — but the strike is remembered as the birth of Zambian nationalism.
