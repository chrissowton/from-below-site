The Brunei Revolt

The Brunei People's Party won every elected seat in 1962, but was kept from power. Its armed wing rose against the Sultan and the British. The revolt was crushed within days — but it changed Brunei's future.

When
8 – 17 December 1962
Where
Brunei Town and Seria, Brunei
Facts checked
2026-11-16

See it on the map →

01

What is the background to this event?

Brunei was a British protectorate whose oil, discovered at Seria in 1929, brought the Sultan great wealth.1

  • In 1959, the Sultan created a legislature, half nominated and half elected.1
  • In September 1962, the left-wing Brunei People's Party (PRB), led by A. M. Azahari, won all the contested seats.1
  • The party opposed joining the proposed Federation of Malaysia, and wanted a united, independent North Borneo with the Sultan as constitutional monarch.1
  • An armed wing, the North Kalimantan National Army (TNKU), was supplied with help from Indonesia.1

In their own words

03

What happened?

On 8 December 1962, the TNKU attacked police stations, government buildings and the Shell oil installations at Seria.1

  • The rebels failed to capture Brunei Town or the Sultan, and the revolt began to break down within hours.1
  • At Seria and elsewhere, rebels took Europeans and others as hostages.
  • British forces, including Gurkhas, Royal Marines and Highlanders, were flown in from Singapore and retook the towns, including Limbang in neighbouring Sarawak.1
  • Dozens of rebels and a smaller number of soldiers, police and civilians were killed; thousands of people were detained.

04

What happened next?

  • Emergency rule: the Sultan declared a state of emergency, banned the party and has ruled by decree ever since.
  • Malaysia: in 1963, the Sultan decided not to join Malaysia.1
  • Confrontation: the revolt is seen as an early stage of the Indonesia–Malaysia confrontation.1
  • Independence: Brunei became fully independent in 1984, as an absolute monarchy.
05

What do others say?

At the time

Britain and the Sultan described the rising as an Indonesian-backed rebellion; the party said it was a struggle against colonialism and the Malaysia plan.1

Debates since

  • Local or foreign? Historians argue that local opposition to Malaysia has been under-represented, though Indonesian support was real.12
  • Violence: rebels attacked police stations and took hostages; British forces killed many rebels and detained thousands.2

07

How is it remembered?

  • The revolt is rarely discussed publicly in Brunei.
08

Why does it matter?

The revolt was the culmination of a popular movement that had won an election but was denied power.

  • Its defeat shaped Brunei as one of the world's few remaining absolute monarchies.

Questions to think about

  1. Why did a party that won every elected seat turn to armed revolt?
  2. Why did the people of Brunei oppose joining Malaysia?
  3. What role did oil play?
