Pūnana Leo: Bringing Back Hawaiian

When fewer than fifty children still spoke Hawaiian, families opened 'language nests' — preschools that were at first illegal — and began the revival of a language that had been banned from schools for ninety years.

When
1983 – present
Where
Kekaha, Kauaʻi, Hawaiʻi

See it on the map →

01

What is the background to this event?

In the nineteenth century, the Hawaiian Kingdom was one of the most literate countries in the world. Hawaiian was the language of government, schools and more than a hundred Hawaiian-language newspapers.1

  • In 1893, American businessmen, backed by US marines, overthrew Queen Liliʻuokalani. Hawaiʻi was annexed by the United States in 1898.
  • In 1896, the new government made English the only language of instruction in schools; using Hawaiian as a medium of education was in effect outlawed. Legal restrictions on its use in education remained until 1986.1
  • Children were punished for speaking Hawaiian at school, and many parents stopped speaking it to their children.
  • By the early 1980s, fewer than fifty children under the age of eighteen were native speakers of Hawaiian, most of them on the tiny island of Niʻihau.

From the 1970s, the "Hawaiian Renaissance" saw a revival of music, hula, voyaging and political activism. In 1978, Hawaiian was made an official language of the State of Hawaiʻi, alongside English.

02

Who were the key people?

  • Larry Kimura, William 'Pila' Wilson and Ilei Beniamina

    Among the teachers and language activists who founded the ʻAha Pūnana Leo in 1983.

  • The families

    Parents who sent their children to the language nests, learned Hawaiian themselves, and lobbied to change the law.

  • The kūpuna (elders)

    Native speakers, many from Niʻihau, who taught in the preschools — even though, at first, the law did not recognise them as qualified teachers.

03

What happened?

The organisation ʻAha Pūnana Leo was founded in 1983 by a group of Hawaiian-language educators and parents.1 Inspired by the Māori kōhanga reo ("language nests") in New Zealand, and by the Hawaiian-language schools of the nineteenth century, they created preschools in which children would be taught entirely in Hawaiian.1

  • The first Pūnana Leo ("voice nest") opened in 1984 at Kekaha, on Kauaʻi. It was initially illegal.1
  • The law effectively prevented the last native speakers of Hawaiian from teaching, because they lacked the formal teaching qualifications required.1
  • Families lobbied for three years to remove these legal barriers. They faced opposition, including from the established preschool sector, which argued that being taught by untrained teachers could harm children's development.1
  • In 1986, the state legislature changed the law to permit Hawaiian-medium education.1

The public school system was slow to provide Hawaiian-language education as the children grew older, so parents kept pushing, step by step: from a preschool that began outside the law, to kindergarten and intermediate classes run as boycotts of the state system, to state-run Hawaiian-medium schools.1 In 1987, the state began Hawaiian immersion programmes in public schools (Kula Kaiapuni).

04

What happened next?

  • A complete education: by the late 1990s, it was possible to be educated in Hawaiian from preschool to high school. The first class of Pūnana Leo students graduated from high school in 1999.
  • University: in 2002, the University of Hawaiʻi at Hilo awarded the first master's degree completed in Hawaiian, and later the first doctorate in an Indigenous language in the United States.
  • Growth: by 2023 there were thirteen Pūnana Leo preschools on five islands, and thousands of children had passed through Hawaiian-medium education.
  • A model: the Pūnana Leo has inspired Indigenous language revival programmes across North America and beyond.
05

What do others say?

At the time

Opponents of the early Pūnana Leo argued that children taught only in Hawaiian would fall behind in English and in school, and that the elders teaching them were not qualified. Supporters argued that bilingual children do well, and that the ban was itself a colonial injustice.

Commentators' debates

  • Outcomes: research has found that students in Hawaiian immersion generally do as well as or better than others in English and other subjects, though debate continues about resources and teacher training.
  • New speakers: some native speakers and linguists note differences between the Hawaiian of the immersion schools and the Hawaiian of Niʻihau and older speakers.
  • Language and sovereignty: for many, the revival of Hawaiian is inseparable from wider questions about the overthrow of the Hawaiian Kingdom and Hawaiian sovereignty.
06

The evidence

  • The 1896 school law and later legislation, and the records of the 1986 law change.
  • The publications and records of the ʻAha Pūnana Leo.
  • The rich archive of nineteenth-century Hawaiian-language newspapers, now being digitised, which provides material for the revival.
07

How is it remembered?

  • The anniversary of the 1986 law change and the founding of the Pūnana Leo are celebrated by the Hawaiian-language community.
  • Hawaiian Language Month (ʻŌlelo Hawaiʻi Month) is celebrated each February in Hawaiʻi.
08

Why does it matter?

The Pūnana Leo movement is one of the most successful examples of Indigenous language revival in the world. Families, teachers and elders, defying the law, brought a language back from the edge of extinction by teaching it to the youngest children.

  • It shows that language loss is not inevitable, and that communities can reverse it.
  • It links language to culture, family and identity: parents are expected to learn Hawaiian and take part in the schools.
  • It is part of the wider struggle of Native Hawaiians for recognition and self-determination.

Questions to think about

  1. Why do you think the 1896 law had such a devastating effect on the Hawaiian language?
  2. Why did the founders choose to begin with preschool children?
  3. Is it ever justified to break the law to save a language?
  4. What can other endangered languages learn from the Pūnana Leo?
