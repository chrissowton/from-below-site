The Battle of Wackersdorf

A rural Catholic region of Bavaria rose against a nuclear reprocessing plant. Farmers, priests, doctors and rock stars joined tens of thousands of protesters — and some violent clashes — until the project was abandoned.

When
1985 – 1989
Where
Wackersdorf, Upper Palatinate, Bavaria, West Germany
Facts checked
2026-11-16

See it on the map →

01

What is the background to this event?

In the early 1980s, the West German nuclear industry and the Bavarian government chose Wackersdorf, a former coal-mining area, as the site for a plant to reprocess spent nuclear fuel (in German, Wiederaufarbeitungsanlage, or WAA).1

  • The region was conservative and Catholic, and the Bavarian government of Franz Josef Strauss was strongly pro-nuclear.1
  • The local district administrator, Hans Schuierer, refused to sign the permits. The Bavarian parliament passed a law to bypass him, known as the "Lex Schuierer".1
02

Who were the key people?

  • Hans Schuierer

    Social Democrat district administrator of Schwandorf, who refused to approve the plant.

  • Franz Josef Strauss

    Minister-President of Bavaria and leading supporter of the plant; died in 1988.

  • Local residents

    Farmers, Catholic parishioners and families who formed the backbone of the resistance.

  • Alois Sonnleitner

    A 38-year-old engineer who died during the protests in March 1986.

03

What happened?

When clearing work began in December 1985, protesters occupied the site and built a camp of huts, the "Free Republic of Wackerland". After 18 days, police cleared it in January 1986. Police repeatedly cleared camps on the site; in one operation, 869 demonstrators were detained.12

  • Protesters held prayers at a roadside shrine near the fence, which became a symbol of the resistance.1
  • At Easter 1986, more than 100,000 people demonstrated. Police used CS gas against demonstrators for the first time in West Germany.2
  • Alois Sonnleitner died during the Easter protests; his death was linked by protesters to the use of tear gas, which the authorities disputed.2
  • After the Chernobyl disaster in April 1986, protests grew. Over Whitsun in May, some militant protesters attacked police with stones and steel balls fired from slingshots, and set police vehicles on fire. Police threw tear gas from helicopters. More than 400 people were injured on both sides.2
  • In 1986, the "Anti-WAAhnsinns" rock festivals — a pun on WAA and Wahnsinn, "madness" — drew huge crowds in support of the protests.1
04

What happened next?

  • Abandoned: in 1989 the project was abandoned. German spent fuel was sent instead to La Hague in France and Sellafield in Britain.1
  • Why? It remains unclear whether protests, economics or the death of Strauss in 1988 led to the decision.1
  • Today: the site is an industrial park, including a BMW plant.1
  • Nuclear exit: Germany later decided to phase out nuclear power; its last reactors closed in 2023.
05

Why does it matter?

Wackersdorf showed that opposition to nuclear power could unite conservative rural communities with radical activists.

  • It was one of the largest protest movements in post-war Germany.
  • It contributed to the growth of the German Greens and the anti-nuclear movement.
  • The plant was abandoned in 1989, though it remains unclear whether protest, economics or the death of Bavaria's leader decided it.1

What do others say?How it was seen at the time, and where historians disagree

At the time

The Bavarian government presented the protesters as violent extremists. Residents insisted most were peaceful local people, and accused the police of brutality.1

Debates since

  • Violence: most protesters were peaceful, but some militants attacked police; police used CS gas, stun grenades and rubber bullets against crowds.12
  • Why it ended: historians debate whether protest, economics or politics was decisive.1
