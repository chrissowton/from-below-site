Martyrs' Day: The Flag Protests in Panama

Panamanian students marched into the US-run Canal Zone to raise their country's flag. Days of fighting followed — and, in time, Panama won back its canal.

When
9 – 12 January 1964
Where
Panama City and the Canal Zone, Panama

See it on the map →

01

What is the background to this event?

Panama became independent from Colombia in 1903 with US help. Under the Hay–Bunau-Varilla Treaty, it gave the United States control of a ten-mile-wide strip across the country — the Canal Zone — "in perpetuity".1

  • The Canal Zone was effectively US territory, with its own police, schools and courts. Its American residents, the "Zonians", lived separate and privileged lives.1
  • Many Panamanians resented this foreign enclave in the middle of their country.
  • In 1963, President Kennedy agreed that Panama's flag would fly alongside the US flag in the Zone. But after his assassination, the Zone governor ordered that neither flag be flown outside some civilian buildings.1
  • Angry Zonians raised US flags anyway, including at Balboa High School, where students guarded the flagpole.1
02

Who were the key people?

  • Students of the Instituto Nacional

    About 150–200 students of Panama's leading high school who marched into the Zone with their flag.

  • Ascanio Arosemena

    A 20-year-old student, the first Panamanian killed, who became a symbol of the martyrs.

  • Canal Zone police and US soldiers

    Who confronted the protesters.

  • Roberto Chiari

    Panama's president, who broke off diplomatic relations with the United States.

03

What happened?

On 9 January 1964, 150 to 200 students from the Instituto Nacional marched into the Canal Zone to Balboa High School, carrying their school's Panamanian flag.1

  • At the flagpole, they were confronted by Zonian students and adults and by Canal Zone police. In the scuffle, the Panamanian flag was torn.1
  • As news spread, thousands of Panamanians gathered at the border of the Zone. Rioting broke out; protesters threw stones, set fire to buildings and attacked US property.
  • Canal Zone police were overwhelmed, and the US Army was called in.1

After three days of fighting, about 22 Panamanians and four US soldiers had been killed, and hundreds were injured.1 Some Panamanians died in a fire in a building set alight during the riots.

Panama broke off diplomatic relations with the United States and demanded a new treaty.

04

What happened next?

  • Negotiations: relations were restored in April 1964, and the United States agreed to negotiate a new treaty.
  • The Torrijos–Carter Treaties: in 1977, the two countries signed treaties to transfer control of the Canal to Panama. The incident is considered a significant factor in that decision.1
  • Handover: Panama took full control of the Canal on 31 December 1999.
  • 1989: the United States invaded Panama in December 1989 to remove General Manuel Noriega, killing hundreds of Panamanians — a reminder that US power remained.
05

What do others say?

At the time

Panama accused the United States of aggression and brought the matter to the Organisation of American States and the United Nations. US officials blamed Panamanian agitators and communists for the riots.

Historians' debates

  • Who tore the flag? Accounts differ: Panamanians say Zonians and police tore it deliberately; some US accounts say it was torn accidentally in the scuffle.
  • Violence: US forces used live ammunition against crowds; rioters also used firearms and burned buildings. An international commission investigated, and historians continue to debate responsibility.
  • Anti-Americanism: Alan McPherson argues that the protests reflected nationalist anger at US policies rather than simple hatred of Americans.
06

The evidence

  • The torn flag, preserved as a national relic.
  • Photographs, newsreels and press reports.
  • US and Panamanian government reports, and the report of the International Commission of Jurists.
07

How is it remembered?

  • 9 January is Martyrs' Day, a national day of mourning in Panama.
  • The Avenida de los Mártires and the Monument to the Martyrs commemorate those killed.
  • A former Canal Zone building is named after Ascanio Arosemena.
08

Why does it matter?

The flag protests showed the strength of Panamanian nationalism and the anger caused by a foreign enclave. Students' actions helped set in motion the return of the Canal.

  • It shows the power of symbols, such as flags, in political struggles.
  • It shows how young people can trigger historic change.

What other events are like this?

  • 1927Sandino's War against the US Marines — When Nicaragua's generals made peace under US supervision, one refused. Augusto César Sandino led a peasant army in a six-year guerrilla war against the US occupation.
  • 1937The Ponce Massacre — On Palm Sunday, police opened fire on a peaceful march by Puerto Rican Nationalists. Seventeen civilians and two police officers died — and an independent inquiry called it a massacre.
  • 1919Egypt's 1919 Revolution — When Britain exiled the nationalist leader Saad Zaghlul, Egyptians of every class and religion rose up in strikes and demonstrations — and women marched in public for the first time.
