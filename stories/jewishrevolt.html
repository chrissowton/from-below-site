The Great Jewish Revolt against Rome

After a Roman governor looted the Temple treasury, the people of Judaea rose against the greatest empire of the age. The revolt ended with the destruction of Jerusalem's Temple and the siege of Masada.

When
66 – 73/74
Where
Jerusalem and Masada, Judaea
Facts checked
2026-11-16

See it on the map →

01

What is the background to this event?

Judaea had been under Roman rule since 63 BCE, and became a Roman province in 6 CE.1

  • Heavy taxes, corrupt governors and Roman disregard for Jewish religion caused growing anger.1
  • In 66, the governor Gessius Florus took money from the Temple treasury.1
  • There was also tension between rich and poor, and between Jews and Greeks in mixed cities.
02

Who were the key people?

  • Eleazar ben Simon

    A leader of the Zealots in Jerusalem.

  • John of Gischala and Simon bar Giora

    Rival rebel commanders in Jerusalem.

  • Eleazar ben Ya'ir

    Leader of the Sicarii at Masada.

  • Josephus

    A Jewish commander who surrendered to Rome and wrote the main history of the war.

  • Vespasian and Titus

    Roman generals, and later emperors.

In their own words

03

What happened?

In 66, protests in Jerusalem turned into an uprising. The rebels overran the Roman garrison and killed its soldiers.1

  • The Roman governor of Syria, Cestius Gallus, marched on Jerusalem but was defeated at Beth Horon.1
  • Vespasian reconquered Galilee in 67. In Jerusalem, rival rebel factions fought a civil war among themselves.1
  • In 70, Titus besieged Jerusalem. The city fell, and the Temple was destroyed.1
  • The last rebels held out at the fortress of Masada until 73 or 74. According to Josephus, the defenders killed themselves rather than surrender.1

04

What happened next?

  • Judaism transformed: without the Temple, rabbinic Judaism developed, centred on the academy at Yavne.1
  • Further revolts: the Diaspora revolt (115–117) and the Bar Kokhba revolt (132–136) followed.
  • Rome: a special tax was imposed on Jews across the empire, and the Arch of Titus celebrated the victory.
05

What do others say?

At the time

Rome saw the rebels as bandits; many Jewish leaders, including some priests, opposed the revolt.1

Debates since

  • Casualties: Josephus claimed 1.1 million died and 97,000 were enslaved; historians think these figures are exaggerated, but agree the toll was enormous.1
  • Violence on all sides: the Romans crucified and enslaved huge numbers; the Sicarii assassinated Jewish opponents as well as Romans, and the factions killed one another.1
  • Masada: archaeologists and historians debate whether the mass suicide happened as Josephus describes.2
06

The evidence

  • Josephus, The Jewish War.
  • Excavations at Masada and Jerusalem; coins minted by the rebels.

Primary source: Coin

What is it?
Coin
Made by, and when
Rebel mint, Jerusalem, 67–68 CE

The rebels minted their own silver coins, dated by the years of the revolt and inscribed in the old Hebrew script. Minting coins was a claim to be an independent state.

Questions to ask of this source

  1. Why would rebels go to the trouble of minting coins in the middle of a war?
  2. Why did they date the coins by the years of the revolt?
  3. What can coins tell us that written histories like Josephus's cannot?
07

How is it remembered?

  • Masada became a powerful symbol in modern Israel; the Arch of Titus still stands in Rome.
08

Why does it matter?

The revolt is one of the most famous risings of a small people against an empire.

  • It also shows how divisions within a movement can weaken it.

Questions to think about

  1. Why did the people of Judaea rebel against Rome?
  2. Why did the rebel factions fight each other?
  3. How reliable is Josephus as a source?
  4. Why has Masada been remembered in such different ways?
09

What other events are like this?

  • 60 CEBoudica's Revolt — When Roman officials seized the kingdom of the Iceni, flogged its queen and raped her daughters, Boudica led the peoples of eastern Britain in a revolt that burned three Roman towns — before ending in a massacre.
  • 69 CEThe Revolt of the Batavi — A people of the Rhine delta who supplied Rome with soldiers turned their weapons on the empire. Led by a prince who had served Rome for decades, they destroyed two legions before making peace.
  • 147 BCEViriathus and the Lusitanian War — After a Roman governor massacred Lusitanians who had laid down their arms, one survivor — a shepherd turned warrior — led eight years of resistance to Rome. Rome could not defeat him in battle, so it paid his own men to kill him.
