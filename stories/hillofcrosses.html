The Hill of Crosses

Soviet authorities bulldozed it three times. Each time, Lithuanians crept back at night and planted new crosses — until the hill became a symbol of peaceful resistance.

When
1831 – 1990
Where
Near Šiauliai, Lithuania
Facts checked
2026-11-16

See it on the map →

01

What is the background to this event?

The first crosses are believed to have been placed on the hill after the uprising of 1831, by families who could not find the bodies of rebels killed by Russian forces.1

  • The hill became a place to pray for loved ones and for the nation.1
  • After the Second World War, Lithuania was occupied by the Soviet Union, which persecuted religion.1
02

Who were the key people?

  • Lithuanian pilgrims

    Who brought crosses, often at night.

  • The Soviet authorities and the KGB

    Who tried to destroy the site.

03

What happened?

Under Soviet rule, the Hill of Crosses became a symbol of opposition to Soviet suppression.1

  • The authorities bulldozed the site three times.1
  • They blocked roads, posted guards from the KGB and the army, and declared that the crosses had no artistic value.1
  • People caught bringing crosses were punished and sometimes imprisoned.1
  • Despite the risks, Lithuanians kept bringing crosses to the hill, often at night.1
04

What happened next?

  • Independence: Lithuania regained its independence in 1990–91.
  • Growth: the number of crosses rose from about 55,000 in 1990 to about 100,000 by 2006.1
  • Pope: Pope John Paul II visited the hill in 1993.
05

Why does it matter?

The Hill of Crosses shows how a simple, repeated act can become a powerful form of non-violent resistance.1

  • The number of crosses grew from about 55,000 in 1990 to about 100,000 by 2006.1
  • Pope John Paul II visited the hill in 1993.
  • It is now one of Lithuania's most visited places of pilgrimage.1

What do others say?How it was seen at the time, and where historians disagree

At the time

The authorities saw the hill as religious "superstition" and nationalist defiance; pilgrims saw it as faith and memory.1

Debates since

  • Faith and nation: the hill mixes Catholic devotion and Lithuanian national identity.1
  • Violence: the resistance was entirely peaceful; the state destroyed the crosses and punished those who brought them.1
