The Chichibu Uprising: 'Year One of Freedom'

Crushed by debt and falling silk and rice prices, thousands of farmers in the hills near Tokyo rose up, seized the district office and declared a new era of 'freedom and self-government'. Within ten days they had been crushed.

When
31 October – November 1884
Where
Chichibu, Saitama, Japan
Facts checked
2026-11-22

See it on the map →

01

What is the background to this event?

After the Meiji Restoration of 1868, Japan modernised rapidly, paid for largely by taxes on land.1

  • The Land Tax Reform of 1873 led many farmers to lose their land when they could not pay. Tenants often paid over half their crop as rent.1
  • From 1881, the government's deflationary policy drove rice prices down, causing more bankruptcies. Some families had to send daughters to textile mills or sell them into prostitution to pay their taxes.1
  • In 1884 alone there were about sixty riots. Many were linked to the Freedom and People's Rights Movement, which called for representative government and basic rights.1

In their own words

03

What happened?

On 31 October 1884, after creditors refused to allow a pause in loan repayments, farmers in the Chichibu district rose up.1

  • Armed with farm tools, bamboo spears, swords, wooden cannon and hunting guns, they seized the district office and named it the "Headquarters of the Revolutionary Army".1
  • They attacked moneylenders' offices to destroy the records of their debts.1
  • The leaders adopted a new calendar and issued decrees dated "Year One of Freedom and Self-Government".1
  • They marched towards Tokyo, where they first met serious resistance. About ten days after the seizure of the district office, the uprising was crushed.1

04

What happened next?

  • Punishment: nearly 3,000 people were tried and convicted, 300 as felons. Seven leaders were sentenced to death, and five were hanged in February 1885.1
  • Dismissed: the government described the rebels as little more than hooligans.1
  • Remembered: decades later a monument to the dead was put up, but many of those involved have never had their names officially cleared. A film, Kusa no Ran, marked the 120th anniversary in 2004.1
05

What do others say?

At the time

The government dismissed the rebels as hooligans; the rebels presented themselves as a revolutionary army bringing a 'new rule of benevolence'.1

Debates since

  • Debt or democracy? The traditional view sees an economic revolt; the historian Irokawa Daikichi argues that its leaders were inspired by the Freedom and People's Rights Movement and sought to challenge the government itself.1
  • Violence: the number of rebels killed is unknown.1
06

The evidence

  • Trial records, government reports, and the rebels' own banners, slogans and decrees.1

There are few surviving words from the rebels themselves, apart from their slogans and decrees. Memorials put up long afterwards show how the uprising, once dismissed as the work of hooligans, came to be honoured locally.

07

How is it remembered?

  • A memorial to the uprising stands at Onraku-ji temple in Chichibu, and a cenotaph honours those who took part.
08

Why does it matter?

Chichibu was the largest popular uprising of the Meiji period. It shows that Japan's modernisation was resisted from below — by farmers who demanded not only relief from debt but freedom and self-government.1

Questions to think about

  1. How did modernisation make life harder for Chichibu's farmers?
  2. Why did the rebels destroy records of debts?
  3. What did they mean by 'Year One of Freedom and Self-Government'?
  4. Was this an economic revolt, a political one, or both?

What other events are like this?

  • 1894The Donghak Peasant Revolution — Korean peasants inspired by a new religion rose against corrupt officials, briefly governed their province through peasant councils, and then fought the Japanese army.
  • 1855The Santal Hul — Santal farmers who had cleared the forests were trapped by moneylenders and landlords backed by colonial courts. Led by four brothers and two sisters, they rose in their tens of thousands.
  • 1851The Taiping Heavenly Kingdom — A failed examination candidate who believed he was the younger brother of Jesus led millions of poor Chinese against the Qing dynasty. His Heavenly Kingdom promised equality — and the war that followed was the deadliest civil war in history.
