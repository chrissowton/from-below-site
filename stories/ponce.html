Civil rights & anti-racism

The Ponce Massacre

On Palm Sunday, police opened fire on a peaceful march by Puerto Rican Nationalists. Seventeen civilians and two police officers died — and an independent inquiry called it a massacre.

When
21 March 1937
Where
Ponce, Puerto Rico

See it on the map →

01

What is the background to this event?

The United States took Puerto Rico from Spain in 1898. Puerto Ricans were made US citizens in 1917, but the island was governed by a governor appointed in Washington, and its economy was dominated by large US-owned sugar companies.

  • The Great Depression brought mass unemployment and hunger.
  • The Nationalist Party, led by the Harvard-educated lawyer Pedro Albizu Campos, demanded independence. In 1936 Albizu Campos and other leaders were convicted of seditious conspiracy and imprisoned in Atlanta.
  • The governor, General Blanton Winship, a former army officer, was determined to crush the Nationalists. Violence had already occurred on both sides, including the killing of Nationalists by police in Río Piedras in 1935 and the killing of a police chief by Nationalists in 1936.
02

Who were the key people?

  • The Nationalists and the Cadets of the Republic

    Members of the Nationalist Party and its uniformed youth organisation, together with their families and onlookers, who joined the march.

  • General Blanton Winship

    The US-appointed governor, who ordered the police to stop the march 'by all means necessary'.

  • Colonel Enrique de Orbeta

    The chief of the Insular Police, who deployed armed units to Ponce.

  • Pedro Albizu Campos

    The imprisoned leader of the Nationalist Party.

  • Arthur Garfield Hays

    The American Civil Liberties Union lawyer who led the independent inquiry into the shooting.

03

What happened?

The Nationalists organised a march in Ponce for Palm Sunday, 21 March 1937, to commemorate the abolition of slavery in Puerto Rico in 1873 and to protest against the imprisonment of Albizu Campos.1 They had received a permit from the mayor, but when Governor Winship learned of the march, he ordered the police chief to stop it "by all means necessary". The permit was withdrawn shortly before the march.1

Police units from across the island were concentrated in Ponce. More than 150 police officers — by some reports over 200 — armed with rifles, Thompson submachine guns and tear gas, surrounded the marchers on three sides.1 As the band played La Borinqueña, Puerto Rico's anthem, the Cadets began to march. The police opened fire:1

  • The shooting lasted more than fifteen minutes.
  • Seventeen unarmed civilians and two police officers were killed, and more than 200 civilians, including women and children, were wounded.
  • Some victims were shot as they fled, and others while helping the wounded.

The government claimed that the Nationalists had fired first. An investigation led by the American Civil Liberties Union, headed by Arthur Garfield Hays, concluded that the event was a massacre and placed the blame squarely on Governor Winship. It found no evidence that the marchers had fired.1

04

What happened next?

  • No prosecutions: no police officer or official was ever prosecuted. Instead, Nationalists were put on trial.
  • Winship removed: after continuing criticism, President Franklin D. Roosevelt removed Winship as governor in 1939.
  • More violence: conflict between the Nationalists and the authorities continued, including the Nationalist uprisings of 1950 and the 1954 shooting in the US House of Representatives, in which Nationalists wounded five congressmen.
  • Commonwealth: in 1952 Puerto Rico became a "Commonwealth" with its own constitution, but its political status remains disputed.
05

What do others say?

At the time

Winship and the police insisted that the Nationalists had fired first and that the police acted in self-defence. Photographs and witness statements collected by the Hays Commission contradicted this, and pointed to the police as the only shooters; the two police officers who died were probably killed in the police crossfire.

Historians' debates

  • Responsibility: historians generally agree with the Hays Commission that responsibility lay with Winship and the police commanders.
  • The Nationalists and violence: historians also note that the Nationalist Party did use armed violence at times, both before and after 1937, and that the conflict escalated on both sides — while stressing that the Ponce marchers were unarmed.
  • Colonialism: some historians see the massacre as part of a wider pattern of US repression of the independence movement, including surveillance of thousands of Puerto Ricans.

07

How is it remembered?

  • The Museum of the Ponce Massacre occupies the building where the shooting took place.
  • The massacre is commemorated every Palm Sunday in Ponce.
  • It has inspired paintings, poems, films and plays in Puerto Rico.
08

Why does it matter?

The Ponce massacre was the deadliest event in Puerto Rico's twentieth-century history. It showed how far colonial authorities were prepared to go against peaceful protest, and how difficult it was to hold them accountable.

  • It raised enduring questions about civil rights and democracy in a territory ruled by the United States.
  • It became a powerful symbol for the Puerto Rican independence movement.
  • The independent ACLU inquiry is an important example of civil society challenging an official version of events.

Questions to think about

  1. Why do you think the governor was so determined to stop the march?
  2. Why are independent inquiries, like the Hays Commission, important?
  3. How should historians treat the Nationalists' use of violence at other times, alongside the violence used against them?
  4. What does the massacre tell us about democracy in a colony?

What other events are like this?

  • 1819The Peterloo Massacre — Tens of thousands of working people gathered peacefully to demand the vote. Within ten minutes, cavalry had cleared the field with sabres.
  • 1907The Santa María School Massacre — Thousands of striking nitrate miners and their families were shot down in and around a school in Iquique — one of the worst massacres of workers in Latin American history.
  • 1865The Morant Bay Rebellion — Thirty years after slavery ended, poor Black Jamaicans marched on a courthouse demanding justice and land. The governor's response — hundreds killed, flogged and hanged — scandalised Britain.
