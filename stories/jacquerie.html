The Jacquerie

After the French nobility lost a war and their king, peasants north of Paris rose against the lords who still demanded their labour and money. Their name became the word for a peasant revolt.

When
May – June 1358
Where
Oise valley (Mello and Meaux), France
Facts checked
2026-11-16

See it on the map →

01

What is the background to this event?

In 1356, the English captured the French king, John II, at the Battle of Poitiers, during the Hundred Years' War.1

  • Many commoners blamed the nobles for the defeat.1
  • Unpaid soldiers formed "free companies" that looted, raped and murdered across the countryside.1
  • A new law required peasants to defend the castles of their lords — the symbols of their oppression.1
  • The nobles mocked peasants as "Jacques Bonhomme" — hence "the Jacquerie".1

In their own words

03

What happened?

In late May 1358, peasants in the Oise valley north of Paris rose up.1

  • They attacked more than 150 noble houses and castles, and killed many noble families.1
  • Townspeople in Beauvais, Senlis, Amiens and Meaux supported them; churches were mostly left alone.1
  • Étienne Marcel sent a force from Paris; at Meaux it was routed by heavily armed knights.1
  • On 10 June, near Mello, Guillaume Cale was invited to truce talks by Charles of Navarre, then seized, tortured and beheaded.1
  • His leaderless army was ridden down by cavalry.1

04

What happened next?

  • Terror: knights, squires and mercenaries roamed the countryside lynching peasants.1
  • Meaux: the nobles burned part of the town, hanged its mayor, and plundered it.1
  • A word: "jacquerie" became a general term for a peasant uprising in both French and English.1
05

What do others say?

At the time

Aristocratic chroniclers such as Froissart portrayed the peasants as savages and greatly exaggerated their numbers.1

Debates since

  • Causes: some historians, such as Samuel K. Cohn, see longer-term causes going back to the famine of 1315.1
  • Who led? Village notables often led peasant bands, though many later claimed they had been forced.1
  • Violence on both sides: the rebels killed noble families; the reprisals by nobles killed many thousands of peasants.12
06

The evidence

  • The chronicles of Jean le Bel, Jean Froissart and Jean de Venette.1
  • Royal letters of pardon issued after the revolt.2

07

How is it remembered?

  • The Jacquerie became a symbol of peasant revolt in French politics and literature.
08

Why does it matter?

The Jacquerie was one of the great peasant revolts of medieval Europe, provoked by war, plunder and a nobility that had failed in its own role.

  • It also shows how violence from below was answered by much greater violence from above.

Questions to think about

  1. Why did the defeat at Poitiers damage the nobility's authority?
  2. Why did peasants and townspeople join forces?
  3. How did Froissart's view of the revolt shape its memory?
  4. How should we compare the violence of the rebels with that of the nobles?

What other events are like this?

  • 1381The Peasants' Revolt — Tens of thousands of villagers and townspeople marched on London to demand an end to serfdom. For a few days, the government of England was at their mercy.
  • 1378The Revolt of the Ciompi — The wool workers of Florence, excluded from the guilds that ran the city, seized the government — and for a few weeks a wool-carder sat at the head of the republic.
  • 1514György Dózsa's Peasant War — Peasants called up for a crusade against the Ottomans turned their weapons on the nobles instead. Led by a soldier and radical friars, they demanded the end of the nobility and the sharing of land.
