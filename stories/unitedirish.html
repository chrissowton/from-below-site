The United Irishmen and the Rising of 1798

Protestants, Catholics and Dissenters united to demand an Irish republic. The rising that followed was crushed with terrible violence — and violence was also committed in its name.

When
May – September 1798
Where
Wexford, Antrim, Down and Mayo, Ireland

01

What is the background to this event?

In the late eighteenth century, Ireland had its own parliament in Dublin, but it was controlled by a small Anglican Protestant elite — the "Protestant Ascendancy" — and took its direction from London. The Catholic majority and the Presbyterians (Dissenters) of the north were excluded from power by the Penal Laws.1

  • Inspired by the American and French revolutions, a group of radicals founded the Society of United Irishmen in Belfast and Dublin in October 1791. They aimed to unite "Protestant, Catholic and Dissenter" in a campaign for parliamentary reform and Catholic emancipation.1
  • Many were Presbyterian merchants and artisans in Belfast; in Dublin, the society brought together people of all denominations.1
  • The government suppressed the society in 1794. Despairing of reform, it became a secret revolutionary organisation seeking an independent republic with French help, and allied with the Defenders, a Catholic agrarian movement.1
  • A French fleet carrying Wolfe Tone reached Bantry Bay in December 1796 but was unable to land. In 1797 the army brutally disarmed Ulster, using floggings, "pitchcapping" and house burnings.1

03

What happened?

The rising began in late May 1798, but many of its leaders had already been arrested, and the risings in different counties were uncoordinated.1

  • Wexford: rebels led by Father John Murphy defeated the militia and took Wexford town, where they installed the Protestant Bagenal Harvey as commander. They were defeated at New Ross, Arklow and finally Vinegar Hill on 21 June.1
  • Ulster: in June, Presbyterian United Irishmen rose in Antrim under Henry Joy McCracken and in Down under Henry Munro; both risings were crushed within days.1
  • Mayo: in August, a small French force under General Humbert landed at Killala. Joined by local men, it won a victory at Castlebar, but surrendered at Ballinamuck in September; many Irish rebels were then killed.1
  • In October, a second French expedition was defeated at sea, and Wolfe Tone was captured. He was sentenced to death and died in prison from a self-inflicted wound.1

The rising was marked by atrocities on both sides. Government forces and loyalist yeomanry killed prisoners, burned villages and executed suspected rebels without trial; at the Gibbet Rath on the Curragh, hundreds of rebels were killed after surrendering. In Wexford, rebels massacred loyalist prisoners, most of them Protestant, at Scullabogue, where about a hundred people were burned alive in a barn, and on Wexford Bridge. Estimates of the total number of deaths range from around 10,000 to 30,000 or more, most of them rebels and civilians.2

04

What happened next?

  • The Act of Union: in 1800 the Irish parliament was abolished, and from 1 January 1801 Ireland became part of the United Kingdom, governed from Westminster.
  • Emmet's rising: Robert Emmet led a short-lived rising in Dublin in 1803, and was executed; his speech from the dock became famous.
  • Catholic emancipation: Catholics were finally allowed to sit in Parliament in 1829, after a mass campaign led by Daniel O'Connell.
  • Division: the sectarian violence of 1798, especially in Wexford, was used to deepen divisions between Catholics and Protestants, and the Orange Order grew.
05

What do others say?

At the time

Loyalists presented the rising, especially in Wexford, as a Catholic plot to massacre Protestants. The United Irishmen and their supporters presented it as a republican rising for liberty and equality, provoked by government terror.

Historians' debates

  • Republican or sectarian? Historians have long argued over whether the Wexford rising was a political, republican movement or a sectarian Catholic rising. Recent historians, such as Daniel Gahan and Kevin Whelan, emphasise its political organisation, while recognising the sectarian killings at Scullabogue and Wexford Bridge.
  • Who was to blame for the violence? Historians debate how far the brutality of the government's disarming campaign in 1797–98 provoked the rising and its atrocities.
  • Memory: in 1898 and 1998, the centenary and bicentenary were used by different groups — nationalists, republicans, unionists and peacemakers — to support very different views of Irish history.
06

The evidence

  • Government records, court martial proceedings and the papers of the Irish administration at Dublin Castle.
  • The writings of Wolfe Tone and other United Irishmen, including Tone's journals.
  • Loyalist and rebel memoirs, many written years later with a political purpose.
  • Songs and ballads, such as "The Boys of Wexford" and "Henry Joy".

07

How is it remembered?

  • Monuments to 1798 stand across Ireland, many erected for the centenary in 1898, including on Vinegar Hill.
  • The National 1798 Rebellion Centre in Enniscorthy tells the story of the rising.
  • The bicentenary in 1998, the year of the Good Friday Agreement, emphasised the ideal of uniting people of different traditions.
08

Why does it matter?

The United Irishmen tried to create an Irish republic in which religion would no longer divide people. Their rising was the most violent event in Irish history before the twentieth century, and its memory has shaped Irish politics ever since.

  • The ideal of uniting "Protestant, Catholic and Dissenter" is still invoked in Ireland, north and south.
  • The rising showed the influence of the American and French revolutions on ordinary people far from Paris.
  • Its suppression led to the Act of Union, which shaped Irish history until independence in 1922.
