The Taiping Heavenly Kingdom

A failed examination candidate who believed he was the younger brother of Jesus led millions of poor Chinese against the Qing dynasty. His Heavenly Kingdom promised equality — and the war that followed was the deadliest civil war in history.

When
1850 – 1864
Where
Nanjing, Jiangsu, China
Facts checked
2026-11-16

See it on the map →

01

What is the background to this event?

By the 1840s, China faced economic crisis and foreign defeat.1

  • Farmers were heavily overtaxed, rents rose sharply, and many peasants abandoned their land.1
  • China's population had nearly doubled between 1766 and 1833, but farmland had not grown.1
  • The Qing had lost the First Opium War, and the opium trade drained the economy.1
  • Hong Xiuquan, a Hakka who had repeatedly failed the civil service exams, had visions after reading Christian tracts and came to believe he was the younger brother of Jesus.1

In their own words

03

What happened?

In late 1850, a Taiping army of 10,000 defeated Qing forces at Jintian in Guangxi, and in January 1851 Hong declared the Taiping Heavenly Kingdom.1

  • In 1853, the Taiping took Nanjing and made it their capital.1
  • They announced sweeping reforms: abolition of foot binding, shared ownership of land, strict separation of the sexes and a ban on opium.1
  • At its height, the Heavenly Kingdom ruled nearly 30 million people.1
  • A failed march on Beijing and a violent power struggle among the leaders in 1856 weakened the movement.1
  • Qing provincial armies besieged Nanjing from 1862. Hong died in June 1864, and Nanjing fell a month later.1
04

What happened next?

  • Death toll: estimates range from 20–30 million to as many as 73–100 million; some 30 million people fled their homes.1
  • The Qing: the dynasty survived, but was weakened and relied on provincial armies.1
  • Influence: the Taiping mix of radical equality and Christianity influenced Sun Yat-sen and later revolutionaries.1
05

Why does it matter?

The Taiping rebellion was one of the largest peasant movements in history, and promised a social revolution.

  • It is also a warning about how utopian movements can become violent and authoritarian.
  • Estimates of the death toll range from 20–30 million to as many as 73–100 million.1
  • Its mix of radical equality and Christianity influenced Sun Yat-sen and later revolutionaries.1

What do others say?How it was seen at the time, and where historians disagree

At the time

The Qing saw heretical rebels; some Western observers at first hoped for a Christian China, then turned against the Taiping.4

Debates since

  • Revolution? Marx saw only destruction; Mao and Chinese Communist historians called it a proto-communist uprising.1
  • Violence on both sides: the war was marked by extreme brutality. The Taiping massacred Manchus, including women burned to death in Nanjing; Qing forces massacred civilians, above all when Nanjing fell.1
How do we know?The evidence, and a source from the time
  • Taiping proclamations and religious texts.
  • Qing records and accounts by Western missionaries and diplomats.4

Qing forces ambush the Taiping army in 1854. The painting was made to celebrate the Qing victory.

Reading it

This painting was made to celebrate a Qing victory, and the composition gives it away: the imperial forces are orderly and triumphant. A victory painting leaves out the cost. For the Taiping's own view, historians turn to their published proclamations and laws.

How is it remembered?Memorials, anniversaries and arguments over memory
  • The People's Republic honoured the Taiping as revolutionaries; a museum stands in Nanjing.
