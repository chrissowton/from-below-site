Curalaba and the Mapuche Uprising

A Mapuche ambush killed the Spanish governor of Chile and set off a general uprising that destroyed seven Spanish cities. The Mapuche kept their independence south of the Biobío River for nearly three centuries.

When
1598 – 1604
Where
Curalaba, Araucanía, Chile
Facts checked
2026-11-16

See it on the map →

01

What is the background to this event?

The Spanish began the Arauco War against the Mapuche in 1536.1

  • Diseases brought by the Spanish may have reduced the Mapuche population by up to 80%.1
  • By the 1590s, the Spanish believed they were close to victory.1
  • The Mapuche had turned to guerrilla tactics, using small mounted groups to ambush the Spanish.1
02

Who were the key people?

  • Pelantaro (Pelantaru)

    Mapuche leader at Curalaba.

  • Anganamón and Guaiquimilla

    His lieutenants.

  • Martín García Óñez de Loyola

    Spanish governor of Chile, killed at Curalaba.

03

What happened?

On 23 December 1598, Pelantaro and about 300 warriors ambushed and killed Governor Óñez de Loyola and nearly all his companions at Curalaba.2

  • It was the second time a Spanish governor of Chile had been killed by the Mapuche.1
  • The news set off a general uprising of Mapuche and Huilliche peoples.2
  • Over the next few years, all seven Spanish cities in Mapuche territory south of the Biobío River were destroyed or abandoned.2
04

What happened next?

  • A frontier: a border between Spanish and Mapuche lands was set at the Biobío River.1
  • Independence: the Mapuche kept their independence until the Chilean state occupied Araucanía in 1883.1
05

Why does it matter?

Curalaba was one of the greatest Indigenous victories over the Spanish Empire.

  • It explains why the Mapuche, unlike most Indigenous peoples of the Americas, stayed independent for centuries.
  • A frontier between Spanish and Mapuche lands was set at the Biobío River.1
  • The Mapuche kept their independence until the Chilean state occupied Araucanía in 1883.1
  • Mapuche organisations remember Curalaba as a symbol of resistance, and a statue of Pelantaro stands in Purén.

What do others say?How it was seen at the time, and where historians disagree

At the time

Spanish chroniclers called it the "Disaster of Curalaba".1

Debates since

  • End of conquest: Chilean historians treat Curalaba as the end of the conquest period.1
  • Violence on both sides: a Spanish chronicler wrote that the Mapuche killed more than 3,000 Spaniards and captured over 500 women; the Spanish conquest had brought massacre, enslavement and disease.2
How do we know?The evidence, and a source from the time
  • Spanish chronicles, including that of Alonso González de Nájera.2
  • Drawings by the friar Diego de Ocaña.

What is it?
Drawing
Made by, and when
Diego de Ocaña, a Spanish friar, about 1605

A drawing by a Spanish friar, made a few years after the battle. It shows the Mapuche leader Anganamón facing Governor Martín García Óñez de Loyola, who was killed at Curalaba in 1598.

Reading it

A Spanish friar drew this a few years after the battle, and he was not there: his information came from Spanish survivors and settlers. Even so, he gives the Mapuche leader Anganamón equal standing with the governor. Images made by the Mapuche themselves from this time do not survive, so we see them only through their enemies' eyes.
