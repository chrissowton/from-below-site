The Homosexual Liberation Front of Argentina

In 1971, a small group of gay writers and students in Buenos Aires founded one of Latin America's first gay liberation movements. They published an underground magazine and marched with the left — until the coming dictatorship forced them to disband.

01

What is the background to this event?

In Argentina, homosexual people faced police raids and arrests. Under Juan Perón's governments, repression of homosexuals had been greater than under any other government in the country's history.1

  • A small group called Nuestro Mundo had already begun to meet and publish.1

In their own words

03

What happened?

In August 1971, members of Nuestro Mundo met with university students and others in Buenos Aires and formed the Frente de Liberación Homosexual (FLH).1

  • The FLH had no leaders: it was made up of semi-autonomous groups that coordinated with each other.1
  • Many members were on the far left. They saw all forms of oppression as connected and campaigned for women's rights and labour rights as well as gay rights.1
  • In August 1972, the magazine Panorama published the first interview with the FLH; two members posed for the photographs with their faces hidden.1
  • In May 1973, FLH members carried a banner at the inauguration of President Héctor Cámpora calling for "love and equality" and freedom for political prisoners. Other left-wing groups kept their distance.1
  • In September 1973, the FLH printed 5,000 copies of a newspaper, Homosexuales. From December 1973 to January 1976, it published six issues of an underground magazine, Somos.1
04

What happened next?

  • Repression: after Perón's death in 1974, attacks on gay people by right-wing paramilitary groups increased, and membership fell from about a hundred to about a dozen.1
  • Before the World Cup: in March 1976, police were ordered to "scare the homosexuals off the streets" ahead of the 1978 World Cup.1
  • Disbanded: after the military coup of March 1976, the FLH dissolved in June. Some members fled abroad; others were tortured, disappeared or murdered during the 'Dirty War'.1
05

Why does it matter?

The FLH was among the first gay liberation movements in Latin America. It linked sexual freedom to the struggles of women and workers, and paid a high price under the dictatorship.1

  • Attacks by right-wing paramilitary groups after 1974 cut its membership from about a hundred to about a dozen.1
  • Before the 1978 World Cup, police were ordered to 'scare the homosexuals off the streets'.1
  • Its banners, leaflets and magazine survive as records of the movement.1

What do others say?How it was seen at the time, and where historians disagree

At the time

The FLH was divided over whether to support Peronism, given Perón's past repression of homosexuals; some members marched with Peronists in 1973, but by the end of 1974 the group had broken with it.1

Debates since

  • Marginalised by the left: FLH members complained that other left-wing organisations shunned them, and some used homophobic chants.1

Buenos Aires, December 1973

The first issue of Somos ('We Are'), the magazine the FLH published secretly from 1973 to 1976. Six issues appeared before the dictatorship forced the group to disband.

Reading it

Somos — 'We Are' — was published in secret from 1973 to 1976; six issues appeared before the dictatorship forced the group to disband. The title itself was a statement of existence. For a small, persecuted movement, a magazine was a lifeline: proof to scattered readers that they were not alone.
