The Bashmurian Revolts

In the reed marshes of the Nile Delta, Coptic Egyptians rose again and again against heavy taxes and the rule of the caliphs. They held out for decades, until a caliph came in person to crush them.

When
749 – 832
Where
Bashmur, northern Nile Delta, Egypt

See it on the map →

01

What is the background to this event?

Egypt had been conquered by the Arabs in the 640s. At first Arab rule over the Copts (Egyptian Christians) was fairly peaceful, but it became harsher over time.1

  • Coptic and Arabic sources both blame the revolts on heavy taxation and the unjust treatment of Christians by some governors.1
  • Bashmur was a region of marsh, sandbanks and reeds in the northern Delta. Arabs did not settle there.1
  • Its people lived by fishing, hunting, limited farming and selling papyrus, so they could survive long sieges.1
02

Who were the key people?

  • Abū Mina (Mina ibn Bāqīra)

    Leader of the revolt of 749, killed in the fighting.

  • Caliph Marwān II

    Umayyad caliph who came with an army in 749–750.

  • Caliph al-Maʾmūn

    Abbasid caliph who crushed the last revolt in 832.

  • Al-Afshīn

    Sogdian general sent against the rebels.

03

What happened?

The Bashmurians were the only Egyptian rebels who resisted for long periods and needed the caliphs themselves to intervene.1

  • 749: under Abū Mina, the Bashmurians defeated several campaigns by the governor. When he took the Coptic patriarch hostage, they sacked Rosetta.1
  • 767: they joined Arab settlers in a general revolt in the Delta and defeated a government force.1
  • 831–832: al-Afshīn crushed rebels elsewhere but could not defeat the Bashmurians, who made their own weapons.1
  • They refused an offer of amnesty in return for leaving their land. Caliph al-Maʾmūn attacked in person, with heavy losses on both sides, and the rebels finally accepted an armistice.1

04

What happened next?

  • Destruction: after the armistice, many armed men were executed, and women and children were deported to Iraq or sold as slaves in Damascus.1
  • Bashmur: the region was burned to prevent further revolts.1
  • Conversion: some historians argue the defeat demoralised the Copts, who came under heavy pressure to convert to Islam.1
05

What do others say?

At the time

The revolts are known from both Coptic sources, such as the History of the Patriarchs of Alexandria, and Arabic sources.1

Debates since

  • National liberation? Some describe the revolts as an attempt to free Egypt from foreign rule; others stress local grievances over tax.12
  • Violence on both sides: in 749 the rebels massacred the Arab inhabitants of Rosetta, and Marwān II razed Coptic villages and monasteries across the Delta; the suppression of 832 ended in executions and enslavement.1
06

The evidence

  • The History of the Patriarchs of Alexandria.
  • The Arabic histories of al-Kindī and al-Maqrīzī.1
07

How is it remembered?

  • The Bashmurians are remembered in Coptic history as defenders of their people and faith.
08

Why does it matter?

The Bashmurian revolts are among the longest resistance movements in Egyptian history, and are central to Coptic memory.

  • They show how geography can help ordinary people resist powerful states.

Questions to think about

  1. Why did the marshes make Bashmur so hard to conquer?
  2. Why did the Bashmurians refuse amnesty in return for leaving their land?
  3. Was this a religious revolt, a tax revolt, or both?
  4. Why were these revolts unknown in Europe until the nineteenth century?
09

What other events are like this?

  • 869The Zanj Rebellion — Enslaved East Africans forced to dig salt-crusted marshland in southern Iraq rose against the Abbasid Caliphate, built their own state and held out for fourteen years.
  • 816Babak Khorramdin and the Khurramites — From a mountain fortress in Azerbaijan, a former cowherd led a religious and social movement against the Abbasid caliphs for more than twenty years.
  • 1919Egypt's 1919 Revolution — When Britain exiled the nationalist leader Saad Zaghlul, Egyptians of every class and religion rose up in strikes and demonstrations — and women marched in public for the first time.
