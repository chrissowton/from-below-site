The Red Eyebrows

When the Yellow River flooded and Wang Mang's land reforms failed, starving farmers painted their eyebrows red to tell friend from foe. Their rebellion helped bring down an emperor — and was joined by a mother avenging her son.

When
17 CE – 27
Where
Shandong and northern Jiangsu, China
Facts checked
2026-11-16

See it on the map →

01

What is the background to this event?

In 9 CE, the official Wang Mang seized the throne of the Han dynasty and founded his own Xin dynasty.1

  • His attempts at land reform and currency changes failed and caused chaos.1
  • Around 11 CE, the Yellow River changed its course in a catastrophic flood, devastating Shandong and northern Jiangsu.1
  • People who could no longer survive by farming were driven to rebel.1
02

Who were the key people?

  • Fan Chong

    A rebel leader who began his rising in 18 CE and became a leader of the Red Eyebrows.

  • Mother Lü

    A landowner whose son, a minor official, was executed; she sold her property, recruited young men and stormed the county seat to avenge him.

  • Wang Mang

    Emperor of the Xin dynasty, 9 CE–23.

  • Liu Xiu (Emperor Guangwu)

    Who restored the Han dynasty and accepted the Red Eyebrows' surrender.

03

What happened?

In 18 CE, Fan Chong began a rising near Mount Tai, gathering about 10,000 men, and allied with other rebel leaders.1

  • Mother Lü, whose son had been executed for a minor offence, raised thousands of poor young men and killed the county magistrate.1
  • The rebels painted their eyebrows red to distinguish themselves from government troops.1
  • Their rebellion, along with others, drained Wang Mang's resources. In 23 CE, he was overthrown and killed.1
  • The Red Eyebrows later overthrew a new emperor and placed their own teenage Han prince, Liu Penzi, on the throne.1
  • Their rule failed, and in 27 CE they surrendered to Liu Xiu, who had founded the Eastern Han dynasty.1
04

What happened next?

  • The Eastern Han: Liu Xiu ruled as Emperor Guangwu, and the Han dynasty lasted until 220 CE.1
  • Later revolts: peasant risings, including the Yellow Turbans of 184 CE, continued to shape Chinese history.
05

Why does it matter?

The Red Eyebrows show how environmental disaster and failed reforms could drive ordinary people to rebellion.

  • Mother Lü is one of the earliest recorded women leaders of a rebellion in China.
  • The rising helped bring Liu Xiu to power as Emperor Guangwu, and the restored Han dynasty lasted until 220 CE.1
  • Peasant risings of this kind, including the Yellow Turbans of 184 CE, went on shaping Chinese history.

What do others say?How it was seen at the time, and where historians disagree

At the time

Han historians, writing for the restored dynasty, presented Wang Mang as a usurper and the rebels as both a punishment for him and a source of disorder.2

Debates since

  • Wang Mang: some historians see him as a reformer whose land policies were well meant; others as a usurper.2
  • Violence: the rebels killed officials and fought large battles; their armies also looted the regions they controlled.1
How do we know?The evidence, and a source from the time
  • The Book of Han and the Book of the Later Han.
How is it remembered?Memorials, anniversaries and arguments over memory
  • The Red Eyebrows are remembered in China as one of the great peasant rebellions.
