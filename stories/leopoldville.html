The Léopoldville Riots

When a nationalist meeting was banned, Kinshasa's unemployed young people rose in three days of rioting. Within eighteen months, the Congo was independent.

When
4 – 7 January 1959
Where
Léopoldville (Kinshasa), Belgian Congo
Facts checked
2026-11-16

See it on the map →

01

What is the background to this event?

From 1885 to 1908 the Congo was the personal property of King Leopold II of Belgium, whose Congo Free State extracted rubber through forced labour and terrible violence. It then became a Belgian colony.1

  • Belgian rule was paternalistic: Congolese were given primary education and health care, but almost no access to higher education or political rights. At independence there were only a handful of Congolese university graduates.
  • After the Second World War, a small African middle class of clerks, nurses and teachers, known as évolués, emerged in the cities.1
  • In 1958, nationalism grew rapidly. New political parties included the Alliance des Bakongo (ABAKO), led by Joseph Kasa-Vubu, and the Mouvement National Congolais (MNC), led by Patrice Lumumba.1
  • In Léopoldville, the colonial capital, the economic boom had ended; tens of thousands of young people were unemployed.

In their own words

03

What happened?

On 28 December 1958, Patrice Lumumba held a large and successful MNC rally in Léopoldville. Kasa-Vubu decided to hold his own ABAKO meeting a week later, on Sunday 4 January 1959. The authorities told ABAKO that the meeting could not be held as planned, and warned that its leaders would be held responsible if it became political.1

When a crowd gathered anyway and was told to go home, it refused. Kasa-Vubu and other ABAKO leaders arrived to try to send the protesters home, but the situation spiralled out of control.1 For several days:12

  • Crowds attacked and looted European-owned shops, Catholic missions, schools and public buildings, and attacked Portuguese traders and other Europeans.
  • The Force Publique was deployed and fired on the crowds.
  • ABAKO's leaders, including Kasa-Vubu, were arrested.

The exact death toll is not known. Official figures put it at 49 Congolese; other estimates put the number of casualties as high as 500.1

04

What happened next?

  • Independence announced: on 13 January 1959, King Baudouin announced that Belgium intended to lead the Congo to independence — without undue delay, but also without rashness.
  • The Round Table: at a Round Table Conference in Brussels in early 1960, Congolese leaders won agreement on a rapid timetable. The Congo became independent on 30 June 1960.
  • Crisis: within days, the army mutinied, Belgian troops intervened and the provinces of Katanga and South Kasai seceded with Belgian and mining-company support. Lumumba was overthrown and, in January 1961, murdered with Belgian complicity.
  • Dictatorship: in 1965 Joseph-Désiré Mobutu seized power and ruled for over thirty years.
05

What do others say?

At the time

Belgian officials blamed ABAKO and "agitators" for the riots, and some settlers called for even harsher repression. Congolese nationalists saw the riots as the inevitable result of colonial injustice and unemployment.

Historians' debates

  • Who rioted, and why? Historians emphasise that the riots were driven as much by unemployment, poverty and resentment of colonial racism as by party politics; ABAKO's leaders lost control of the crowd.
  • The violence: the attacks on Europeans and on missions and schools frightened Belgian opinion; the Force Publique's response killed far more people. Historians try to account for both.
  • Too fast? Some historians argue that Belgium's sudden decision to leave, after decades of refusing to train Congolese leaders, set the stage for disaster; others place the blame on Belgian and Western interference after independence.

07

How is it remembered?

  • 4 January is a public holiday in the DR Congo: the Day of the Martyrs of Independence.
  • The riots and the independence struggle are a recurring subject of Congolese popular painting and music.
08

Why does it matter?

The Léopoldville riots were the moment when Belgium's plan for a slow, controlled decolonisation collapsed. The anger of ordinary Congolese, especially the young urban poor, forced the pace of independence.

  • They showed that colonial rule could no longer be maintained without force.
  • They are commemorated in the Democratic Republic of the Congo as the "Day of the Martyrs of Independence".
  • The speed of the transition that followed — and the lack of preparation for it — helped to produce the Congo Crisis.

Questions to think about
