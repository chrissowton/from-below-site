The War of the Unstamped

A tax made newspapers too expensive for working people. Radical printers sold cheap papers illegally — and hundreds of sellers went to prison for it.

When
1830 – 1836
Where
London and across Britain
Facts checked
2026-11-16

01

What is the background to this event?

In the early nineteenth century, newspapers in Britain carried a government stamp duty. By 1815 it had reached 4d per copy, making a newspaper far too expensive for most working people.1

  • After the Peterloo Massacre of 1819, the government passed the Six Acts. One extended the stamp duty to cheap political periodicals, to drive the radical press out of business.12
  • Radicals called these taxes on newspapers, advertisements and paper "taxes on knowledge".
  • Working people were demanding the vote, and their leaders believed that a free and cheap press was essential to political education.
02

Who were the key people?

  • Henry Hetherington

    A printer and publisher of The Poor Man's Guardian, imprisoned three times for refusing to pay stamp duty.

  • James Watson, John Cleave and William Lovett

    Radical printers and organisers who worked with Hetherington and later helped to found the Chartist movement.

  • Bronterre O'Brien

    Editor of The Poor Man's Guardian from 1832, later a leading Chartist writer.

  • The street sellers

    Hundreds of men, women and boys who sold the illegal papers and were fined or jailed.

In their own words

03

What happened?

From 1830, radical publishers began deliberately publishing newspapers without the stamp, selling them for a penny.1

  • Henry Hetherington's Poor Man's Guardian, launched in July 1831, carried on its front page the declaration that it was published in defiance of the law, "to try the power of 'might' against 'right'".2
  • Its motto was "Knowledge is power". It sold around 15,000 copies a week across the country.2
  • Hetherington recruited hundreds of sellers, and supporters raised money to support those sent to prison and their families.1
  • The National Union of the Working Classes took up the campaign against the "taxes on knowledge".1

The government prosecuted printers, publishers and sellers. In all, nearly 800 people were imprisoned during the "war of the unstamped".1 Hetherington himself was jailed three times. But the unstamped papers kept appearing, and they reached a huge working-class readership.

04

What happened next?

  • A partial victory: in 1836 the government cut the stamp duty from 4d to 1d, but increased penalties for evading it, which made unstamped publishing much harder.1
  • Chartism: many of the unstamped campaigners, such as Hetherington, Lovett and Watson, helped to found the London Working Men's Association in 1836 and draw up the People's Charter.
  • Final repeal: the campaign continued. Advertisement duty was abolished in 1853, newspaper stamp duty in 1855 and paper duty in 1861, opening the way for a cheap popular press.
05

What do others say?

At the time

The government argued that the stamp duty was a legitimate tax and that cheap papers spread sedition and irreligion. Radicals argued that it was a deliberate tax on the knowledge of the poor.

Historians' debates

  • How radical was the press? Patricia Hollis showed how the "pauper press" developed working-class ideas about labour and exploitation, not just about political reform.
  • Who won? Some historians see the 1836 reduction as a defeat for the radicals, because it helped respectable stamped papers to drive the unstamped out of business. Others see it as a step towards a free press.
  • Commercial cheap papers: after repeal, a mass commercial press grew. Historians debate whether it served working people better than the radical press had.

The evidence

  • Court and prison records of prosecutions.
  • The memoirs of campaigners, such as William Lovett's autobiography (1876).

07

How is it remembered?

  • Hetherington's name is inscribed on the Reformers' Memorial in Kensal Green Cemetery, London.
  • Press freedom campaigners still cite the "taxes on knowledge".
08

Why does it matter?

The War of the Unstamped was one of the first great campaigns for freedom of the press led by working people. It linked the right to information with the right to vote.

  • It shows how ordinary people — street sellers as well as editors — risked prison for free expression.
  • It helped create a working-class reading public and the movement that became Chartism.
  • Debates about who can afford access to news and information continue today.

Questions to think about

  2. What did the radicals mean by 'Knowledge is power'?
