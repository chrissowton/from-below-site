Civil rights & anti-racism

The Australian Freedom Ride

Aboriginal activist Charles Perkins and a busload of students toured country towns in New South Wales, exposing segregation in pools, pubs and cinemas — and taking Aboriginal children into a whites-only swimming pool.

When
12 – 27 February 1965
Where
Moree, Walgett and country New South Wales, Australia
Facts checked

01

What is the background to this event?

In the 1960s, Aboriginal Australians faced widespread discrimination. In many country towns, they were barred from swimming pools, pubs, clubs and shops, or segregated in cinemas.1

  • Some Aboriginal people were counted separately in the census, and their rights as citizens were regularly ignored.1
  • In 1964, students at the University of Sydney formed Student Action for Aborigines (SAFA), led by Charles Perkins, an Arrernte man from Alice Springs.1
  • They were inspired by the Freedom Riders of the US civil rights movement and by the anti-apartheid movement.1

In their own words

03

What happened?

On 12 February 1965, 29 people left Sydney in a hired bus.1 Over 15 days, they travelled to country towns in New South Wales.

  • They surveyed Aboriginal living conditions and protested for hours at segregated places — pools, parks and pubs.1
  • In Walgett, they picketed the Returned Services League club, which barred Aboriginal people, even Aboriginal ex-servicemen. As they left, a car repeatedly rammed the bus and forced it off the road.
  • In Moree, the students took Aboriginal children to the town's swimming baths, which barred Aboriginal people. A hostile crowd gathered, and the students were pelted with eggs and tomatoes. The ride met violence in both towns.1
  • The council briefly lifted the ban, then restored it, and the students returned to confront the town again.

The ride received major coverage in national and international media, and succeeded in exposing the extent of racial discrimination in Australia.1

04

What happened next?

  • Desegregation: the publicity helped end formal segregation in many country towns.
  • The 1967 referendum: two years later, Australians voted by more than 90 per cent to count Aboriginal people in the census and allow the federal government to make laws for them.
  • Perkins: Charles Perkins became a senior public servant and a leading Aboriginal rights campaigner. He died in 2000.
05

What do others say?

At the time

Many townspeople resented the students as outsiders stirring up trouble. Supporters saw them as exposing a national shame.

Debates since

  • Students and communities: some Aboriginal people felt the students left them to face the anger of the towns after they left; others welcomed the attention.
  • Violence: the riders were committed to non-violence, and faced violence from some townspeople.1
  • Lasting change? Historians note that discrimination continued long after the ride, even where formal segregation ended.

How is it remembered?

  • Anniversaries of the Freedom Ride have been marked by re-enactments, including in 2015, with surviving riders and Aboriginal communities.
08

Why does it matter?

The Freedom Ride brought the reality of racism in Australia to national attention. It showed how a small group using non-violent direct action could change public opinion.

  • It linked the Australian struggle to the global civil rights movement.
  • It showed the importance of Aboriginal leadership.
