The Israeli Black Panthers

Young Jews from Moroccan families in a poor Jerusalem neighbourhood took the name of the American Black Panthers to protest discrimination against Mizrahi Jews.

When
1971 – 1973
Where
Musrara, Jerusalem

01

What is the background to this event?

In the 1950s, hundreds of thousands of Jews from North Africa and the Middle East — Mizrahi Jews — moved to Israel. Many were housed in transit camps and poor neighbourhoods, and faced discrimination from the dominant Ashkenazi (European Jewish) establishment.

  • Musrara, in Jerusalem, had been a Palestinian Arab neighbourhood before 1948; its residents fled or were expelled during the war, and their homes were given to Jewish immigrants from North Africa and the Middle East.1
  • Located on the border with Jordanian-ruled East Jerusalem, Musrara became a neglected area.1
  • After 1967, the government planned to redevelop it with housing for new immigrants from the Soviet Union, which would displace existing Moroccan Jewish residents.1
  • Earlier, in 1959, Mizrahi anger had exploded in the Wadi Salib riots in Haifa.1

In their own words

03

What happened?

In early 1971, about ten young men from Moroccan families in Musrara founded the Black Panthers, inspired by the African American movement.1 Most had left school early, and some had spent time in juvenile institutions.1

  • The police refused them permission to demonstrate and arrested several members before their first protest. They demonstrated anyway outside Jerusalem City Hall on 2 March 1971.1
  • In April 1971, Prime Minister Golda Meir met them, and later described them as "not nice".1
  • On 18 May 1971, 5,000 to 7,000 people joined the "Night of the Panthers" protest. Seven hours of clashes with police followed. More than 100 people were arrested, there were reports of police brutality, and some demonstrators threw Molotov cocktails.1

The protests forced the government to set up a public committee, which acknowledged ethnic and social inequalities in Israeli society, and spending on social services was increased.1

04

What happened next?

  • Elections: the Panthers narrowly failed to enter the Knesset in 1973. Charlie Biton was elected in 1977 and served for many years.1
  • The 1973 war: the Yom Kippur War shifted government priorities to defence, limiting reforms.1
  • Political change: Mizrahi anger contributed to the Likud's victory in 1977, ending decades of Labour rule. Later parties such as Shas also claimed to represent Mizrahi voters.1
  • Solidarity: some Panthers built links with Palestinian activists, arguing that both suffered from the same system.
05

What do others say?

At the time

The government and much of the press portrayed the Panthers as criminals and troublemakers. The Panthers argued that they were exposing real discrimination.

Historians' debates

  • Class or ethnicity? Historians debate whether the Panthers were primarily a class movement or an ethnic one.
  • Tactics: the use of Molotov cocktails on the Night of the Panthers alarmed authorities; supporters point to police brutality and the refusal of permits.1
  • Legacy: some argue the Panthers' radical message was absorbed by right-wing and religious parties, which offered recognition but not structural change.

How is it remembered?

  • A tile in Musrara commemorates the movement.
  • The Panthers are remembered as pioneers by Mizrahi activists today.
08

Why does it matter?

The Israeli Black Panthers put the "Mizrahi question" — discrimination among Jews in Israel — on the political agenda.1 Young people from a poor neighbourhood forced the government to listen.

  • It shows how movements borrow names and ideas from struggles elsewhere.
  • It raises questions about race and class within a nation.

Questions to think about

  1. Why do you think the young men chose the name 'Black Panthers'?
  2. Why was the government so hostile at first?
  3. How did the protests change Israeli politics?
  4. Can people face discrimination within their own nation or religious group?
