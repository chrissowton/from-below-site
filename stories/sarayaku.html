Sarayaku and the Living Forest

A small Kichwa community in the Amazon stopped an oil company exploring its land — and won a landmark case at the Inter-American Court of Human Rights on the right of indigenous peoples to be consulted.

When
2002 – 2012
Where
Sarayaku, Pastaza province, Ecuadorian Amazon
Facts checked
2026-11-16

See it on the map →

01

What is the background to this event?

Sarayaku is a Kichwa territory of about 135,000 hectares on the Bobonaza River, reached only by river or small plane. Between 1,000 and 2,000 people live there.12

  • Ecuador's economy depended heavily on oil exports.1
  • In 1996, the government granted an oil concession covering much of Sarayaku's land to the Argentine company CGC, without consulting the community.3

03

What happened?

In 2002–03, CGC began seismic exploration on Sarayaku land, with the support of the military.1

  • The community resisted. Families set up "peace and life camps" in the forest to block the work.3
  • The government sent soldiers and restricted traffic on the Bobonaza River.1
  • The company left explosives buried in the forest — more than 1,400 kg of pentolite, according to the court.3
  • Community members were threatened and some were attacked.3
  • Sarayaku took its case to the Inter-American system. In 2011, members of the community travelled to Costa Rica to testify; some had never been to Ecuador's capital.1
  • On 27 June 2012, the Inter-American Court of Human Rights ruled that Ecuador had violated Sarayaku's rights by failing to consult the community.3

04

What happened next?

  • Reparations: the court ordered Ecuador to remove the explosives, pay compensation and apologise publicly.3
  • Apology: the government made a public apology in Sarayaku in 2014.
  • Kawsak Sacha: Sarayaku has proposed that its territory be recognised as a "Living Forest", a protected and sacred space.2
05

What do others say?

At the time

The government argued that oil revenue was essential for the national economy and to pay its debts; Sarayaku argued that oil would destroy its forest and way of life.1

Debates since

  • Consultation or consent? The ruling requires consultation; activists argue communities should also be able to say no.
  • Violence: the community's resistance was largely non-violent, though there were confrontations with oil workers and soldiers.3

The evidence

07

How is it remembered?

  • Sarayaku leaders have spoken at UN climate conferences, presenting the Living Forest proposal.
08

Why does it matter?

The Sarayaku ruling became a landmark in international law on the right of indigenous peoples to free, prior and informed consultation.

  • It shows how a small community could challenge a state and an oil company.
  • It linked indigenous rights to the protection of the Amazon.
