Standing Rock and the Dakota Access Pipeline

Indigenous 'water protectors' and their allies camped beside the Missouri River to oppose an oil pipeline, in the largest gathering of Native Americans in more than a century.

When
April 2016 – February 2017
Where
Standing Rock Sioux Reservation, North Dakota, USA
Facts checked
2026-11-16

01

What is the background to this event?

The Dakota Access Pipeline was designed to carry oil some 1,170 miles (1,880 km) from the Bakken oil fields of North Dakota to Illinois. Its route crossed beneath the Missouri River at Lake Oahe, just upstream of the Standing Rock Sioux Reservation, which relies on the river for its water.

The Standing Rock Sioux Tribe raised several objections:

  • A leak or spill under the river could contaminate the tribe's water supply.
  • The route passed through land the tribe considered sacred, including burial grounds, on territory guaranteed to the Sioux under the Fort Laramie treaties of 1851 and 1868.
  • The tribe argued that it had not been properly consulted, and that an earlier route north of the state capital, Bismarck, had been rejected partly because of risks to that city's water.

These concerns belonged to a long history of broken treaties and of the loss of Sioux land — including land flooded by the dams that created Lake Oahe in the 1950s and 1960s.

03

What happened?

In April 2016 young people from Standing Rock and other communities began a campaign against the pipeline. On 1 April, LaDonna Brave Bull Allard established the Sacred Stone Camp as a centre for cultural preservation and spiritual resistance.1 Over the summer, delegations arrived from tribes across North America and beyond, and further camps, including the large Oceti Sakowin camp, grew up beside the Cannonball River. By September it was described as the largest gathering of Native Americans in more than a hundred years.1

Conflict escalated through the autumn:1

  • In early September, after construction crews bulldozed land the tribe had identified as sacred, private security workers used attack dogs on protesters, biting at least six people.
  • In October, militarised police cleared a camp on the pipeline's route; hundreds of people were arrested during the protests, and some described being held in what appeared to be dog kennels.
  • On the night of 20 November, police used water cannon in sub-freezing temperatures, along with tear gas and rubber bullets, against protesters at Backwater Bridge, injuring many.

At the United Nations Human Rights Council in September 2016, Chairman Dave Archambault II said that "the oil companies and the government of the United States have failed to respect our sovereign rights."1 On 4 December 2016, the US Army Corps of Engineers announced that it would not grant the easement needed to drill under Lake Oahe, and called for a full environmental review.1

On 24 January 2017, however, the new President, Donald Trump, signed a memorandum directing that construction proceed. The easement was granted, and on 22–23 February 2017 the National Guard and police cleared the remaining camps.1 Oil began flowing through the pipeline in mid-2017.1

04

What happened next?

  • In the courts: in 2020 a federal judge found that the Army Corps had failed to carry out a proper environmental review and cancelled the easement. A court order to shut the pipeline down was overturned on appeal, and the pipeline has continued to operate while a new review is carried out.
  • Prosecutions and lawsuits: many protesters faced criminal charges. In 2025 a North Dakota jury found against Greenpeace in a lawsuit brought by Energy Transfer, and the court ordered Greenpeace to pay hundreds of millions of dollars in damages — a case widely criticised as an attempt to deter protest.
  • A wider movement: Standing Rock inspired Indigenous-led campaigns against other pipelines, such as Keystone XL (cancelled in 2021) and Line 3, and new laws in several states increased penalties for protests near "critical infrastructure".
05

What do others say?

At the time

The pipeline company, the state of North Dakota and many local residents argued that the pipeline was legal, safer than transporting oil by rail or road, and important for jobs and energy security. Law enforcement described some protests as riots and pointed to fires, blocked roads and property damage. Supporters of the water protectors stressed that the protests were overwhelmingly peaceful and that the police response was excessive.

Historians' and commentators' debates

  • Consultation: a judge found in 2016 that the Army Corps had made dozens of attempts to consult the tribe; the tribe argued that these efforts were inadequate and came too late.
  • Success or failure? The pipeline was built, but many argue the movement succeeded in transforming public awareness and inspiring later victories.
  • Policing and surveillance: investigations revealed that a private security firm used counter-terrorism tactics against protesters, raising questions about the policing of protest.

07

How is it remembered?

  • The phrase "water protectors" and the slogan "Water is Life" have been taken up by movements around the world.
  • The story has been told in documentaries such as Awake: A Dream from Standing Rock (2017) and End of the Line: The Women of Standing Rock (2021).
  • Flags and objects from the camps have been preserved by museums and tribal archives.
08

Why does it matter?

Standing Rock brought together questions of Indigenous sovereignty, treaty rights, clean water and climate change in a way that drew worldwide attention. Under the slogan Mni Wiconi — "Water is Life" — it united hundreds of Native nations and many non-Native allies.

  • It renewed national and international attention to Native American treaty rights.
  • It became a model for Indigenous-led environmental movements around the world.
  • The struggle over the pipeline, and over the right to protest, continues in the courts.

Questions to think about

  1. Why do you think Standing Rock drew support from so many different nations and groups?
  2. How should governments balance energy needs against Indigenous rights and environmental risks?
  3. What does the idea of 'water protectors' rather than 'protesters' change?
  4. Should there be limits on lawsuits against protest groups?

What other events are like this?

  • 1966The Wave Hill Walk-Off — Gurindji stockmen and their families walked off a British-owned cattle station. What began as a strike over pay became the foundation of the Aboriginal land rights movement.
  • 1974The Chipko Movement — Village women in the Himalayas placed themselves between the loggers and the forest, and forced a ban on commercial felling.
  • 1981The Greenham Common Women's Peace Camp — Women camped outside an air base for nineteen years to protest against nuclear weapons, inventing new forms of protest along the way.
