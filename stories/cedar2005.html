The Cedar Revolution

After a former prime minister was killed by a truck bomb, hundreds of thousands of Lebanese filled Beirut demanding an end to Syria's thirty-year military presence. Within ten weeks the last Syrian soldiers had gone.

When
14 February – 27 April 2005
Where
Martyrs' Square, Beirut, Lebanon
Facts checked
2026-11-17

See it on the map →

01

What is the background to this event?

Syrian troops had been in Lebanon since 1976, during the civil war, and Syria had great influence over Lebanese politics.1

  • At the start of 2005, Syria had about 14,000 soldiers and intelligence agents in Lebanon.1
  • In 2004, the UN Security Council passed Resolution 1559, calling for the withdrawal of Syrian troops and the disarming of Hezbollah.1
  • On 14 February 2005, the former prime minister Rafic Hariri was assassinated by a truck bomb in Beirut. The attack killed 21 people and wounded nearly 100.1

In their own words

03

What happened?

After Hariri's death, daily protests against the Syrian presence drew tens of thousands to central Beirut. The movement relied on non-violent civil resistance.1

  • Protesters wore red-and-white scarves and blue ribbons, and chanted "Freedom, Sovereignty, Independence".1
  • On 28 February, Prime Minister Omar Karami's pro-Syrian government resigned. Crowds in Martyrs' Square cheered.1
  • On 2 March, Syria's president Bashar al-Assad announced that his troops would leave Lebanon completely "in the next few months".1
  • Hezbollah and its allies organised a large pro-Syrian rally on 8 March. Estimates ranged from 200,000 to more than 500,000.1
  • On 14 March, a month after the assassination, hundreds of thousands rallied in Martyrs' Square, where Hariri was buried.1
  • Karami was reappointed, but his government finally resigned on 19 April. The last Syrian troops left Lebanon on 27 April 2005.1

04

What happened next?

  • Elections: in the 2005 elections, an anti-Syrian bloc led by Saad Hariri won 72 of 128 seats.1
  • Violence: from March 2005 a series of bombings and assassinations killed critics of Syria, including Samir Kassir, George Hawi and Gebran Tueni. These killings remain unsolved.1
  • Justice: a Special Tribunal for Lebanon was set up to try those responsible for Hariri's assassination.1
05

What do others say?

At the time

The name 'Cedar Revolution' was coined by a US official, comparing it with the revolutions in Georgia and Ukraine. In Lebanon it was also called the 'Independence Intifada'.1

Debates since

  • From below or from outside? Some saw the movement as part of a US-backed wave; the journalist Samir Kassir wrote that democracy was spreading "not because of George Bush but despite him".1
  • Whose revolution? The 14 March protests were led by Christian, Sunni and Druze parties; Hezbollah and its allies organised the 8 March counter-demonstrations. The two camps came to dominate Lebanese politics.1
  • Violence: the protests were peaceful, but they took place between a bombing that killed 21 people and a year of further bombings and assassinations.1

07

How is it remembered?

  • The anniversary of Hariri's death has been marked in Martyrs' Square; in 2007, crowds fell silent at 12:55, the time of the explosion.1
08

Why does it matter?

The Cedar Revolution ended Syria's military presence in Lebanon through mass, peaceful protest. But it also showed the country's deep divisions, as the 8 March and 14 March camps became the two poles of Lebanese politics.1

Questions to think about

  1. Why did one assassination bring so many people onto the streets?
  2. Why did Hezbollah organise a counter-demonstration?
  3. Who gave the movement the name 'Cedar Revolution', and why does that matter?

What other events are like this?

  • 2004The Orange Revolution — When a presidential run-off was rigged, hundreds of thousands of Ukrainians camped in the snow in central Kyiv for weeks. The Supreme Court annulled the result, and the election was held again.
  • 2019Lebanon's 17 October Revolution — A tax on WhatsApp calls sparked a nationwide uprising against a political system built on sectarian division. 'All of them means all of them,' protesters chanted — demanding the removal of the entire political class.
  • 2003The Rose Revolution — After a rigged election, Georgians filled the streets for three weeks. Protesters carrying roses entered parliament, and the president resigned without a shot being fired.
