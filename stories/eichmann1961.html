The Eichmann Trial: When Survivors Took the Stand

Adolf Eichmann organised the deportation of Europe's Jews to the death camps. At his trial in Jerusalem, survivors told their stories on the witness stand, night after night, to a worldwide audience — changing how the Holocaust was remembered.

01

What is the background to this event?

Adolf Eichmann, an SS officer, managed the logistics of deporting millions of Jews to ghettos and extermination camps. Captured by the Allies in 1945, he escaped and settled in Argentina, where Israel's Mossad abducted him in May 1960.1

  • At Nuremberg, survivors' testimony had played only a small part.2
02

03

What happened?

The trial began on 11 April 1961 before a special court in Jerusalem. Eichmann was charged with 15 crimes, including crimes against the Jewish people and crimes against humanity.3

  • Unlike Nuremberg, which relied heavily on documents, the trial put Holocaust survivors on the world stage.3
  • It was one of the first trials to be widely televised. Night after night, the public heard first-hand accounts of atrocity from the witness stand.3
  • Survivors such as Jankiel Wiernik, of Treblinka, and Yehiel De-Nur, of Auschwitz, described the camps. De-Nur collapsed in court — a moment, as one account puts it, seared in public memory.3
  • Eichmann was convicted on all fifteen counts and sentenced to death. He was hanged on 1 June 1962.3
04

What happened next?

  • Testimony: the broadcasts brought survivors' first-hand accounts to a worldwide audience as never before.3
  • A debate: Hannah Arendt's book Eichmann in Jerusalem coined the phrase 'the banality of evil', portraying Eichmann as an ordinary bureaucrat rather than a fanatic — a view that has been fiercely debated ever since.3
05

Why does it matter?

The Eichmann trial showed that a courtroom could be a place for victims to tell their history in their own words, in front of the world.3

  • Broadcasts carried survivors' first-hand accounts to a worldwide audience as never before.3
  • Hannah Arendt's account coined the phrase 'the banality of evil', setting off a debate that continues.3
  • The trial was recorded on video, and the recordings survive as a record of the survivors' testimony.3

What do others say?How it was seen at the time, and where historians disagree

At the time

Argentina protested that Eichmann's abduction by Israeli agents violated its sovereignty, and in June 1960 the UN Security Council agreed.1

Debates since

  • Arendt: her portrait of Eichmann as an ordinary bureaucrat remains one of the most famous, and most contested, interpretations of the perpetrators.3

Reading it

The survivor Moshe Bejski gives evidence. Compare this with the photograph of the Nuremberg defendants: there the camera pointed at the accused; here it points at a witness. A face giving testimony carries something no document can — which is exactly why the prosecution put survivors on the stand.
