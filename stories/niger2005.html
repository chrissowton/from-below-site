Niger's Campaign Against the High Cost of Living

When the government of one of the world's poorest countries put VAT on flour, milk and sugar, a coalition of unions and associations brought tens of thousands into the streets — and forced a climbdown.

When
March – April 2005
Where
Niamey, Niger
Facts checked
2026-11-16

See it on the map →

01

What is the background to this event?

In January 2005, the government of Prime Minister Hama Amadou introduced a 19 per cent value added tax (VAT) on basic goods, including flour, milk, sugar, water and electricity.1

  • The tax was part of a budget agreed with international lenders.1
  • Niger was then one of the poorest countries in the world, and a food crisis was developing.

03

What happened?

About 30 associations formed the Coalition Against the High Cost of Living (Coalition contre la vie chère) to oppose the tax.1

  • On 15 March 2005, between 100,000 and 150,000 people marched in Niamey, according to the organisers.1
  • On 22 March, the coalition called a "dead city" day, when shops and markets closed.1
  • Between 25 and 27 March, five leaders of the coalition, including Nouhou Arzika, were arrested.1
  • Protests and strikes continued, and the government opened negotiations.
  • On 19 April, an agreement limited the VAT increase to sugar, and raised taxes on companies and property instead.1

04

What happened next?

  • The food crisis: later in 2005, Niger's food crisis drew international attention.
  • Civil society: the coalition became a model for later campaigns, including against President Tandja's attempt to extend his rule in 2009.
05

What do others say?

At the time

The government argued the tax was needed to balance the budget; the coalition argued it would push the poorest into hunger.1

Debates since

  • Lenders: some see the tax as an example of the costs of austerity demanded by international lenders; others argue that Niger needed new revenue.
  • Numbers: the size of the 15 March march comes from the organisers and may be overstated.1

07

How is it remembered?

  • The coalition's success is remembered as a high point of Nigerien civil society.
08

Why does it matter?

The campaign showed that citizens in a very poor country could resist economic policies made with international lenders.

  • It used non-violent methods: marches, strikes and shop closures.
  • It linked the cost of food to democracy and accountability.

Questions to think about

  1. Why did the VAT on basic goods cause such anger?
  2. What methods did the coalition use?
  3. Why did the government back down?
  4. How do international lenders influence national budgets?

What other events are like this?

  • 2008Mahalla and the April 6 Strike — Women textile workers walked out of Egypt's largest factory, starting a wave of strikes. Their call for a general strike on 6 April 2008 spread through Facebook — and helped create the movement that toppled Mubarak three years later.
  • 2000The Cochabamba Water War — When Bolivia sold its city water system to a multinational consortium and bills soared, farmers, factory workers and residents rose up — and won.
  • 2007Guinea's General Strike of 2007 — When Guinea's ailing president freed two men accused of corruption, the trade unions called a general strike. Weeks of shutdown and protest, met with deadly force and martial law, forced him to share power.
