Djibouti's Road to Independence

When de Gaulle visited in 1966, crowds demanded independence. France answered with a referendum, a barbed-wire fence around the city and mass deportations. It took eleven more years of protest and armed struggle before Djibouti became free in 1977.

01

What is the background to this event?

French Somaliland was a small French colony at the mouth of the Red Sea, of strategic value to France.2

  • Its people were mainly Somali (Issa) and Afar.1
  • In a referendum in 1958, voters chose to stay with France.1
  • The Front for the Liberation of the Somali Coast (FLCS) was founded in 1960 to campaign for independence.2
  • In 1966, France rejected a United Nations recommendation to grant independence.3

03

What happened?

In August 1966, de Gaulle visited the colony and was met by demonstrations.3

  • Police and nationalists clashed on 25–26 August 1966; ten civilians and a police officer were killed.2
  • France announced a new referendum. More demonstrations followed in September, and 21 people died.2
  • A barbed-wire barrier was built around Djibouti City, officially against illegal immigration; nationalists called it a 'wall of shame'.2
  • About 10,000 Somalis were deported, said to lack valid identity papers. Only 14,689 Somalis were allowed to register, compared with 22,004 Afars, although Somalis were the larger group.1
  • In the referendum of 19 March 1967, 60% voted to stay with France. Somali representatives accused France of rigging the vote.1

04

What happened next?

  • Riots and repression: riots broke out after the result. French forces, up to 7,000 strong, crushed them; estimates of the Somali dead range from about 11 to 20. Thousands were rounded up and held in a desert camp.12
  • Armed struggle: many of the deported joined the FLCS, which began armed attacks in 1968.12
  • International pressure: in December 1975 the UN General Assembly called on France to withdraw, and the Organisation of African Unity sent a fact-finding mission in 1976.2
  • Independence: in a referendum on 8 May 1977, voters backed independence almost unanimously. Djibouti became independent on 27 June 1977.34
05

What do others say?

At the time

The French governor claimed that independence would lead to war between Ethiopia, Kenya and Somalia over the territory; nationalists said France had rigged the vote.1

Debates since

  • A fair vote? Most historians accept that the 1967 referendum was manipulated, although the French authorities denied it.1
  • Violence on both sides: French forces killed dozens of protesters between 1966 and 1976 and deported thousands. The FLCS carried out attacks, including a bomb at a bar in Djibouti City in 1970, and claimed the hijacking of a school bus in 1976, which ended with two children dead.12

How is it remembered?

08

Why does it matter?

Djibouti was one of the last African colonies to win independence. Its story shows how a colonial power could use referendums, fences and deportations to hold on — and how protest and international pressure eventually forced it to let go.
