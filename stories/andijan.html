The Andijan Massacre

Thousands gathered in a city square to protest against poverty and corruption, after armed men freed prisoners from the local jail. Government troops sealed off the square and opened fire.

When
13 May 2005
Where
Babur Square, Andijan, Uzbekistan
Facts checked
2026-11-16

See it on the map →

01

What is the background to this event?

Uzbekistan became independent from the Soviet Union in 1991 and was ruled by President Islam Karimov until his death in 2016. His government was one of the most repressive in the world.

  • Opposition parties and independent media were banned. Thousands of people were imprisoned for their religious beliefs, accused of Islamic extremism.
  • The Fergana Valley, where Andijan lies, was densely populated and poor.
  • In June 2004, 23 local businessmen were arrested and charged with "extremism" and membership of a banned Islamic group, Akromiya. They denied it, saying they were targeted because of their growing local popularity.1
  • Their trial drew daily peaceful protests by family members and supporters.

In their own words

03

What happened?

On the night of 12–13 May 2005, armed men attacked the prison where the businessmen were held and freed them, along with hundreds of other prisoners. Several prison guards were killed.1

  • The armed men occupied the regional administration building and took at least twenty officials hostage, demanding the resignation of President Karimov.1
  • Encouraged by the events, thousands of people gathered in Babur Square to voice their anger about poverty and corruption, speaking at microphones set up in the square.1
  • Some stayed because of rumours that Karimov would come to hear their demands.1

In the evening, according to Human Rights Watch, government forces sealed off the area and opened fire on the crowd.1 There are reports that the armed men used hostages as human shields as they tried to escape.1

The government's official count was 187 dead. Other estimates range to several hundred; a former security officer claimed 1,500 were killed.1

04

What happened next?

  • Refugees: hundreds of people fled across the border to Kyrgyzstan, and many were later resettled abroad.
  • Trials and repression: the government held show trials of alleged organisers and harassed survivors, witnesses and journalists.
  • Foreign policy: when Western governments called for an international investigation, Uzbekistan closed a US air base and moved closer to Russia and China, which supported its response.1
  • No inquiry: no independent international investigation has ever been allowed.
05

What do others say?

At the time

The government said that Islamic extremists organised the unrest and that troops responded to armed attackers. Human rights groups and witnesses said soldiers fired on unarmed civilians.1

Debates since

  • Three narratives: the government blamed Islamist extremists; critics argue this was a pretext for repression; others see a struggle between local clans for power.1
  • Violence on both sides: the armed men killed guards and took hostages, and may have used them as shields; the government's response killed far more people, most of them unarmed.1
  • Grievances: even the government acknowledged that poverty and resentment played a role.1

07

How is it remembered?

  • Inside Uzbekistan, public commemoration has been banned. Survivors in exile mark the anniversary each year.
08

Why does it matter?

Andijan was one of the worst massacres of protesters in the post-Soviet world. It showed how the label of "extremism" could be used to silence people's grievances.

  • It raises questions about how to judge a protest that began with an armed attack.
  • It shows how great-power politics can shield governments from accountability.

Questions to think about

  1. Why do you think so many people gathered in the square?
  2. How should we judge a protest that began with an armed attack on a prison?
  3. Why do you think the government refused an international investigation?
  4. How can the label 'extremism' be misused?

What other events are like this?

  • 1986Jeltoqsan: The December Protests of 1986 — When Moscow replaced Kazakhstan's leader with an outsider, Kazakh students marched into the main square of Alma-Ata — one of the first mass protests of the Gorbachev era, crushed by force.
  • 1962The Novocherkassk Strike and Massacre — When the price of meat and butter went up on the same day that their pay was cut, Soviet locomotive workers went on strike. Soldiers opened fire, and the state kept the massacre secret for thirty years.
  • 1989Tiananmen Square, 1989 — Students and workers occupied the heart of Beijing for seven weeks, calling for freedom of speech and an end to corruption, until the army cleared the streets with tanks and gunfire.
