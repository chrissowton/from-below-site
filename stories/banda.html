Banda Singh Bahadur and Land to the Tillers

A Sikh commander raised an army of peasants against Mughal rule in Punjab. He abolished the great landlords' estates and gave the land to those who farmed it — before he was captured and tortured to death in Delhi.

When
1709 – 1716
Where
Sirhind and Lohgarh, Punjab, India
Facts checked
2026-11-16

See it on the map →

01

What is the background to this event?

In the early eighteenth century, Punjab was ruled by the Mughal Empire. Much of the land was held by zamindars — landlords who collected rent and taxes from peasants.1

  • Banda Singh, born Lachman Dev in 1670 in a poor farming family, became an ascetic and later a disciple of the tenth Sikh Guru, Gobind Singh.1
  • The governor of Sirhind, Wazir Khan, had ordered the execution of two of the Guru's young sons, and was suspected in the Guru's own murder.1

In their own words

03

What happened?

From 1709, Banda Singh raised an army in Punjab and attacked Mughal strongholds, beginning with Samana.1

  • He attacked the imperial treasury and the rich.1
  • In 1710, his forces defeated and killed Wazir Khan at Chappar Chiri and took Sirhind.
  • He set up a capital at Lohgarh and issued coins in the name of the Sikh Gurus.
  • He abolished the zamindari system and granted property rights to the tillers of the land.1
  • The Mughals sent large armies. In 1715, after a long siege at Gurdas Nangal, Banda and his followers were captured.1
  • In 1716, they were taken to Delhi. Hundreds of his followers were executed, and Banda was tortured to death.1

04

What happened next?

  • Sikh power: Sikh resistance continued, and by the late eighteenth century Sikh chiefs controlled Punjab.
  • Land: Banda's land reform is remembered as one of the earliest in Indian history.
05

What do others say?

At the time

Mughal chroniclers described Banda as a brutal rebel; Sikh tradition remembers him as a hero and martyr.2

Debates since

  • Violence on both sides: Banda's forces sacked towns such as Samana and Sirhind, and Mughal accounts describe the killing of Muslim civilians; the Mughals in turn executed hundreds of captured Sikhs.12
  • Land reform: historians debate how far the abolition of zamindari was a deliberate policy or a result of war.2

The evidence

Primary source: Seal on an edict

What is it?
Seal on an edict
Made by, and when
Banda Singh Bahadur's chancery, early 1700s

The Persian seal on an edict (hukamnama) issued by Banda Singh Bahadur. Its legend speaks of the kettle to feed and the sword to defend, and credits victory to Guru Nanak and Guru Gobind Singh. Issuing sealed orders was a sign that the Sikh rebels were now governing territory.

Questions to ask of this source

  1. Why would a rebel leader issue orders with a seal?
  2. Why is the seal in Persian, the language of the Mughal court?
  3. What does 'the kettle to feed, the sword to defend' say about the movement's aims?
07

How is it remembered?

  • Banda Singh is honoured as a Sikh hero; memorials stand at Chappar Chiri and in Delhi.
08

Why does it matter?

Banda Singh's rule briefly gave land to those who worked it, and challenged one of the world's most powerful empires.

  • It also shows the violence of war on all sides.

Questions to think about
