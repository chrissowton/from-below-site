The Novocherkassk Strike and Massacre

When the price of meat and butter went up on the same day that their pay was cut, Soviet locomotive workers went on strike. Soldiers opened fire, and the state kept the massacre secret for thirty years.

When
1 – 2 June 1962
Where
Novocherkassk, Rostov region, Russia (USSR)
Facts checked
2026-11-16

01

What is the background to this event?

In the Soviet Union, the Communist Party claimed to rule on behalf of the working class, and strikes were in effect forbidden. After Stalin's death in 1953, his successor Nikita Khrushchev denounced Stalin's crimes and promised a better life: the Soviet Union would overtake the United States in the production of meat, milk and butter.1

  • Soviet agriculture was failing to meet its targets, and food shortages were common.1
  • On 31 May 1962, the government announced that the retail price of meat would rise by 30 per cent and butter by 25 per cent from 1 June.1
  • At the Novocherkassk Electric Locomotive Works (NEVZ), which employed around 13,000 people, conditions were poor, and management had been raising work norms — in effect cutting pay by up to 30 per cent for many workers.1

The coincidence of the price rises and the pay cuts proved explosive.

  • Anastas Mikoyan and Frol Kozlov

    Members of the Soviet leadership sent from Moscow to deal with the crisis.

  • General Issa Pliev

    Commander of the North Caucasus Military District, ordered to send troops.

  • The seven

    Seven men sentenced to death and shot as 'ringleaders' after show trials.

In their own words

03

What happened?

On the morning of 1 June 1962, workers in the steel foundry of NEVZ stopped work and gathered to complain about the price rises and their pay. When a woman worker asked the director, Boris Kurochkin, how they could afford meat, he reportedly replied: "If there isn't enough money for meat and sausage, let them eat pirozhki" (pies).1 His remark spread through the factory:1

  • Workers sounded the factory siren, bringing thousands more from the plant and nearby settlements.
  • By noon, 6,000–7,000 people had gathered. Strikers stopped a passenger train on the nearby railway line and painted slogans on the locomotive: "Meat, butter, a pay raise" and "Make sausage out of Khrushchev".
  • That afternoon, troops and tanks were sent into the city, and a curfew was imposed.

On the morning of 2 June, workers found the factory occupied by soldiers. Between 5,000 and 12,000 people formed a column and marched nine kilometres into the city centre, carrying portraits of Lenin and red flags and singing the "Internationale". At the bridge over the Tuzlov River, tanks blocked the way, but did not move as the marchers climbed over them.1

The crowd reached the Communist Party headquarters (the gorkom) on the main square and occupied the building, demanding to speak to officials. Soldiers opened fire on the crowd. According to official records, 23 or 24 people were killed; other sources say 26 were killed and 87 seriously wounded. The bodies were buried secretly in unmarked graves in several cemeteries.1

04

What happened next?

  • Show trials: more than 100 people were convicted of "mass disorder" and "banditry". Seven men were sentenced to death and shot; others received long prison sentences.
  • Silence: the events were kept secret. Nothing was reported in the Soviet press, and the people of Novocherkassk were warned not to speak about them.
  • Food supplies: the city was quickly supplied with extra food. The regime became much more cautious about raising food prices, and spent heavily on importing grain and subsidising food for the rest of the Soviet era.
  • Truth emerges: under glasnost, journalists began to investigate from 1989. Official investigations followed after 1991, the burial places were found, and most of those convicted were rehabilitated in the 1990s.
05

What do others say?

At the time

The authorities blamed "hooligans", "criminal elements" and "provocateurs" for the disorder, and claimed that troops had acted in self-defence. Survivors and witnesses described a peaceful march fired upon without warning.

Historians' debates

  • Who gave the order to fire? Investigations in the 1990s did not establish clearly who gave the order; historians point to the senior leaders sent from Moscow, while some witnesses claimed that soldiers fired into the air and that other forces fired into the crowd.
  • Spontaneous or organised? Samuel Baron and Vladimir Kozlov describe a largely spontaneous revolt that the authorities mishandled.
  • How many died? Official figures of around 24 are disputed by some survivors, who believe that more died.
06

The evidence

  • KGB photographs and files, opened in the 1990s, including photographs taken to identify participants.
  • The records of the trials and of the investigations of the Russian Chief Military Prosecutor (1991–92).
  • Survivors' testimonies, collected by journalists and historians from 1989 onwards.
  • Aleksandr Solzhenitsyn's account in The Gulag Archipelago, based on reports that reached him.

07

How is it remembered?

  • A memorial stone, the "Stone on the Blood", stands on the site of the shootings in Novocherkassk.
  • A plaque on Palace Square bears seven bullet holes, for the seven executed men.
  • The film Dear Comrades! (2020), directed by Andrei Konchalovsky, dramatised the events.
08

Why does it matter?

The Novocherkassk strike shows workers in the "workers' state" rising up against the regime that claimed to represent them — and being shot down. It exposed the limits of Khrushchev's "thaw" and the brutality the Soviet state was still prepared to use.

  • It is the best-known of a number of workers' revolts in the post-Stalin Soviet Union, all hidden at the time.
  • Its cover-up shows how authoritarian states control memory.
  • Its lesson — that food prices could provoke revolt — shaped Soviet economic policy for decades.

What other events are like this?

  • 1980The Gdańsk Shipyard Strike and Solidarity — A strike over the sacking of a crane operator grew into the first independent trade union in the Soviet bloc, with ten million members.
  • 1956The Hungarian Revolution of 1956 — Students, workers and soldiers rose against Hungary's Soviet-backed dictatorship and pulled down Stalin's statue. For twelve days they won — until Soviet tanks returned.
  • 1907The Santa María School Massacre — Thousands of striking nitrate miners and their families were shot down in and around a school in Iquique — one of the worst massacres of workers in Latin American history.
