Workers & unions

Rebel Patagonia

Sheep-farm workers in the far south of Argentina went on strike for better conditions. The army was sent in, and hundreds — perhaps 1,500 — were shot, many after they had surrendered.

When
1920 – 1922
Where
Río Gallegos, Santa Cruz, Argentina
Facts checked

01

What is the background to this event?

Southern Patagonia was dominated by huge sheep ranches, worked by rural labourers, many from Chile and Spain.1

  • Workers' unions grew in the region from the 1910s.1
  • Many workers had fled to Argentina after a strike in Puerto Natales in Chile was crushed in 1919.1
02

Who were the key people?

  • Rural workers of Santa Cruz

    Shearers, farmhands and labourers, many Chilean and Spanish.

  • Antonio Soto

    Spanish anarchist who led the Río Gallegos workers' society.

  • Colonel Héctor Benigno Varela

    Army officer who led the suppression.

  • President Hipólito Yrigoyen

In their own words

03

What happened?

Between 1920 and 1922, rural workers in Santa Cruz went on strike and took control of parts of the territory.1

  • President Yrigoyen sent Colonel Varela's 10th Cavalry Regiment.1
  • Between 300 and 1,500 rural workers were shot, many by firing squad after surrendering.1
  • Most of the executed were Spanish and Chilean workers.1
  • At least two soldiers, three police officers and a number of ranch owners and their relatives also died.1
04

What happened next?

  • Revenge: in 1923, the German anarchist Kurt Wilckens assassinated Colonel Varela in Buenos Aires; Wilckens was himself killed in prison.
  • Silence: the government avoided an official investigation.1
05

Why does it matter?

Rebel Patagonia was one of the worst massacres of workers in Latin American history.

  • For decades, it was almost erased from Argentina's official memory.
  • In 1923 an anarchist assassinated the colonel who led the killings; the government avoided any official investigation.1
  • A journalist's history became the film La Patagonia rebelde (1974), which was banned for a time.
  • Monuments to the strikers now stand in Santa Cruz.

What do others say?How it was seen at the time, and where historians disagree

At the time

The army and landowners described the strikers as bandits; unions and anarchists called it a massacre.1

Debates since

  • The numbers: estimates of the dead range from 300 to 1,500.1
  • Violence on both sides: strikers took ranches and hostages, and soldiers, police and some ranchers died; the army shot hundreds of workers, many as prisoners. Claims that strikers raped captive women were spread by the army and landowners and are disputed.1
