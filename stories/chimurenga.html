The First Chimurenga

Six years after Cecil Rhodes's company seized their land, the Ndebele and Shona rose together. The spirit medium Nehanda, hanged for her part, became the symbol of Zimbabwe's struggle.

When
1896 – 1897
Where
Matabeleland and Mashonaland (Zimbabwe)
Facts checked
2026-11-15

See it on the map →

01

What is the background to this event?

In 1890, the British South Africa Company (BSAC) of Cecil Rhodes occupied Mashonaland, and in 1893 it conquered the Ndebele kingdom of Matabeleland.

  • The company seized land and cattle, imposed a hut tax and forced Africans to work for settlers and mines.
  • In 1896, drought, locusts and the cattle disease rinderpest devastated the country. Spiritual leaders blamed the settlers.1
  • The company's administrator, Leander Starr Jameson, had sent most of his troops on the failed Jameson Raid into the Transvaal, leaving the colony nearly defenceless.1
02

Who were the key people?

  • Mbuya Nehanda (Charwe Nyakasikana)

    A Shona spirit medium who inspired resistance. She was hanged in 1898.

  • Sekuru Kaguvi

    A Shona spirit medium and leader of the rising, hanged alongside her.

  • The Mlimo

    The Ndebele religious oracle, whose priests helped plan the rising in Matabeleland.

  • The settlers

    Almost 4,000 white settlers, several hundred of whom were killed in the rising.

In their own words

03

What happened?

In March 1896, the Ndebele rose against the company.1 The Shona of Mashonaland joined in June, surprising the settlers, who had believed them peaceful.

  • Within a week, 141 settlers were killed in Matabeleland and, as the rising spread, another 103 in Mashonaland. Men, women and children were killed, and hundreds of homes, farms and mines were burned.1
  • Settlers retreated into fortified laagers in Bulawayo and Salisbury, while armed patrols went out to rescue survivors and attack the Ndebele.1
  • Spiritual leaders, including Nehanda and Kaguvi in Mashonaland, played a central role in inspiring and coordinating resistance.

Company and British imperial forces, armed with Maxim machine guns, crushed the rising. Rhodes negotiated a peace with Ndebele leaders in the Matopos hills in 1896. In Mashonaland, the fighting lasted into 1897; soldiers dynamited caves where Shona families had taken refuge, killing many.

Nehanda and Kaguvi were captured. Nehanda was convicted of ordering the killing of a company official, Henry Pollard, and hanged in March 1898.3

04

What happened next?

  • Colonial rule: Southern Rhodesia became a settler colony, and Africans lost most of the best land.
  • "My bones will rise again": according to tradition, Nehanda prophesied before her death that her bones would rise again. The nationalist war of the 1960s and 1970s was named the Second Chimurenga.
  • Independence: Zimbabwe became independent in 1980.
  • Remains: skulls taken to Britain after the rising are held in the Natural History Museum in London; Zimbabwe has demanded their return.
05

Why does it matter?

The First Chimurenga was one of the largest African risings against colonial rule. It showed that Ndebele and Shona could unite against a common enemy, and its memory inspired Zimbabwe's liberation struggle.

  • It shows the role of religion and spiritual leaders in resistance.
  • It raises questions about how violence by colonised people and by colonisers is remembered.
  • The nationalist war of the 1960s and 1970s was named the Second Chimurenga, and Zimbabwe became independent in 1980.

What do others say?How it was seen at the time, and where historians disagree

At the time

Settlers described the rising as a savage massacre. Africans saw it as a war to recover their land and freedom.

Historians' debates

  • Coordinated or separate? Terence Ranger argued that religious leaders coordinated the risings; later historians, such as Julian Cobbing, questioned how centralised it was.
  • Violence: the killing of settler families, including women and children, is part of this history; so are the far larger numbers of Africans killed by machine guns, burnings and dynamited caves.
  • Political memory: after independence, Nehanda became a national symbol; some historians criticise how ZANU-PF has used her memory for political purposes.

Reading it

The colonial police took and published this photograph of Nehanda and Kaguvi after their capture, to show that the rising had been crushed. Both were executed. For Zimbabweans today the same picture means something else entirely — dignity in defeat. What it cannot show is what either of them thought or said.

How is it remembered?Memorials, anniversaries and arguments over memory

  • Nehanda appears in songs, novels such as Yvonne Vera's Nehanda (1993), and street names.3
