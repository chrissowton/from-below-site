Otpor! and the Fall of Milošević

A handful of students with a clenched-fist logo and a sense of humour built a movement of 70,000 young people. Their non-violent campaign helped bring down Slobodan Milošević.

When
1998 – 5 October 2000
Where
National Assembly, Belgrade, Serbia
Facts checked
2026-11-15

01

What is the background to this event?

In the 1990s, Serbia was ruled by Slobodan Milošević, whose government was linked to wars in Croatia, Bosnia and Kosovo.

  • In 1998, a new university law ended the independence of universities.1
  • The government banned independent newspapers.1
02

Who were the key people?

  • Students of Belgrade's universities

    Who founded Otpor! ('Resistance!').

  • Slobodan Milošević

In their own words

03

What happened?

Otpor was founded in Belgrade on 10 October 1998 by students and young activists.1

  • Its symbol was a clenched fist.1
  • The authorities reacted heavily: students were arrested just for stencilling its logo.1
  • Some leaders used Gene Sharp's writings on non-violent action as a guide.1
  • Otpor united opposition parties, and its "He's finished!" campaign urged young people to vote.1
  • Nearly 2,000 activists were arrested, and some were beaten.1
  • When Milošević refused to accept defeat in the September 2000 election, huge crowds took over the parliament on 5 October, and he fell.1

04

What happened next?

  • Watchdog: after the overthrow, Otpor monitored the new government and pressed for co-operation with the war crimes tribunal in The Hague.1
  • Influence: Otpor's methods were copied in Georgia, Ukraine and the Arab Spring; some former leaders founded a centre to train activists.2
05

What do others say?

At the time

The government called Otpor a terrorist group serving foreign powers; supporters saw a movement of young citizens.1

Debates since

  • Foreign help: US organisations gave Otpor money and training; critics see foreign interference, while others stress that Serbians made the movement.1
  • Violence: Otpor was non-violent; police arrested and beat activists. On 5 October, some protesters set fire to the parliament and state television.1

How is it remembered?

  • The clenched fist has been copied by protest movements around the world.
08

Why does it matter?

Otpor showed how a decentralised, non-violent youth movement could help topple an authoritarian leader.

Questions to think about

  1. Why did Otpor use humour and symbols?
  2. Why did the government arrest people for stencilling a logo?
  3. How important was foreign funding?
  4. Why did Otpor's methods spread to other countries?
09

What other events are like this?

  • 2003The Rose Revolution — After a rigged election, Georgians filled the streets for three weeks. Protesters carrying roses entered parliament, and the president resigned without a shot being fired.
  • 1968Belgrade 1968: 'Down with the Red Bourgeoisie' — In socialist Yugoslavia, students occupied their university and accused the party elite of betraying socialism. Tito went on television and told the nation: 'The students are right.'
  • 2020The Belarusian Protests of 2020 — When Alexander Lukashenko claimed 80 per cent of the vote after 26 years in power, Belarusians came out in the largest protests in their history. Three women led the challenge. Tens of thousands were arrested.
