The Pidjiguiti Dock Strike and Massacre

Dockers and sailors in Bissau stopped work for better pay. Portuguese police opened fire, killing at least 25 in minutes. The massacre convinced the independence movement that peaceful protest was impossible.

When
3 August 1959
Where
Pidjiguiti docks, Bissau, Portuguese Guinea (now Guinea-Bissau)
Facts checked
2026-11-15

See it on the map →

01

What is the background to this event?

Portuguese Guinea was a small, poor colony ruled by the dictatorship of António Salazar, which refused to consider independence.

  • A Portuguese conglomerate, CUF, controlled much of the trade at the docks through its subsidiary Casa Gouveia.1
  • Wages were very low. A dock strike in 1956 had failed.1
  • In late 1958, a senior Portuguese official warned that a revolt was likely and advised granting the workers' demands. His advice was ignored.1
  • The PAIGC, the independence party founded by Amílcar Cabral in 1956, knew of the strike plans and supported them.1

In their own words

03

What happened?

On the morning of 3 August 1959, the workers met the manager of Casa Gouveia to ask for higher wages. When he refused, they stopped work at 3pm, as planned.1

  • The manager called the PIDE, who arrived at about 4pm and ordered the men back to work.1
  • The strikers refused, closed the gates of the quay and armed themselves with oars and harpoons.1
  • The police opened fire and threw grenades. The workers had nowhere to run.1
  • Some escaped in boats; others were shot in the water or arrested.1
  • Between 25 and 50 workers were killed, and many more wounded.1

04

What happened next?

  • Arrests: the authorities blamed the PAIGC and arrested its members.1
  • Armed struggle: the PAIGC abandoned non-violent resistance, moved to organise in the countryside, and began a war of independence in 1963.1
  • Independence: the PAIGC declared independence in 1973; Portugal recognised it in 1974, after the Carnation Revolution.
05

What do others say?

At the time

The Portuguese authorities presented the strike as subversion organised by the PAIGC.1

Debates since

  • The PAIGC's role: historians debate how far the party organised the strike or simply supported it.2
  • Death toll: estimates range from 25 to 50 or more.1
  • Violence: the strikers carried oars and harpoons; the police used guns and grenades against men trapped on a quay.1

07

How is it remembered?

  • 3 August is a public holiday in Guinea-Bissau, the Day of the Martyrs of Colonialism.
  • A monument in the shape of a clenched fist stands near the docks.
08

Why does it matter?

Pidjiguiti was a turning point: a strike over wages that became the starting point of a war for independence.

  • It shows how repression of peaceful protest can push movements towards armed struggle.
  • The war in Guinea helped bring down the dictatorship in Portugal itself.

09

What other events are like this?

  • 1960The Mueda Massacre — Makonde farmers gathered peacefully to ask the Portuguese for better prices and independence. Soldiers opened fire. The massacre convinced many that only armed struggle could end colonial rule.
  • 1961The Baixa de Cassanje Revolt — Angolan farmers forced to grow cotton for a colonial company refused to work and burned their identity cards. Portugal answered with air raids — and within weeks Angola's war of independence had begun.
  • 1974The Carnation Revolution — Young officers overthrew Europe's oldest dictatorship with barely a shot fired. Then the people took to the streets with carnations — and kept going, occupying land, factories and homes.
