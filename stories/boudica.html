Boudica's Revolt

When Roman officials seized the kingdom of the Iceni, flogged its queen and raped her daughters, Boudica led the peoples of eastern Britain in a revolt that burned three Roman towns — before ending in a massacre.

When
60 CE – 61
Where
Camulodunum (Colchester), Londinium (London) and Verulamium (St Albans), Roman Britain
Facts checked
2026-11-16

See it on the map →

01

What is the background to this event?

Rome invaded south-eastern Britain in 43 CE. Some kingdoms were conquered; others, such as the Iceni of what is now Norfolk, became Roman allies.1

  • The Iceni king, Prasutagus, left his kingdom jointly to his daughters and the emperor. When he died, his will was ignored.1
  • According to Tacitus, Romans seized land and enslaved Iceni; Prasutagus's widow, Boudica, was flogged and their daughters raped.1
  • According to Cassius Dio, Roman financiers, including Seneca, suddenly called in their loans.1
  • At Camulodunum, a colony of Roman veterans, a huge temple to the emperor Claudius had been built at local expense.1

In their own words

03

What happened?

In 60 CE or 61, while the governor was attacking the druid stronghold of Anglesey, the Iceni and the Trinovantes rose under Boudica.1

  • They destroyed Camulodunum. The last defenders held out in the temple of Claudius for two days before it fell.1
  • A Roman legion sent to relieve the town was defeated; only its commander and some cavalry escaped.1
  • The rebels then burned Londinium and Verulamium (St Albans).1
  • Tacitus says that 70,000 Romans and their British allies were killed in the three towns; Roman writers describe brutal killings of civilians.12
  • Suetonius gathered his army and defeated the Britons in a battle somewhere in the Midlands. Tacitus claims 80,000 Britons were killed, including women and children.12
  • Boudica died soon after — by poison, according to Tacitus, or of illness, according to Dio.1

04

What happened next?

  • Reprisals: the Romans laid waste to the lands of the rebel peoples.
  • A change of policy: Suetonius was replaced by a governor who followed a more conciliatory policy.
  • Roman Britain lasted until about 410 CE.1
05

What do others say?

At the time

Roman writers presented the revolt as a savage outburst, but Tacitus also blamed Roman greed and cruelty for provoking it.2

Debates since

  • Sources: all our written evidence comes from Roman authors writing decades later, and their numbers are almost certainly exaggerated.3
  • Violence on both sides: the rebels killed many civilians in the towns; the Romans killed many fighters, women and children in the final battle and afterwards.12
  • The burned layer: archaeologists have found a layer of burned debris in Colchester, London and St Albans from this period.3
06

The evidence

  • Tacitus and Cassius Dio.2
  • Burned layers and destroyed buildings found in excavations of the three towns.

Primary source: Object: a bronze head

What is it?
Object: a bronze head
Made by, and when
Roman Britain, AD 54–61

The head of a life-size bronze statue of the emperor Nero, found in the River Alde in Suffolk. The statue was broken and the head thrown into the river. It may have been torn down during the revolt or left as an offering — either way, it shows how the image of Roman power could be attacked.

07

How is it remembered?

  • Thomas Thornycroft's statue of Boudica stands at Westminster, opposite Parliament.
  • She has been claimed by Victorian imperialists, suffragettes and modern feminists alike.3
08

Why does it matter?

Boudica's revolt is one of the most famous anti-colonial risings of the ancient world — and a reminder that its violence fell on civilians on both sides.

  • It shows how imperial greed and humiliation could provoke revolt.

Questions to think about
