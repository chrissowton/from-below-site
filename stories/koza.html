The Koza Uprising

After 25 years of US military rule and repeated acquittals of servicemen who harmed Okinawans, a traffic accident sparked a night of fury outside one of America's largest air bases.

When
20 December 1970
Where
Koza (now Okinawa City), Okinawa, Japan
Facts checked
2026-11-16

See it on the map →

01

What is the background to this event?

After the Battle of Okinawa in 1945, in which tens of thousands of Okinawan civilians died, the United States occupied the island. While the rest of Japan regained independence in 1952, Okinawa stayed under US military administration for another twenty years.1

  • The US built huge bases, seizing farmland. Okinawans had limited political rights.
  • Crimes and accidents involving US servicemen were common, and servicemen were tried by US military courts, not Okinawan ones.1
  • In September 1970, a serviceman who killed an Okinawan woman in a road accident in Itoman was acquitted by a court-martial, causing anger across the island.1
  • By 1970, it was known that Okinawa would return to Japan in 1972 — but that the US bases would stay.1

03

What happened?

At about 1 a.m. on 20 December 1970, a drunk American serviceman driving near Kadena Air Base hit an Okinawan man with his car. The man was not seriously hurt, but taxi drivers who had seen the accident surrounded the Americans, and a crowd gathered, shouting "no more acquittals" and "Yankee go home".1

  • When US military police arrived, they focused on extracting the Americans, angering the crowd.1
  • After warning shots were fired, the crowd grew to about 5,000 people, facing around 700 military police.1
  • Rioters overturned and burned about 80 cars belonging to Americans, threw rocks and Molotov cocktails, and pulled some servicemen from their cars and beat them.1
  • Some danced traditional Okinawan folk dances amid the chaos.1
  • Around 500 people broke through the fence into Kadena Air Base, burning buildings including the military employment office.1

The military police used tear gas, and the riot ended at around 7 a.m. About 60 Americans and 27 Okinawans were injured, and 82 people were arrested.1 No one was killed.

04

What happened next?

  • Reversion: Okinawa returned to Japan on 15 May 1972 — but most US bases remained.
  • Continuing protest: today about 70 per cent of the land used exclusively by US forces in Japan is on Okinawa, which makes up less than 1 per cent of Japan's land area. In 1995, the rape of a 12-year-old girl by three US servicemen led to protests by tens of thousands of people.
  • Henoko: plans to move the Futenma air base to Henoko, in the north of the island, have been opposed by repeated protests and votes by Okinawans.
05

What do others say?

At the time

US authorities described the event as a riot by a lawless mob. Many Okinawans saw it as an understandable explosion of anger after years of injustice, though many also regretted the violence.

Historians' debates

  • Riot or uprising? Some Okinawans call the event the Koza bōdō (riot), others the Koza hanran or uprising, reflecting different judgements.
  • The violence: rioters attacked Americans and destroyed property; at the same time, accounts note that the crowd mainly targeted American-owned cars and the base itself, and that no one was killed.
  • Spontaneous or planned? Most accounts describe it as spontaneous, with no organisers.1

07

How is it remembered?

  • Okinawa City has held exhibitions and events on the anniversary, especially the fiftieth anniversary in 2020.
  • The uprising appears in Okinawan music, film and literature.
08

Why does it matter?

The Koza uprising expressed decades of anger at foreign military rule and at a system that seemed to put servicemen above the law. It is a key moment in the history of Okinawan resistance.

  • It raises questions about justice for civilians living near military bases around the world.
  • It shows how a minor incident can spark an explosion when grievances have built up.
  • Okinawa's struggle over the bases continues today.

Questions to think about
