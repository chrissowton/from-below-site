Workers & unions

The Canton–Hong Kong Strike

After British police shot protesters in Shanghai, a quarter of a million workers left Hong Kong and boycotted British goods. The colony became a ghost town.

When
June 1925 – October 1926
Where
Hong Kong and Guangzhou (Canton), China

See it on the map →

01

What is the background to this event?

In the 1920s, much of China's trade was controlled by foreign powers, which held "concessions" in Chinese cities. Chinese workers in foreign-owned factories and ports worked long hours for low pay, and in Hong Kong, Chinese residents faced discriminatory laws.

  • Hong Kong seamen had already won a major strike in 1922.
  • The Kuomintang and the young Chinese Communist Party were cooperating in Guangzhou, with Soviet advisers.
  • On 30 May 1925, police in the Shanghai International Settlement fired on Chinese demonstrators protesting the killing of a striking worker, killing at least nine people.1 Anger spread across China — the May Thirtieth Movement.
02

Who were the key people?

  • Su Zhaozheng

    A seamen's union leader who chaired the strike committee.

  • Deng Zhongxia

    A communist labour organiser who helped lead the strike.

  • The strikers

    Seamen, dockers, servants, tram workers and many others who left Hong Kong.

  • Cai Linzheng

    A picket leader shot dead during the strike.

03

What happened?

Communist-affiliated unions began the strike on 19 June 1925.1 Their demands included:1

  • freedom of assembly, speech, the press and the right to strike;
  • equal treatment of all residents under the law and the abolition of laws targeting Chinese people;
  • universal suffrage;
  • an eight-hour day, a minimum wage, better conditions for women and children, and a 40 per cent cut in rents.

On 23 June 1925, a march in Guangzhou passed the Shamian (Shakee) island concession. British soldiers on the island opened fire, killing over 50 Chinese protesters and wounding almost 200.1

  • The strikers set up a Strikers' Delegates Congress of 800 delegates, an elected strike committee and even their own court.1
  • Some 250,000 people left Hong Kong for Guangdong. Food prices soared, and by July the colony was a ghost town.1
  • Strikers' pickets blockaded British shipping and patrolled the borders to stop smuggling. They had powers of arrest, which were sometimes used harshly.1

In 1926, as Chiang Kai-shek turned against the communists, the KMT moved to end the strike. It finished in October 1926 with few of its demands met.1

04

What happened next?

  • Economic damage: Britain had to provide a £3 million loan to save Hong Kong's economy.1
  • The split: in April 1927, Chiang Kai-shek massacred communists and labour activists in Shanghai, ending the alliance and beginning a long civil war.
  • Hong Kong: the colonial government passed laws restricting strikes and unions, but also began to include more Chinese residents in government.
05

What do others say?

At the time

The British blamed Bolshevik agitators for the strike. Strikers and nationalists saw it as resistance to imperialism and exploitation.

Historians' debates

  • Spontaneous or organised? Historians debate the role of communists and Soviet advisers versus workers' own grievances.
  • Pickets' violence: the strike pickets' powers to arrest and punish those who broke the boycott led to abuses, and some Hong Kong Chinese merchants opposed the strike.
  • Success or failure? The strike won few demands, but some historians argue it forced Hong Kong's government to reform.

The evidence

07

How is it remembered?

  • A monument at Shamian in Guangzhou commemorates the victims of 23 June 1925.
08

Why does it matter?

The Canton–Hong Kong strike was one of the longest and largest strikes in history. It showed the power of workers combining labour demands with anti-imperialism.

  • The strikers' congress was an experiment in workers' democracy.
  • It shows how labour movements could be caught up in party politics.

Questions to think about

  1. Why did the shooting in Shanghai spark a strike in Hong Kong?
  2. Why do you think so many workers left Hong Kong?
  3. Was the strikers' congress a form of democracy?
  4. Why did the strike end with few demands met?
09

What other events are like this?

  • 1915The Glasgow Rent Strikes — Working-class women in wartime Glasgow refused to pay rent rises and physically blocked evictions — forcing the government to introduce rent controls.
  • 1919The Winnipeg General Strike — For six weeks, 30,000 workers brought Canada's third-largest city to a halt to win the right to collective bargaining. It ended with 'Bloody Saturday' — and transformed Canadian politics.
  • 1955The Hock Lee Bus Strike — Bus workers sacked for joining a union went on strike, and Chinese-school students came to support them. A night of rioting left four people dead and became a defining moment in Singapore's history.
