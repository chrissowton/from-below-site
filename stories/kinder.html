The Kinder Scout Mass Trespass

Hundreds of young working-class walkers from Manchester deliberately trespassed on a private grouse moor to demand the right to roam.

When
24 April 1932
Where
Kinder Scout, Peak District, Derbyshire, England
Facts checked
2026-11-16

See it on the map →

01

What is the background to this event?

In the 1930s the open moorland of the Peak District lay within easy reach of the industrial cities of Manchester and Sheffield. Thousands of factory and office workers took trains and buses into the hills at weekends to escape the smoke and poverty of the cities — rambling was one of the most popular working-class pastimes of the age.

Yet most of the high moorland was closed to them:

  • Large areas, including the Kinder Scout plateau, were privately owned and kept as grouse moors for shooting, used by their owners for only a few days each year.
  • Gamekeepers patrolled the moors and turned walkers back, sometimes with violence.
  • Attempts to win access through Parliament, beginning with James Bryce's Access to Mountains Bill in 1884, had repeatedly failed.

Established rambling clubs preferred negotiation. Frustrated by the lack of progress, some younger activists — many of them connected with the British Workers' Sports Federation, which was linked to the Communist Party — decided on direct action.

03

What happened?

On Sunday 24 April 1932, the trespassers gathered at Bowden Bridge quarry near the village of Hayfield. The police, having heard of the plan, were waiting at the railway station, but the walkers avoided them. Rothman addressed the crowd from a ledge in the quarry.1

Accounts of the numbers vary widely: The Times reported about 100, Rothman later claimed 600 to 800, and the generally accepted figure is the estimate of about 400 given by the Manchester Guardian.1

  • The walkers set off up William Clough towards the Kinder plateau.
  • On the slopes they were confronted by gamekeepers, and there were scuffles; one keeper was injured.1
  • The trespassers reached the plateau near Ashop Head, where they met a group of ramblers who had come from Sheffield, before returning to Hayfield.1

On the return, five ramblers were arrested, and another had been detained earlier.1 At their trial at Derby Assizes, one was acquitted; one was sentenced to six months in prison for causing bodily harm to a keeper; and the remainder, including Rothman, received sentences of between two and four months for incitement to riotous assembly.1 Many observers regarded the sentences as excessive, and the case attracted widespread sympathy for the cause of access.

04

What happened next?

  • Rallies: later in 1932 an access rally in Winnats Pass, near Castleton, drew around 10,000 people.
  • National Parks: the National Parks and Access to the Countryside Act 1949 led to the creation of the Peak District as Britain's first National Park in 1951, with some access agreements.
  • Long-distance paths: the Pennine Way, which begins in Edale at the foot of Kinder Scout, opened in 1965.
  • The right to roam: the Countryside and Rights of Way Act 2000 finally gave walkers a legal right of access to large areas of open moor, mountain and common land in England and Wales.
05

What do others say?

At the time

The landowners and the courts treated the trespass as a criminal assault on property and on the gamekeepers. Many established ramblers' organisations also disapproved, fearing that confrontation would damage their patient negotiations with landowners.

Historians' debates

  • Did it help or hinder? In 2002 Philip Day of the Manchester Ramblers' Federation argued that opponents of access used the trespass as an argument against reform for decades, making it a "positive hindrance" to the movement.1
  • Did they reach the summit? The access campaigner Tom Stephenson argued in 1989 that the trespassers reached only Ashop Head, not the summit plateau of Kinder Scout proper.1
  • A legend? The historian David Hey questioned the story of the trespass as "a simple explanation of the triumph of the 'right to roam' movement", stressing the long, quieter campaigns of other ramblers.1 Other historians accept this while still emphasising the trespass's symbolic importance.

The evidence

  • Contemporary newspaper reports, which differed sharply in their estimates and their sympathies.

07

How is it remembered?

  • A plaque at Bowden Bridge quarry, unveiled by Benny Rothman in 1982 on the 50th anniversary, marks the start of the trespass.
  • The National Trust bought the Kinder estate in 1982, opening it to the public.
  • On the 70th anniversary in 2002, the Duke of Devonshire, whose family had owned grouse moors in the area, apologised for the treatment of the trespassers.
  • Anniversary walks and events are held regularly, and the story is told in a permanent exhibition in Hayfield.
08

Why does it matter?

The mass trespass has become the most famous symbol of the struggle for public access to the countryside in Britain. It framed the question as one of class and justice: whether a small number of landowners should be able to exclude the public from vast areas of open land for their private recreation.

  • It helped build public support for National Parks and, eventually, the right to roam.
  • It shows how direct action by young, working-class people can shift a debate, even when established organisations disapprove.
  • Debates about access to land — in Scotland, England and elsewhere — continue today.

Questions to think about

  1. Should people have a right to walk on private land? Where should the limits be?
  2. Why might established rambling organisations have disapproved of the trespass?
  3. Can a protest be important as a symbol even if its immediate effects were limited?
  4. Who has access to green space in your area, and who does not?
