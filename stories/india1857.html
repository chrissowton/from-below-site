The Indian Rebellion of 1857

Indian soldiers in the East India Company's army mutinied at Meerut and marched on Delhi. Their revolt spread into a great rebellion of soldiers, peasants and princes across northern India.

When
May 1857 – 1858
Where
Meerut, Delhi and northern India
Facts checked

See it on the map →

01

What is the background to this event?

By 1857, the East India Company ruled much of India.1

  • Resentment had grown over British-style social reforms, harsh land taxes and the treatment of landowners and princes.1
  • Many doubted British claims that their rule improved India's economy.1
  • Sepoys (Indian soldiers) had grievances over overseas service and the new Enfield rifle cartridges, rumoured to be greased with cow and pig fat.1
  • In March 1857, Mangal Pandey, a sepoy at Barrackpore, attacked his officers.1

In their own words

03

What happened?

On 10 May 1857, sepoys at Meerut mutinied and marched to Delhi, where Bahadur Shah Zafar was declared emperor.1

  • The rebels took large parts of the North-Western Provinces and Awadh.1
  • In Awadh especially, the revolt became a popular rising against British rule.1
  • At Cawnpore (Kanpur), British soldiers and civilians who surrendered were killed, and women and children held hostage were later massacred at the Bibighar.1
  • Company forces retook Kanpur in July and Delhi in September, and Delhi and Lucknow were laid waste.1
  • Many Indians fought for the British, including the Sikh princes of Punjab, and many princely states stayed loyal.1
  • The rebellion was defeated at Gwalior in June 1858.1
04

What happened next?

  • End of the Company: the Government of India Act 1858 abolished Company rule, and India was ruled directly by the British Crown.1
  • The last Mughal: Bahadur Shah Zafar was exiled to Rangoon.3
05

Why does it matter?

1857 was the greatest challenge to British rule in India in the nineteenth century, and changed how India was governed.

  • It remains one of the most contested events in the history of empire.
  • It ended the rule of the East India Company: from 1858, India was ruled directly by the British Crown.1
  • The last Mughal emperor was exiled to Rangoon.3

What do others say?How it was seen at the time, and where historians disagree

At the time

The British called it a mutiny; many rebels fought to restore older rulers and ways of life.1

Debates since

  • Its name: it is called the Sepoy Mutiny, the Great Rebellion and the First War of Independence; V. D. Savarkar's 1909 book popularised the last name.1
  • Violence on both sides: rebels killed British officers and civilians, including women and children; British reprisals hanged rebels, blew some from cannon and destroyed whole villages.1

The Illustrated London News, 31 October 1857

An engraving 'from a photograph' of sepoys at Lucknow, printed for British readers while the war was still being fought. Newspapers could not yet print photographs, so engravers copied them by hand.

Reading it

Printed for British readers while the war was still being fought, this engraving was copied by hand 'from a photograph', since newspapers could not yet print photographs. Something is always lost or added in that copying. Indian accounts of 1857 tell a very different story from the one British readers were given.
