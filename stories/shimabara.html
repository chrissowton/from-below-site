The Shimabara Rebellion

Starving peasants, fishermen and masterless samurai, many of them secret Christians, rose against crushing taxes and persecution. They held a ruined castle for months against an army of 125,000.

When
December 1637 – April 1638
Where
Hara Castle, Shimabara Peninsula, Japan
Facts checked
2026-11-16

See it on the map →

01

What is the background to this event?

In the 1630s, the peasants of the Shimabara Peninsula and the Amakusa islands suffered from famine and overtaxation.1

  • The local lord, Matsukura Katsuie, raised taxes to build a new castle and violently persecuted Christians.1
  • The area had been ruled by a Christian clan, so many local people were Christians.1

In their own words

03

What happened?

In December 1637, peasants and rōnin, many of them Catholics, rose under the leadership of Amakusa Shirō.1

  • The rebels occupied the abandoned Hara Castle.1
  • The Tokugawa shogunate sent more than 125,000 troops, supported by Dutch ships.1
  • After a long siege, the castle fell in April 1638.1
  • Shirō and an estimated 37,000 rebels and sympathisers were beheaded.1
04

What happened next?

  • Punishment: Katsuie was investigated for misrule and beheaded — the only daimyō executed in this way in the Edo period.1
  • Seclusion: Portuguese traders were expelled, and Japan's policies of national seclusion and persecution of Christians were tightened until the 1850s.1
05

What do others say?

At the time

The shogunate presented the rising as a Christian rebellion.1

Debates since

  • Religion or misrule? The academic consensus holds that it was mainly driven by peasant anger at misgovernment, with Christians joining later.1
  • Not only peasants: rōnin, fishermen, craftsmen and merchants also took part.1
  • Violence: rebels killed local officials and attacked castles; the shogunate killed almost all the defenders of Hara Castle.1
06

The evidence

  • Shogunate records and the account of Yamada Emosaku, a rebel who survived.
  • Dutch East India Company records.

What is it?
Object: a banner
Made by, and when
Shimabara and Amakusa, 1637

A banner said to have been carried by the rebels under Amakusa Shirō. It shows a chalice and two kneeling angels, Catholic symbols at a time when Christianity was banned in Japan. It is attributed to Yamada Emosaku, a rebel who survived the siege of Hara Castle.

Things worth asking

  • Why might Christian symbols have been dangerous to carry in 1637?

07

How is it remembered?

  • Hara Castle is part of the UNESCO World Heritage Site of Hidden Christian Sites in the Nagasaki Region.
08

Why does it matter?

Shimabara was the largest civil conflict of the Edo period.1

  • It shows how economic hardship and religious persecution combined to drive ordinary people to revolt.

Open questions

09

What other events are like this?

  • 1488The Kaga Rebellion: 'A Province Held by Peasants' — Peasants, monks and local warriors united by their Buddhist faith overthrew the governor of Kaga and governed the province themselves for almost a century.
  • 1894The Donghak Peasant Revolution — Korean peasants inspired by a new religion rose against corrupt officials, briefly governed their province through peasant councils, and then fought the Japanese army.
  • 1419The Hussite Revolution — After the reformer Jan Hus was burned at the stake, Czech townspeople and peasants rose against the Church and the Emperor. Radicals founded a town where property was held in common, and peasant armies defeated five crusades.
