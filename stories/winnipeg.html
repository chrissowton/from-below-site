The Winnipeg General Strike

For six weeks, 30,000 workers brought Canada's third-largest city to a halt to win the right to collective bargaining. It ended with 'Bloody Saturday' — and transformed Canadian politics.

When
15 May – 26 June 1919
Where
Winnipeg, Manitoba, Canada
Facts checked

01

What is the background to this event?

The end of the First World War brought hardship and anger across North America and Europe. In Winnipeg, then Canada's third-largest city:1

  • Wages were low, prices had risen sharply, and employment was unstable. Housing and health conditions were poor.
  • Returning soldiers found few jobs, and immigrants — especially those from Central and Eastern Europe — faced discrimination.
  • Inspired in part by the Russian Revolution, a meeting of western labour delegates in Calgary in March 1919 adopted radical resolutions, including a six-hour day, and proposed a new "One Big Union" of all workers.1

The immediate cause of the strike was the refusal of employers in the metal and building trades to recognise and negotiate with their workers' unions. When the Metal Trades Council and the Building Trades Council failed to win contracts, they went on strike on 1 and 2 May. The Winnipeg Trades and Labour Council called a vote on a general strike in support, and almost every union voted in favour.1

02

Who were the key people?

  • R. B. Russell

    A Scottish-born machinist and leading supporter of the One Big Union, sentenced to two years in prison after the strike.

  • J. S. Woodsworth

    A Methodist minister arrested for seditious libel for quoting the Bible in the strikers' newspaper. He later founded the Co-operative Commonwealth Federation.

  • Helen Armstrong

    A leader of the Women's Labour League, who organised women workers and was arrested several times.

  • Fred Dixon

    A labour politician and editor, who defended himself at his trial and was acquitted.

  • The Citizens' Committee of One Thousand

    The group of businessmen, professionals and politicians that organised opposition to the strike.

  • Mike Sokolowski

    A worker killed by the police on Bloody Saturday, 21 June 1919.

In their own words

03

What happened?

At 11 a.m. on Thursday 15 May 1919, virtually the entire working population of Winnipeg went on strike. Around 30,000 workers in the public and private sectors walked off their jobs — most of them not union members.1

  • Factories, shops, streetcars, telephones, the post office and deliveries of bread and milk stopped. Firefighters walked out, and the city police voted to support the strike, though they stayed on duty at the Strike Committee's request.
  • A Central Strike Committee, elected by the unions, organised the strike and allowed essential services, such as milk and bread deliveries, to continue. Delivery vehicles carried signs saying they were "permitted by authority of the Strike Committee".
  • Many returned soldiers marched in support of the strikers.

Opponents organised the Citizens' Committee of One Thousand, which accused the strikers of plotting revolution and blamed "alien" immigrants.1 The authorities responded:1

  • The city dismissed almost the entire police force, which refused to sign a pledge not to join the strike, and replaced it with special constables armed with clubs.
  • The federal government rushed through an amendment to the Immigration Act to allow British-born immigrants to be deported without trial if charged with sedition.
  • In the early hours of 17 June, the Royal North-West Mounted Police arrested ten strike leaders.

On Saturday 21 June, strike supporters, many of them veterans, gathered outside City Hall for a silent parade in protest against the arrests. The mayor read the Riot Act. Mounted police charged the crowd and then fired three volleys from their revolvers. Mike Sokolowski was killed on the spot, and another man died later of his wounds; many more were injured. Soldiers with machine guns took control of the streets.1

The day became known as "Bloody Saturday". Facing defeat, the Strike Committee called off the strike, and workers returned to their jobs on 26 June 1919.1

04

What happened next?

  • Trials: seven strike leaders were convicted of seditious conspiracy; R. B. Russell was sentenced to two years in prison. Fred Dixon, who defended himself, was acquitted, and the charges against J. S. Woodsworth were dropped.
  • Political change: in the Manitoba election of 1920, several strike leaders were elected to the legislature, some while still in prison. In 1921 Woodsworth was elected to the federal parliament; in 1932 he became the first leader of the Co-operative Commonwealth Federation, forerunner of today's New Democratic Party.
  • Collective bargaining: the right to union recognition and collective bargaining — the strikers' central demand — was finally guaranteed in Canada in 1944.
05

Why does it matter?

The Winnipeg General Strike was the largest strike in Canadian history and one of the most important labour struggles in North America. For six weeks, workers ran their own city — and the reaction of business and government showed how far they would go to stop them.

  • It was a struggle for the basic right of workers to organise and bargain collectively.
  • It helped to give birth to Canada's democratic socialist and labour politics.
  • The use of immigration law against strikers is a reminder of how fear of "foreigners" can be used against workers' movements.

What do others say?How it was seen at the time, and where historians disagree

At the time

The Citizens' Committee, most newspapers and the federal government claimed that the strike was an attempted Bolshevik revolution, led by "alien" agitators. The strikers insisted that they were fighting only for collective bargaining and a living wage.

Historians' debates

  • Revolution or industrial dispute? Historians such as David Bercuson argue that the strike was essentially about collective bargaining, not revolution; others stress the radical ideas of some leaders and the wider "labour revolt" of 1919.
  • Who was to blame for the violence? Most historians argue that the federal and civic authorities, encouraged by the Citizens' Committee, deliberately provoked and broke the strike.
  • Women and immigrants: recent historians have recovered the role of women such as Helen Armstrong and of immigrant workers, who were often blamed but rarely heard.

How is it remembered?Memorials, anniversaries and arguments over memory
  • A sculpture commemorating the strike stands near Winnipeg's City Hall, and walking tours follow the events of 1919.
  • The musical film Stand! (2019) and the play Strike! The Musical retold the story for its centenary.
  • The strike is taught as a key event in Canadian history, and is commemorated by the labour movement every year.
