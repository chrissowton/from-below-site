The Kanak Struggle: 'Les Événements'

The Indigenous Kanak people of New Caledonia boycotted elections and set up a provisional government. Four years of violence on all sides ended in a historic peace agreement — and a debate about independence that continues.

When
1984 – 1988
Where
New Caledonia (Kanaky)

See it on the map →

01

What is the background to this event?

France annexed New Caledonia, in the south-west Pacific, in 1853. The Indigenous Melanesian people, the Kanak, were dispossessed of much of their land, confined to reservations and excluded from the colonial economy. A revolt led by the High Chief Ataï in 1878 was crushed; around 200 French settlers and 1,000 Kanak were killed.2 The Kanak population fell from around 60,000 in 1878 to 27,100 in 1921.2

  • New Caledonia also became a penal colony, and from the late nineteenth century a major producer of nickel. Settlers from France (Caldoches), and migrants from Wallis and Futuna, Asia and elsewhere, made the Kanak a minority in their own land.2
  • In the 1970s, Kanak activists, inspired by decolonisation across the Pacific, began to demand independence and the return of land.
  • In 1983, a French proposal for "enlarged autonomy" promised a referendum in 1989, but many Kanak feared that settlers and migrants would outvote them.2
02

Who were the key people?

  • Jean-Marie Tjibaou

    A former Catholic priest and ethnologist, leader of the Kanak and Socialist National Liberation Front (FLNKS). He signed the Matignon Accords in 1988 and was assassinated in 1989.

  • Éloi Machoro

    An FLNKS leader who famously smashed a ballot box with an axe in 1984. He was shot dead by French gendarmerie marksmen in January 1985.

  • Yeiwéné Yeiwéné

    Tjibaou's deputy, assassinated with him in 1989.

  • Jacques Lafleur

    Leader of the anti-independence party RPCR, who signed the Matignon Accords with Tjibaou.

  • Alphonse Dianou

    The leader of the group that took gendarmes hostage on Ouvéa in 1988, who died after the French assault.

  • Michel Rocard

    The French prime minister who negotiated the Matignon Accords.

03

What happened?

The FLNKS was founded in 1984 to unite the independence movement.1 In November 1984, it boycotted territorial elections, set up roadblocks across the territory and, in December, proclaimed a provisional government of Kanaky with Jean-Marie Tjibaou as president.2 The period of conflict that followed, known as les Événements (the Events), saw violence on all sides:

  • On 5 December 1984, settlers ambushed a group of Kanak activists near Hienghène, killing ten, including two of Tjibaou's brothers. The killers were later acquitted on grounds of self-defence.
  • Kanak activists occupied farms, and there were attacks on settlers and their property; settlers formed armed groups.
  • In January 1985, Éloi Machoro was shot dead by French gendarmerie marksmen.
  • The Socialist government in Paris offered a form of sovereignty in 1985, but a centre-right government elected in 1986 reversed many of the reforms and redistributed land with little regard for Kanak claims.2 The FLNKS boycotted a 1987 referendum on independence.1

The violence peaked in April 1988, on the island of Ouvéa. A group of FLNKS militants led by Alphonse Dianou attacked a gendarmerie, killing four gendarmes and taking others hostage in a cave.1 The French government refused to negotiate. On 5 May, special forces stormed the cave: all the hostages were freed, two soldiers were killed, and nineteen Kanak militants died.1 There were later allegations that some militants were killed after they had surrendered.1

04

What happened next?

  • The Matignon Accords: in June 1988, Tjibaou and Lafleur signed the Matignon Accords, negotiated by Michel Rocard, which provided for a ten-year period of development, the rebalancing of the economy towards the Kanak-majority provinces, an amnesty, and a future referendum.
  • Assassination: on 4 May 1989, at a ceremony on Ouvéa, Tjibaou and Yeiwéné were assassinated by Djubelly Wéa, an FLNKS militant who saw the accords as a betrayal.
  • The Nouméa Accord: in 1998, a further agreement recognised Kanak identity and the "shadows" of colonisation, transferred powers to New Caledonia, and provided for up to three referendums on independence.
  • Referendums: majorities voted against independence in 2018 and 2020. The third referendum, in 2021, was boycotted by the independence movement and produced a large "No" vote, whose legitimacy the FLNKS rejected.
  • 2024: a French plan to change who could vote in provincial elections sparked serious unrest in May 2024, in which more than a dozen people were killed.
05

What do others say?

At the time

Anti-independence parties and the French right described the FLNKS as terrorists and stressed attacks on settlers and gendarmes. The independence movement described a colonial system defended by force and by armed settlers, and pointed to the Hienghène ambush and the killings at Ouvéa.

Historians' and commentators' debates

  • Violence on all sides: the period saw killings of Kanak activists by settlers and police, killings of gendarmes and settlers by Kanak militants, and the deaths at Ouvéa, where allegations of extrajudicial killings by French forces have never been fully resolved. Each community has its own martyrs.
  • The accords: some see the Matignon and Nouméa accords as a model of reconciliation; others argue that they delayed independence and entrenched the interests of settlers and nickel companies.
  • Who should vote? The question of who has the right to vote on New Caledonia's future — only long-term residents, or all French citizens living there — lies at the heart of the conflict, and was the trigger for the unrest of 2024.
06

The evidence

  • The writings and speeches of Jean-Marie Tjibaou and other Kanak leaders.
  • French government records, court records and the reports of inquiries into Ouvéa.
  • Testimonies of survivors from all communities, and the film L'Ordre et la Morale (2011), directed by Mathieu Kassovitz, about Ouvéa.
07

How is it remembered?

  • The Jean-Marie Tjibaou Cultural Centre in Nouméa, designed by Renzo Piano, opened in 1998 as a centre for Kanak culture.
  • The dead of Hienghène and Ouvéa are commemorated by Kanak communities; the gendarmes and soldiers killed at Ouvéa are commemorated in France.
  • The Kanaky flag is flown by the independence movement and its supporters across the Pacific.
08

Why does it matter?

The Kanak struggle is one of the most important Indigenous independence movements in the Pacific. It forced France to recognise the Kanak people, their identity and their claim to self-determination, and produced, in the Matignon and Nouméa accords, a rare example of a negotiated path out of colonial conflict.

  • It shows how Indigenous people can become a minority in their own land through colonial settlement.
  • The accords are studied as a model of conflict resolution — though their outcome remains contested.
  • The question of New Caledonia's future is still unresolved.

Questions to think about

  1. Why did the FLNKS boycott elections and referendums?
  2. How should the violence of 1984–88 be remembered by communities with very different experiences of it?
  3. Why was Jean-Marie Tjibaou assassinated by a member of his own movement?
  4. Who should have the right to decide the future of a territory with a colonial history?
