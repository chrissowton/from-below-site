LGBTQ+ liberation

The Stonewall Uprising

A routine police raid on a gay bar met resistance, and several nights of protest gave rise to the modern LGBTQ+ liberation movement.

When
28 June – early July 1969
Where
Stonewall Inn, Greenwich Village, New York City
Facts checked

01

What is the background to this event?

In the United States of the 1950s and 1960s, gay men, lesbians and transgender people faced discrimination at almost every level of society:

  • Sexual relations between people of the same sex were criminal offences in almost every state.
  • Federal employees could be dismissed on the grounds of homosexuality; a 1950 Senate report asserted that "sex perverts in Government constitute security risks".
  • In New York, the State Liquor Authority treated the presence of gay customers as grounds for closing a bar, so many gay bars were run by organised crime, which paid off the police.
  • Police raids, entrapment and arrests for "masquerading" in clothing of the opposite sex were common.

Resistance had already begun. "Homophile" organisations such as the Mattachine Society and the Daughters of Bilitis campaigned for acceptance; in 1966 activists staged a "Sip-In" in New York to challenge the liquor rules; and in the same year transgender women and drag queens fought back against police at Compton's Cafeteria in San Francisco.

The Stonewall Inn on Christopher Street was a Mafia-owned bar without a liquor licence. It was one of the few places where gay people could dance, and it drew a mixed crowd, including young people who had been thrown out of their homes, drag queens and people of colour.

02

Who were the key people?

  • Marsha P. Johnson

    A Black transgender activist and well-known figure in Greenwich Village. Contemporary accounts place her among those 'in the vanguard' of the pushback against the police.

  • Sylvia Rivera

    A Latina transgender activist who was present during the uprising and later fought for the inclusion of trans people and the poorest in the gay liberation movement.

  • Stormé DeLarverie

    A butch lesbian performer whose struggle with police during the raid is often credited with sparking the resistance.

  • Zazu Nova and Jackie Hormona

    Named with Johnson as being among the first to resist the police.

  • Craig Rodwell

    An activist who distributed thousands of leaflets the morning after the first night and later helped organise the first Pride march.

  • The street kids of Christopher Street

    Young, often homeless gay and trans people who were among the most determined of those resisting the police.

In their own words

03

What happened?

In the early hours of Saturday 28 June 1969, police officers raided the Stonewall Inn. As usual, they checked identification and arrested employees and people dressed in clothing of the opposite sex. This time, however, many patrons refused to cooperate, and a crowd gathered outside instead of dispersing.1

Accounts describe the crowd's mood shifting from humour to anger:1

  • Someone shouted "Gay power!", and others began singing "We Shall Overcome".
  • When someone said the bar had been raided because "they didn't pay off the cops", another person yelled, "Let's pay them off!" — and coins were thrown at the police.
  • The police, outnumbered, barricaded themselves inside the bar as the crowd threw bottles and bricks.

The New York Police Department's Tactical Patrol Force was sent in to clear the streets. The crowd responded with defiance and ridicule, forming kick lines in front of the riot police. Inspector Seymour Pine, who led the raid, later recalled that "fights erupted with the transvestites, who wouldn't go into the patrol wagon."1 Sylvia Rivera remembered "howling all through the streets, 'The revolution is here!'"2

Protests continued on the following nights. Graffiti appeared on the walls of the bar reading "Drag power", "They invaded our rights" and "Legalize gay bars".1 The morning after the first night, Craig Rodwell and Fred Sargeant printed and distributed 5,000 leaflets, one of which read:

"Get the Mafia and the Cops out of Gay Bars."Leaflet distributed in Greenwich Village, 28 June 19691

Even the established homophile organisations recognised that something had changed. The Mattachine Society's newsletter headlined its report: "The Hairpin Drop Heard Around the World."1

04

What happened next?

  • New organisations: within weeks, activists founded the Gay Liberation Front, which aligned itself with other radical movements of the time, followed by the Gay Activists Alliance. Similar groups soon formed in Britain and elsewhere.
  • The first Pride: on 28 June 1970, the first anniversary of the raid, thousands marched from Greenwich Village to Central Park in the Christopher Street Liberation Day march. Marches were also held in Los Angeles and Chicago.
  • Trans activism: Marsha P. Johnson and Sylvia Rivera founded Street Transvestite Action Revolutionaries (STAR) in 1970 to house and support homeless trans youth.
  • Recognition: in 2016 President Obama designated the Stonewall National Monument, the first US national monument dedicated to LGBTQ+ history.
05

What do others say?

At the time

Police and much of the mainstream press presented the events as a riot by disorderly people; the New York Daily News ran a mocking headline. Some established homophile activists were uncomfortable with the rioting, fearing it would damage their efforts to present gay people as respectable.

Historians' debates

  • Who threw the first brick? Popular accounts often credit specific individuals, such as Marsha P. Johnson or Sylvia Rivera. Historians, including David Carter, have found the evidence for "firsts" uncertain; Johnson herself said she arrived after the rioting had started.
  • Was it the beginning? Many historians stress that Stonewall was preceded by earlier acts of resistance, such as the Compton's Cafeteria riot, and that its symbolic importance owes much to the Pride marches that commemorated it.
  • Whose story? There are ongoing debates about the erasure of trans people and people of colour, for example in the 2015 film Stonewall, which was widely criticised.

  • Very few photographs exist from the first night.

07

How is it remembered?

  • Pride marches are held each June around the world.
  • The Stonewall Inn was listed on the National Register of Historic Places in 1999 and became a National Monument in 2016.
  • Statues by George Segal, Gay Liberation, stand in Christopher Park opposite the Inn.
  • The National Park Service's removal of the "T" from "LGBT" on its website in 2025 provoked protests.
08

Why does it matter?

Stonewall was not the first act of resistance by gay and trans people, but it became the symbolic starting point of a mass movement for LGBTQ+ liberation. It is also a reminder that those with the least protection — homeless young people, trans women, people of colour — were often at the forefront.

  • Pride marches held around the world each June commemorate the uprising.
  • It marked a shift from appeals for tolerance to open demands for liberation and equal rights.
  • Debates about whose story Stonewall tells, and about the role of trans people within the movement, continue to shape LGBTQ+ politics today.

Questions to think about

  1. Why do you think some homophile activists were uncomfortable with the riots?
  2. Why do 'firsts' — who threw the first brick — matter so much in popular memory?
  3. How has Pride changed since the first march in 1970?
  4. Whose stories are most at risk of being left out of LGBTQ+ history?
