After Milan: Deaf People Defend Sign Language

In 1880, a congress of hearing teachers voted to banish sign language from deaf schools. That same year, deaf Americans founded their own association — and its leaders later filmed signers so their language would not be lost.

Where

01

What is the background to this event?

In the 19th century, teachers of deaf children argued over whether to teach through sign language or only through speech and lip-reading ('oralism').1

  • In the United States, Alexander Graham Bell championed oralism, while Edward Miner Gallaudet supported sign language.1
  • In 1878, a congress in Paris, organised by supporters of oralism, resolved that sign language should be only an "auxiliary teaching tool".1

In their own words

03

What happened?

From 6 to 11 September 1880, the Second International Congress on Education of the Deaf met in Milan.1

  • The organisers, strong supporters of oralism, chose who was invited. More than half were known oralists, and only between one and three delegates were deaf.1
  • Nine of the twelve speakers argued for oralism. Only the Gallaudet brothers and the English teacher Richard Elliot spoke for sign language.1
  • The congress voted 160 to 4 that speech was superior to signs, and resolved that the 'pure oral method' should be used. In practice, this meant banning sign language from schools.1
  • The same year, deaf Americans founded the National Association of the Deaf in Cincinnati — an organisation run by deaf people to defend their rights.3
  • Under George Veditz, the association used the new technology of film to record people signing, so that their language would never be lost.3
04

What happened next?

  • Banished from schools: after Milan, schools in Europe and America largely switched to speech-only teaching until the late 1960s and early 1970s.1
  • An apology: in 2010, the International Congress on Education of the Deaf, meeting in Vancouver, formally apologised for the harm the ban had caused.1
  • Renounced: in 2025, the congress "fully and unequivocally" renounced the Milan resolutions.1
  • Exclusion within: the National Association of the Deaf itself did not let women vote until 1964 or accept Black members until 1965.3
05

Why does it matter?

Milan shows what happens when decisions about a group are made without them. The deaf community's answer — organising for itself and preserving its language on film — is an early example of disabled people speaking for themselves.13

  • Films the National Association of the Deaf made from 1913 survive, preserving the signing of a century ago.3

What do others say?How it was seen at the time, and where historians disagree

At the time

The congress declared the "incontestable superiority" of speech in 'restoring' deaf people to society; the Gallaudet brothers and Richard Elliot spoke for the use of sign language.1

Debates since

  • A rigged congress? The historian Harlan Lane describes Milan as "a brief rally by and for opponents of manual language".1
  • The harm: the 2025 renunciation spoke of the "language deprivation" suffered by generations of deaf children.1

Reading it

In this 1913 film, George Veditz signs a defence of the language the Milan congress had tried to banish. Film was the only technology that could record a language made of movement. Veditz was speaking to the future — to deaf people who might otherwise never see how their language had been used.
