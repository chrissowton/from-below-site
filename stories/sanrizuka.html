The Sanrizuka Struggle against Narita Airport

When the government chose their farmland for Tokyo's new airport without asking them, farmers and student radicals fought for decades. Police and protesters died, and the airport opened years late — with one runway.

When
1966 – 1991
Where
Sanrizuka, Chiba Prefecture, Japan

01

What is the background to this event?

In the 1960s, Japan's government decided that Tokyo needed a new international airport.

  • In 1966, after protests at another proposed site, the government chose Sanrizuka, an area of farms and the imperial family's former ranch, without consulting most of the residents.1
  • Many families had farmed the land for generations, and some had cleared it after the Second World War.1
  • The region had a long tradition of farmers' movements and defiance of authority.1

03

What happened?

In 1966, local farmers formed an opposition union, at first with the support of the Socialist and Communist parties.1

  • They bought tiny plots of land within the airport site so that many people would have to be expropriated.1
  • New Left student groups joined the farmers, building fortified towers and tunnels.
  • At its height, the movement mobilised 17,500 people for a rally.1

When the government began expropriating land in 1971, violence escalated.1

  • On 16 September 1971, three police officers were killed by rioters.1
  • Days later, riot police removed an elderly woman, Yone Koizumi, and demolished her house.1
  • On 1 October 1971, Fumio Sannomiya, a young farmer in the movement, took his own life, leaving a note saying he hated those who brought the airport.1
  • In March 1978, days before the planned opening, radicals stormed the airport and destroyed equipment in the control tower, delaying the opening.1

04

What happened next?

  • Opening: Narita airport opened in May 1978 with one runway, under heavy security.
  • Reconciliation: in the early 1990s, government-farmer round-table talks were held, and the government apologised for its handling of the airport's planning. Some farmers reached agreements; others refused.
  • Holdouts: a few farmers still live and farm inside or beside the airport, and the second runway was shortened to avoid their land.
05

What do others say?

At the time

The government saw the airport as essential to Japan's economic future. The farmers saw it as theft of their land; the New Left saw it as part of a struggle against the state and US imperialism.

Debates since

  • Violence: police officers and protesters died, and later radical groups carried out bombings and attacks. Many farmers distanced themselves from the violence, while others argued it had been forced on them.
  • Government responsibility: the government later acknowledged that its failure to consult was a major cause of the conflict.
  • Farmers and radicals: historians debate how far the New Left helped or harmed the farmers' cause.

08

Why does it matter?

The Sanrizuka struggle is one of the longest and most bitter protest movements in post-war Japan. It raises fundamental questions about who decides how land is used.

  • It shows the consequences of decisions taken without consulting those affected.
  • It also shows how a farmers' movement could be overtaken by violent groups with other agendas.

Questions to think about
