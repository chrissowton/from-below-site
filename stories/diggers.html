The Diggers of St George's Hill

In the year the king was executed, a small group of poor men and women began to dig and plant common land, declaring the earth 'a common treasury for all'.

When
1 April 1649 – April 1650
Where
St George's Hill, Weybridge, Surrey, England

See it on the map →

01

What is the background to this event?

By 1649 England had been through seven years of civil war. Parliament had defeated Charles I, and in January 1649 the king was tried and executed. For a brief period, the question of who should hold power — and on what principles — was wide open.

For the poor, the war years had been extremely hard:

  • A series of bad harvests in the late 1640s pushed bread prices to their highest levels for decades.
  • Taxation and the quartering of soldiers weighed heavily on ordinary households.
  • The long process of enclosure — the fencing off of common land for private use by landowners — had removed many villagers' rights to graze animals, gather fuel and grow food.

Radical ideas flourished. In the Putney Debates of 1647, the Leveller-supporting officer Thomas Rainsborough had argued that "the poorest he that is in England hath a life to live, as the greatest he", and that every man should have a voice in choosing the government. The Levellers called for a wide male franchise and religious toleration. A smaller group went further, arguing that true freedom required equal access to the land itself.

02

Who were the key people?

  • Gerrard Winstanley

    A former cloth merchant from Wigan, ruined in the depression of the 1640s and working as a cowherd in Surrey. He was the movement's chief writer and theorist.

  • William Everard

    A former soldier and co-leader of the Diggers in their first weeks.

  • The diggers themselves

    A few dozen labourers, men and women, many of them local, who dug, manured and planted the common land and built shelters there.

  • Francis Drake

    Lord of the manor and MP (not the Elizabethan seafarer), who organised the harassment of the St George's Hill colony.

  • Parson John Platt

    Lord of the manor of Cobham, who led the campaign to drive the Diggers off Little Heath in 1650.

  • Lord Fairfax

    Lord General of Parliament's army, who met the Diggers twice and judged them harmless, but did not protect them.

03

What happened?

On 1 April 1649, a small group of men and women began to dig and sow vegetables on the common land of St George's Hill, near Weybridge in Surrey. They invited others to join them, and a local informant, Henry Sanders, reported to the Council of State that they had promised "all to come in and help them" and would give them "meat, drink, and clothes".1

Later that month Winstanley and his companions published their manifesto, The True Levellers Standard Advanced, which opens:

"In the beginning of Time, the great Creator Reason made the Earth a Common Treasury, to preserve Beasts, Birds, Fishes, and Man, the lord that was to govern this Creation."Gerrard Winstanley and others, The True Levellers Standard Advanced, April 16492

The Diggers rejected violence and did not attempt to seize enclosed land; they argued only that the common and waste land should be cultivated freely by the poor. The response of local landowners was nevertheless hostile:1

  • The lord of the manor, Francis Drake, organised gangs who beat the Diggers and set fire to one of their communal houses.
  • The Diggers were taken to court, where they were not allowed to speak in their own defence, and were found guilty.
  • In August 1649 they abandoned St George's Hill and moved to Little Heath, near Cobham.

Other colonies were founded, inspired by the Surrey example, at Wellingborough in Northamptonshire, Iver in Buckinghamshire and elsewhere.1 In March 1650 emissaries carrying a letter from the Surrey Diggers encouraging new colonies were arrested, and on 15 April 1650 the Council of State ordered proceedings against "the Levellers" in Northamptonshire.1 By April 1650 Parson John Platt and other local landowners had destroyed the Cobham colony, pulling down houses and driving the Diggers away.1

04

What happened next?

  • Winstanley's later work: in 1652 he published The Law of Freedom in a Platform, addressed to Oliver Cromwell, setting out a plan for a society without private property in land or wage labour. In it he wrote that "true freedom lies where a man receives his nourishment and preservation, and that is in the use of the earth."
  • Disappearance: after 1650 the Diggers vanished as an organised movement. Winstanley himself later became a corn chandler and a Quaker, and died in 1676.
  • Enclosure continued: over the following two centuries, and especially through the Enclosure Acts of 1750–1850, most remaining common land in England was enclosed.
05

What do others say?

At the time

Local landowners and clergy regarded the Diggers as a threat to property and order and accused them, falsely, of being "Ranters" given to sexual licence. The army leadership, after meeting them, considered them more eccentric than dangerous.

Historians' debates

  • Proto-communists or religious visionaries? Marxist historians, especially Christopher Hill, presented Winstanley as an early communist. Others, such as J.C. Davis, emphasise that his ideas were grounded in radical Protestant theology and millenarian expectation.
  • How significant were they? Some historians argue that a movement of a few dozen people has been given disproportionate attention because of later political sympathies; others, such as John Gurney, have shown how the Diggers drew on real local grievances in Surrey.
  • Winstanley's later life: his move into trade and parish office has been read by some as a retreat from radicalism.
06

The evidence

  • Winstanley's own pamphlets — about twenty were published between 1648 and 1652 — are the main source, and present the Diggers' own case.
  • Reports to the Council of State, such as Henry Sanders's, and Lord Fairfax's correspondence, give the view of the authorities.
  • Newsbooks (early newspapers) reported on the colony, often mockingly.
  • Manorial and court records from Surrey help historians identify individual Diggers.
07

How is it remembered?

  • Leon Rosselson's song "The World Turned Upside Down" (1975), later recorded by Billy Bragg and others, made the Diggers widely known.
  • Kevin Brownlow and Andrew Mollo's film Winstanley (1975) dramatised the story.
  • On 1 April 1999, the 350th anniversary, the land rights group The Land Is Ours occupied land on St George's Hill — now a private gated estate of expensive houses.
  • An annual Diggers' Festival has been held in Winstanley's home town of Wigan since 2011, and a memorial stone stands near Weybridge station.
08

Why does it matter?

The Diggers were few in number and their colonies lasted barely a year, yet their ideas have had a remarkably long life. They raised a question that remains central to radical politics: whether political freedom is meaningful without economic freedom, and in particular without access to land.

  • Winstanley is often regarded as one of the earliest English advocates of common ownership, and as a forerunner of later socialist, anarchist and environmental thought.
  • The Diggers' non-violent direct action anticipated later land occupations and protest camps.
  • Their story shows how, in moments of upheaval, ordinary people can imagine radically different ways of organising society.

Questions to think about

  1. Can people be politically free if they do not have access to land or a livelihood?
  2. Why did the Diggers choose to dig common land rather than seize enclosed fields?
  3. Why do you think a small movement that lasted a year has been remembered for so long?
  4. Who owns land in your area today, and who has the right to use it?

What other events are like this?

  • 1932The Kinder Scout Mass Trespass — Hundreds of young working-class walkers from Manchester deliberately trespassed on a private grouse moor to demand the right to roam.
  • 1966The Wave Hill Walk-Off — Gurindji stockmen and their families walked off a British-owned cattle station. What began as a strike over pay became the foundation of the Aboriginal land rights movement.
  • 1974The Chipko Movement — Village women in the Himalayas placed themselves between the loggers and the forest, and forced a ban on commercial felling.
