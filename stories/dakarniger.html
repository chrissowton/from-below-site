The Dakar–Niger Railway Strike

African railway workers in French West Africa held out for more than five months to win equal treatment with their European colleagues.

When
10 October 1947 – 19 March 1948
Where
Thiès, Dakar and the Dakar–Niger line, French West Africa

See it on the map →

01

What is the background to this event?

The Dakar–Niger railway linked the port of Dakar in Senegal with Koulikoro on the River Niger, in what is now Mali, through the railway town of Thiès and the cities of Kayes and Bamako. It was the backbone of the French colonial economy in West Africa, carrying groundnuts and other goods to the coast.1

The railway's workforce was divided by race:

  • European workers enjoyed permanent status, higher wages and family allowances. Most African workers were "auxiliaries", with lower pay and fewer rights, even when they did the same jobs.2
  • The colonial workforce had been built with the help of forced labour, and wages had fallen during the Depression and the Second World War, when food shortages were severe.2
  • After the war, France's new constitution of 1946 abolished forced labour and made colonial subjects citizens of the "French Union", raising hopes of equality.

A general strike in Dakar in 1945–46, led by teachers and metalworkers, had already won wage increases and recognition of trade-union rights. In 1946 railway workers struck again. They obtained an agreement, but in August 1947 the railway administration (the Régie) changed the new collective agreement unilaterally.2

02

Who were the key people?

  • Ibrahima Sarr

    Secretary-general of the African railway workers' federation and leader of the strike. He was sentenced to a month in prison and a heavy fine during the strike, and later became a politician.

  • The railway workers

    Some 20,000 African railway workers in Senegal, French Sudan (Mali), Guinea and Côte d'Ivoire, and their families.

  • The women of Thiès and Dakar

    Wives, mothers and market traders who supported the strikers with food and money.

  • Paul Béchard

    The socialist governor-general appointed in 1948 to settle the conflict.

  • Ousmane Sembène

    A Senegalese docker and later writer and film-maker, whose novel about the strike made it famous.

03

What happened?

The unions called a strike from 10 October 1947 to demand a single status for all railway workers — the cadre unique — without distinction of race, as well as family allowances and better pay.2

The strike was massive:2

  • The four railway networks of French West Africa were brought to a halt, and port workers on the wharves joined the movement, stopping the loading and unloading of goods.
  • On 1 November 1947, only 525 railway workers were not on strike — and only 38 of them were Africans.
  • The administration supported the Régie, which refused to negotiate. The strike was declared illegal, union offices were searched, and the union leader Ibrahima Sarr was sentenced to a month in prison and a fine of 12,000 francs.
  • The Régie hired 2,416 replacement workers. Twelve strikers were arrested and accused of sabotage after a fatal accident on the line, although the accident was blamed on a driver the company had re-hired.

The strikers organised daily general assemblies and strike committees, which distributed food and essential goods. Although Muslim religious leaders opposed the strike, the wider population supported the railway workers: traders gave food and money, and donations flowed into strike funds.2

After 160 days, 62 per cent of workers were still on strike. The French government appointed a new governor-general, Paul Béchard, to resolve the conflict, and the Régie was forced to negotiate. An agreement was signed on 16 March 1948:2

  • the strikers were not treated as having resigned, and sanctions were lifted;
  • the cadre unique was introduced, and 64 per cent of the auxiliaries were integrated into it;
  • wages and allowances were raised by 20 per cent, although strike days were not paid.

The railway workers returned to work on 19 March 1948.2 Some strike leaders were later dismissed or jailed.1

04

What happened next?

  • A labour code: unions across French West Africa continued to campaign, and in 1952 the French parliament passed an Overseas Labour Code that guaranteed a forty-hour week, paid holidays and, later, family allowances.
  • Unions and nationalism: the labour movement became closely linked to anti-colonial politics. In 1957 trade unionists across French West Africa formed the General Union of Black African Workers (UGTAN).
  • Independence: Senegal and Mali became independent in 1960. The railway itself declined in later decades and freight services were suspended in 2018.
05

What do others say?

At the time

The railway administration and colonial officials presented the strike as illegal and politically motivated, and accused strikers of sabotage. Some African politicians, including Léopold Sédar Senghor, were cautious in their support, while religious leaders opposed the strike.

Historians' debates

  • Labour or nationalism? Historian Frederick Cooper argues that the strikers' demand for equality within the French system was not simply nationalist: they used the promises of post-war French policy to win concrete gains. Others, such as the Senegalese historian Mar Fall, see the union movement as a form of anti-colonial political opposition.
  • Fiction and history: Sembène's novel gives a central role to the women of Thiès, including a famous march to Dakar. Historians have pointed out that the novel is fiction, and that the evidence for some events, such as the women's march, is limited — but also that the novel captured how the strike was remembered.
  • Who won? The strikers won the cadre unique and a pay rise, but not everyone was integrated into it, and some leaders lost their jobs.
06

The evidence

  • French colonial archives, including reports by the railway administration, police and labour inspectors.
  • Union records and newspapers, such as Réveil, the newspaper of the African Democratic Rally (RDA).
  • Oral testimony of former railway workers, collected by historians.
07

How is it remembered?

  • Ousmane Sembène's novel Les bouts de bois de Dieu (God's Bits of Wood, 1960) is one of the classics of African literature and is widely read in schools in West Africa.
  • Thiès is known in Senegal as the "city of the railway workers" and has a strong labour tradition.
  • Historians have noted that the strikes of 1945–48 are less well remembered than they deserve to be.
08

Why does it matter?

The Dakar–Niger strike was one of the longest and most successful strikes in colonial Africa. African workers used the French government's own language of equality and citizenship to demand equal pay and conditions — and won.

  • It showed the power of organised African workers, and of the communities that supported them.
  • It helped to build the trade-union and political movements that led to independence.
  • Through Ousmane Sembène's novel, it became one of the best-known stories of African resistance to colonialism.

Questions to think about

  1. Why was the demand for a 'single status' (cadre unique) so important to African workers?
  2. Why do you think the wider population supported the strike, even though it caused shortages?
  3. How can a novel help us understand a historical event? What are its limits as a source?
  4. Was the strike a labour dispute, an anti-colonial struggle, or both?

  • 1976The Grunwick Strike — South Asian women workers walked out of a photo-processing factory in north London and became the centre of one of the biggest industrial disputes of the 1970s.
