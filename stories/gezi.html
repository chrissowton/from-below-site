The Gezi Park Protests

A sit-in to save the trees of one of Istanbul's last green spaces grew into nationwide protests involving millions of people against the government of Recep Tayyip Erdoğan.

When
28 May – June 2013
Where
Gezi Park and Taksim Square, Istanbul, Turkey

See it on the map →

01

What is the background to this event?

By 2013, Recep Tayyip Erdoğan's Justice and Development Party (AKP) had governed Turkey for ten years. The economy had grown rapidly, but many people were increasingly concerned:1

  • Since 2011, the government had increased restrictions on freedom of speech, the press, the internet and the right to assemble. Critical media groups faced heavy fines, and many journalists were imprisoned.
  • Secular Turks feared an Islamist agenda, including new restrictions on alcohol.
  • Huge construction projects were transforming Istanbul, often with little consultation, and environmental protests had grown across the country.

Gezi Park, beside Taksim Square in the centre of Istanbul, is one of the few green spaces left in the city centre. The government planned to pedestrianise Taksim and to rebuild on the park the Ottoman-era Taksim Military Barracks, demolished in 1940. The rebuilt barracks were expected to house a shopping centre.1

02

Who were the key people?

  • The Taksim Solidarity platform

    A coalition of architects, urban planners, environmentalists and residents' groups that organised the original sit-in.

  • Ceyda Sungur

    A young academic photographed being sprayed with tear gas at close range in a red dress. The 'woman in red' became an emblem of the protests.

  • Erdem Gündüz

    A performance artist who began the 'standing man' protest by standing silently in Taksim Square for hours.

  • Berkin Elvan

    A fifteen-year-old boy hit on the head by a tear-gas canister in Istanbul in June 2013 while going to buy bread. He died after 269 days in a coma.

  • Osman Kavala

    A businessman and philanthropist who was later accused of organising the protests and sentenced to life imprisonment.

03

What happened?

On 27–28 May 2013, a small group of environmentalists set up tents in Gezi Park to stop bulldozers uprooting the trees. Police used tear gas to disperse them and burned their tents. Images of the police action — including the photograph of Ceyda Sungur, the "woman in red" — spread quickly on social media.1

On 31 May, police raided the camp again with water cannon and tear gas. The response was enormous:1

  • Tens of thousands of people poured into the streets of Istanbul. On 1 June, police withdrew from Taksim Square, and the park became a protest camp with a library, a medical centre, a kitchen and a garden.
  • Protests spread across Turkey. An estimated three and a half million people took part in almost 5,000 demonstrations in nearly every province.
  • On 2 June, Erdoğan dismissed the protesters as "a few looters" (çapulcu). Protesters adopted the word with pride, and coined the term "chapulling" to mean fighting for your rights.
  • The protesters were remarkably diverse: environmentalists, secularists, Kurds, Alevis, feminists, LGBT activists, anti-capitalist Muslims and rival football supporters stood side by side.

The police response was harsh, with mass use of tear gas, water cannon and plastic bullets. On 15 June, riot police cleared the Gezi Park camp. Afterwards, protesters held public forums in parks around Turkey, and on 17 June Erdem Gündüz began the silent "standing man" protest in Taksim Square, which was copied by hundreds of others.1 Over the course of the protests, at least eleven people were killed and more than 8,000 injured, and over 3,000 people were arrested.1

04

What happened next?

  • The park saved: an Istanbul court suspended the barracks project in 2013, and Gezi Park was not built on.
  • Prosecutions: many protesters, doctors who treated the injured and journalists were prosecuted. In 2022, Osman Kavala was sentenced to aggravated life imprisonment for allegedly organising the protests, and seven others to eighteen years each. The European Court of Human Rights had already ruled that Kavala's detention violated his rights.
  • Erdoğan's power: Erdoğan became President in 2014, and a referendum in 2017 created a powerful executive presidency.
  • A generation: the protests shaped a generation of activists, and their memory remains strong in Turkish politics and culture.
05

What do others say?

At the time

The government and pro-government media described the protests as the work of looters, terrorists and foreign powers seeking to overthrow an elected government, and pointed to the AKP's continuing electoral support. Protesters and human rights organisations described a peaceful movement met with excessive force.

Commentators' debates

  • Trees or politics? Commentators debate how far Gezi was about the environment and urban space, and how far it was a broader revolt against the government's authoritarianism.
  • Leaderless movements: Zeynep Tufekci argues that social media allowed huge numbers to mobilise quickly without organisations, but that such leaderless movements found it harder to negotiate or to turn protest into lasting political change.
  • Who protested? Surveys suggested protesters were mostly young, urban and educated; critics argued that this limited the movement's appeal to wider society.
06

The evidence

  • Photographs, videos and social media posts by protesters and journalists.
  • Reports by Amnesty International, Human Rights Watch and Turkish doctors' and lawyers' organisations.
  • Court records from the trials of protesters and of Osman Kavala and others.
07

How is it remembered?

  • Gezi Park still stands, and the anniversary of the protests is marked each year, although commemorations are often banned.
  • The images of the "woman in red" and the "standing man" have become symbols of peaceful protest.
  • Berkin Elvan is remembered as a symbol of the young victims of the protests.
08

Why does it matter?

The Gezi protests began as a small sit-in to save a few hundred trees, and became the largest wave of protest in Turkey for decades. They showed how a local environmental struggle can become a wider movement for democracy, freedom and the right to the city.

  • They brought together groups that had rarely cooperated, in a spirit of humour and creativity.
  • They are an important example of the "squares" movements of the early 2010s, alongside the Arab Spring, Occupy and the Indignados.
  • The prosecutions that followed raise serious questions about the right to protest.

Questions to think about

  1. How did a protest about trees become a nationwide movement?
  2. Why do you think protesters adopted the insult 'looters' as their own name?
  3. What are the strengths and weaknesses of protest movements without leaders?
  4. Should people be prosecuted for organising peaceful protests?

What other events are like this?

  • 1974The Chipko Movement — Village women in the Himalayas placed themselves between the loggers and the forest, and forced a ban on commercial felling.
  • 2010The Tunisian Revolution — A street vendor's desperate protest in a small town sparked a month of uprising that toppled a dictator of 23 years and began the Arab Spring.
  • 1932The Kinder Scout Mass Trespass — Hundreds of young working-class walkers from Manchester deliberately trespassed on a private grouse moor to demand the right to roam.
