The Cuban Revolution

Against the dictatorship of Fulgencio Batista, guerrillas in the mountains, students and workers in the cities fought a six-year struggle. On 1 January 1959, Batista fled. The revolution transformed Cuba — bringing literacy and land reform, but also executions, one-party rule and exile.

01

What is the background to this event?

Fulgencio Batista seized power in a coup on 10 March 1952, before elections due that June.1

  • His regime used censorship, torture and public executions against opponents. Estimates of the number killed under it range from hundreds to 20,000.2
  • Cuba's economy was dominated by sugar and closely tied to the United States.

03

What happened?

On 26 July 1953, Castro led a failed attack on the Moncada Barracks in Santiago; many of the attackers were killed after capture.3

  • The Sierra Maestra: in December 1956, 82 fighters landed from the yacht Granma. After heavy losses, the survivors built a guerrilla army in the Sierra Maestra mountains, with the help of women such as Celia Sánchez and Haydée Santamaría.3
  • The cities: in March 1957, students of the Directorio Revolucionario stormed the Presidential Palace in an attempt to kill Batista. In September, sailors rose at Cienfuegos.3
  • Victory: in late December 1958, Che Guevara's forces captured Santa Clara. Batista fled on 1 January 1959.3

04

What happened next?

  • Tribunals: supporters of Batista were tried by revolutionary tribunals; 176 executions are documented at La Cabaña fortress during Guevara's command in 1959.5
  • Literacy: in 1961, about 100,000 young volunteers went to the countryside to teach people to read.4
  • One-party rule: the new government nationalised industry, took control of the press and concentrated political power, alienating many Cubans and the United States.3
05

What do others say?

At the time

Batista's government met the rebels with force; in his defence speech, Castro argued for nationalism and for programmes to benefit ordinary Cubans.3

Debates since

  • Achievements and costs: supporters point to literacy, health care and independence from US domination; critics point to executions, political prisoners and the exile of many Cubans.3
  • Who made the revolution? Historians debate how far it was won by the guerrillas, and how far by urban movements, students and workers.
06

The evidence

  • Castro's defence speech 'History Will Absolve Me', guerrilla diaries, photographs, newspapers and the records of the revolutionary government.3

07

How is it remembered?

  • In Cuba, 1 January and 26 July are national days; among Cubans abroad, the revolution is remembered very differently.
08

Why does it matter?

The Cuban Revolution became a symbol for movements across Latin America and the world — and a point of fierce disagreement. It brought major gains in education and health, alongside the suppression of political freedom.3

Questions to think about

  • 1927Sandino's War against the US Marines — When Nicaragua's generals made peace under US supervision, one refused. Augusto César Sandino led a peasant army in a six-year guerrilla war against the US occupation.
  • 1944Guatemala's 'Ten Years of Spring' — Students, teachers and workers brought down a dictator. A decade of democracy and land reform followed — until a US-backed coup ended it.
