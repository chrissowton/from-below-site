The 8888 Uprising

Students, monks, workers and civil servants rose against a quarter-century of military rule. On 8.8.88 the whole country went on strike — until the army struck back.

When
March – September 1988
Where
Rangoon (Yangon) and across Burma (Myanmar)
Facts checked
2026-11-15

See it on the map →

01

What is the background to this event?

Since a military coup in 1962, Burma had been ruled by General Ne Win and his Burma Socialist Programme Party as a one-party state. Its "Burmese Way to Socialism" isolated the country and impoverished one of the richest nations in South-East Asia.1

  • Long civil wars with ethnic armed groups absorbed a large share of the national budget.1
  • In November 1985, the government suddenly declared 20, 50 and 100 kyat banknotes invalid, refunding only part of their value.1
  • On 5 September 1987, Ne Win cancelled the 25, 35 and 75 kyat notes without warning, wiping out most of the money in circulation. New 45 and 90 kyat notes were chosen because the numbers were divisible by nine, Ne Win's lucky number. Students' savings for tuition fees disappeared overnight.1
  • In December 1987 the United Nations classified Burma as a "Least Developed Country".
02

Who were the key people?

  • The students

    Students of the Rangoon Institute of Technology and Rangoon Arts and Sciences University, who began the protests. Many later fled to the borders and took up arms.

  • Phone Maw

    A student shot dead by riot police in March 1988, whose death sparked the movement.

  • Ne Win

    Burma's military ruler since 1962, who resigned as party chairman in July 1988 but kept power behind the scenes.

  • Sein Lwin

    The general who led the crackdown, known as 'the Butcher of Rangoon', briefly head of state in July–August 1988.

  • Aung San Suu Kyi

    The daughter of the independence hero Aung San, who emerged as the leader of the democracy movement in August 1988.

  • Min Ko Naing

    A student leader who became one of Burma's best-known political prisoners.

In their own words

03

What happened?

In March 1988, a brawl in a Rangoon teashop led to protests by students of the Rangoon Institute of Technology. Riot police were sent in, and a student, Phone Maw, was shot dead.1 Protests spread, and were met with further violence. On 23 July, Ne Win announced his resignation and promised a referendum on multi-party democracy — but warned that "when the army shoots, it shoots to kill". He handed power to Sein Lwin, the general most hated for repression.1

Students called for a nationwide general strike on 8 August 1988 — 8.8.88, a date chosen for its auspicious numbers.1 The movement spread far beyond the students:1

  • Hundreds of thousands of people — monks, schoolchildren, housewives, doctors, civil servants, even air force and navy personnel — marched in Rangoon, Mandalay and towns across the country.
  • Neighbourhood and strike committees took over the running of many districts.
  • From the night of 8 August, troops fired on crowds. The military put the death toll at about 95; other estimates run into the thousands.

Sein Lwin resigned on 12 August, and a civilian, Maung Maung, was appointed. But the protests continued, demanding an interim government. On 26 August, Aung San Suu Kyi addressed a crowd of half a million people at the Shwedagon Pagoda, calling for democracy and describing the movement as Burma's "second struggle for national independence".13

On 18 September, the army seized power directly, forming the State Law and Order Restoration Council (SLORC). Troops fired on demonstrators across the country. Thousands of deaths have been attributed to the military during the uprising; the authorities put the figure at around 350.1 Thousands of students fled to the border regions.

04

What happened next?

  • A stolen election: the military held elections in May 1990. Aung San Suu Kyi's National League for Democracy won 392 of 492 seats, but the junta refused to hand over power.
  • House arrest: Aung San Suu Kyi was placed under house arrest in 1989 and spent much of the next twenty years in detention. She was awarded the Nobel Peace Prize in 1991.
  • Armed resistance: many students joined the All Burma Students' Democratic Front and fought alongside ethnic armed groups.
  • A brief opening, then a coup: after reforms from 2011, the NLD won elections in 2015 and 2020. In February 2021 the army seized power again; activists compared the mass resistance that followed to 8888.
05

Why does it matter?

The 8888 Uprising was the largest popular challenge to military rule in Burma's history. People from every part of society joined together, organised their own committees and demanded democracy — and paid a terrible price.

  • It brought Aung San Suu Kyi into politics and created the democracy movement that shaped Burma for the next thirty years.
  • The "88 Generation" of student activists remained a powerful force, many spending decades in prison.
  • Its memory has inspired every later wave of resistance to military rule, including after the coup of 2021.

What do others say?How it was seen at the time, and where historians disagree

At the time

The military government blamed the unrest on communists, foreign agents and "destructive elements", and claimed that it had seized power to prevent anarchy. Protesters and foreign observers described peaceful demonstrations met with massacres.

Historians' and commentators' debates

  • How many died? The figures range from the government's few hundred to estimates of 3,000 or more; no independent count has ever been possible.
  • A divided movement? Some writers argue that divisions among opposition leaders, and their failure to form an interim government in August, gave the army the opportunity to strike.
  • Aung San Suu Kyi: once a global symbol of non-violent resistance, her reputation abroad was severely damaged when, as State Counsellor, she defended the army against accusations of genocide against the Rohingya in 2019. Her role in Burmese history remains intensely debated.
How do we know?The evidence, and a source from the time
  • Reports by foreign journalists and diplomats, including the BBC's Burmese service, which many Burmese listened to.
  • Photographs and video smuggled out of the country.
  • Testimonies of former students and political prisoners, collected by organisations such as the Assistance Association for Political Prisoners (Burma).
How is it remembered?Memorials, anniversaries and arguments over memory
  • The anniversary of 8 August is marked by Burmese communities and activists around the world.
  • Public commemoration inside Burma has been restricted or banned under military rule.
  • The numbers 8888 have become a symbol of resistance, used again in the protests against the 2021 coup.
