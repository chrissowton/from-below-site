22 May 1848: The Enslaved of Martinique Free Themselves

France had decreed the end of slavery, but the news had not arrived. When an enslaved drummer was jailed, thousands marched on Saint-Pierre — and forced the governor to declare abolition at once.

When
20 – 23 May 1848
Where
Saint-Pierre, Martinique

See it on the map →

01

What is the background to this event?

Martinique had been a French plantation colony since the 1600s, its sugar produced by enslaved Africans.1

  • The enslaved resisted for two centuries through revolts, escape, sabotage and culture.1
  • Slavery had been abolished in 1794 but never applied in Martinique, which was under British occupation; Napoleon kept it in 1802.1
  • On 27 April 1848, the new French Republic decreed the abolition of slavery, but the decree had not reached the island.12

03

What happened?

On 20 May 1848, Romain, an enslaved man from Le Prêcheur, was arrested for drumming and jailed in Saint-Pierre.1

  • On 22 May, thousands of enslaved men and women from the plantations converged on Saint-Pierre to free him.1
  • Houses of slaveholders and warehouses were set on fire, and the uprising spread through the north of the island; several people were killed and many injured.1
  • That afternoon, Governor Rostoland proclaimed the immediate abolition of slavery, without waiting for the decree.1

04

What happened next?

  • Freedom: 74,447 enslaved people in Martinique became free.1
  • Compensation: a law of 1849 compensated slave owners across the French colonies; the formerly enslaved received nothing.1
05

What do others say?

At the time

Planters feared disorder; the enslaved demanded freedom now.1

Debates since

  • Who freed whom? Martinicans chose 22 May to mark freedom won by the insurgents, not granted by the Schœlcher decree.1 In 2020, activists pulled down statues of Schœlcher, arguing that official memory had given him the credit.
  • Violence: slavery itself rested on violence; the insurrection involved burning and deaths, and the colonial militia tried and failed to put it down.1
06

The evidence

  • Colonial records and the governor's proclamation.1
07

How is it remembered?

  • 22 May is a public holiday in Martinique, recognised in French law in 1983 — chosen by Martinicans to mark the freedom they won.1
08

Why does it matter?

22 May shows that the enslaved won their own freedom rather than simply receiving it from Paris.1

Questions to think about

  1. Why did the arrest of a drummer spark an uprising?
  2. Why did the governor declare abolition before the decree arrived?
  3. Who deserves the credit for ending slavery in Martinique?
  4. Why was the statue of Schœlcher pulled down in 2020?
09

What other events are like this?

  • 1791The Haitian Revolution — The only revolution in history in which enslaved people overthrew their enslavers and founded an independent state.
  • 1831The Baptist War — A Baptist deacon called on enslaved workers to strike after Christmas until they were paid. Up to 60,000 people joined what became the largest slave rising in the British Caribbean — and it helped end slavery across the empire.
  • 1967Mé 67: The Pointe-à-Pitre Massacre — Construction workers struck for a 2.5 per cent pay rise. When negotiations broke down, French police opened fire. The official toll was 7 dead; decades later France admitted at least 87.
