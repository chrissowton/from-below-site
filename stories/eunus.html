Eunus and the First Sicilian Slave War

Enslaved workers on Sicily's great estates rose up under a Syrian slave and prophet named Eunus. They took cities, crowned him king, and held off Roman armies for three years.

When
135 – 132 BCE
Where
Enna and eastern Sicily
Facts checked
2026-11-16

See it on the map →

01

What is the background to this event?

After Rome expelled Carthage from Sicily, speculators from Italy bought or seized huge estates on the island.1

  • Rome's wars flooded the market with captives, and pirates and corrupt governors added more. Slaves were cheap.1
  • According to the historian Diodorus, owners gave enslaved workers too little food and clothing, and worked them to exhaustion.1
  • Some herdsmen were armed and turned to banditry.

In their own words

03

What happened?

In 135 BCE, Eunus and 400 enslaved people seized the city of Enna in the centre of the island.1

  • Soon afterwards, Cleon, another slave, led a rising near Agrigentum on the south coast, whose inhabitants were massacred. He joined Eunus as his military commander.1
  • Eunus was proclaimed king under the name Antiochus, after the kings of his native Syria.1
  • The rebels took the eastern cities of Catana and Tauromenium (Taormina), and defeated several Roman forces.1
  • The revolt inspired smaller risings in Italy and as far away as Delos in the Aegean.1
  • In 134–132 BCE, the consul Rupilius besieged the rebel cities. Tauromenium and Enna fell, and the revolt ended.1

04

What happened next?

  • Punishment: ancient writers say thousands of captured rebels were crucified or killed. Eunus was taken prisoner and died in captivity.
  • More revolts: a second slave war broke out in Sicily in 104 BCE, and the revolt of Spartacus in Italy in 73 BCE.
05

What do others say?

At the time

Diodorus, our main source, blamed the cruelty of Sicilian slave-owners for the revolt, though he also described the rebels as violent.2

Debates since

  • Aims: historians such as Keith Bradley argue that the rebels sought freedom for themselves, not the abolition of slavery; they may have kept slaves of their own.3
  • Violence on both sides: rebels killed slave-owners and townspeople; Rome killed the rebels in large numbers.13
06

The evidence

  • Diodorus's account, preserved in later summaries.2
  • Coins thought to have been issued by Eunus as King Antiochus.
07

How is it remembered?

  • Enna remembers Eunus as part of its history.
08

Why does it matter?

The first Sicilian slave war showed that enslaved people from many lands could unite against Rome — and that Roman wealth rested on slavery.

  • It was the first of three great slave wars against the Roman Republic.

09

What other events are like this?

  • 73 BCESpartacus and the Slave Revolt — Seventy gladiators broke out of a training school armed with kitchen knives. Within two years, an army of some 120,000 enslaved people had defeated Rome's legions again and again.
  • 464 BCEThe Helot Revolt — Sparta's power rested on the forced labour of the helots, a conquered people. When an earthquake shattered Sparta, the helots of Messenia rose up and held out on a mountain fortress for years.
  • 869The Zanj Rebellion — Enslaved East Africans forced to dig salt-crusted marshland in southern Iraq rose against the Abbasid Caliphate, built their own state and held out for fourteen years.
