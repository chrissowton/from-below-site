The Athenian Revolution

When a Spartan king and his Athenian ally tried to dissolve Athens's council and exile hundreds of families, the ordinary people of Athens rose up, besieged them on the Acropolis and drove them out. Democracy followed.

When
508 – 507 BC
Where
The Acropolis, Athens

See it on the map →

01

What is the background to this event?

In the sixth century BC, Athens was ruled by aristocrats and, for long periods, by tyrants.1

  • In 510 BC, King Cleomenes of Sparta helped expel the tyrant Hippias.1
  • Two aristocrats competed for power: Isagoras, supported by Sparta, and Cleisthenes, of the Alcmaeonid family, who sought the support of the people.1
  • Cleisthenes was driven into exile.1
02

Who were the key people?

  • The people (demos) of Athens

    Ordinary citizens who rose up and besieged the Acropolis.

  • Cleisthenes

    An aristocrat whose reforms created the institutions of Athenian democracy.

  • Isagoras

    The Spartan-backed leader of the oligarchs.

  • Cleomenes I

    King of Sparta.

03

What happened?

With Cleisthenes in exile, Isagoras and Cleomenes expelled hundreds of Athenian families and tried to dissolve the city's council.1

  • The council refused to be dissolved, and the people rose up.1
  • Cleomenes, Isagoras and their supporters were forced to take refuge on the Acropolis, where the people besieged them for two or three days.1
  • They were allowed to leave under truce; some of Isagoras's supporters were later executed.
  • The people recalled Cleisthenes and the exiles.1
  • Cleisthenes reorganised the citizens into ten new tribes, mixing people from different regions, and created a council of 500 chosen by lot.1

04

What happened next?

  • Democracy: Athens became a direct democracy of free male citizens, which lasted, with interruptions, for almost two centuries.1
  • Limits: women, enslaved people and foreigners were excluded.
05

What do others say?

At the time

Our sources, Herodotus and Aristotle, focus on Cleisthenes and say little about how the people organised.3

Debates since

  • From below or above? Ober's view of a popular uprising is influential but debated; others stress Cleisthenes' role.2
  • Democracy for whom? Athenian democracy depended on slavery and excluded women.
06

The evidence

  • Herodotus and the Aristotelian Constitution of the Athenians.3
  • Inscriptions and ostraka (voting shards).
07

How is it remembered?

  • Athens is widely remembered as the birthplace of democracy.
08

Why does it matter?

Historian Josiah Ober argued that Athenian democracy began not as a gift from a reformer but as a revolution from below.2

  • It created institutions — the assembly, the council chosen by lot, ostracism — that have inspired democrats ever since.

Questions to think about

  1. Who made Athenian democracy: Cleisthenes or the people?
  2. Why did the people rise up against Isagoras and Sparta?
  3. Who was excluded from Athenian democracy?
  4. What can modern democracies learn from Athens?
09

What other events are like this?

  • 494 BCThe First Secession of the Plebs — Crushed by debt and denied any say in government, the common people of Rome — who made up most of its army — simply walked out of the city. It was perhaps the world's first general strike.
  • 1647The Putney Debates — Ordinary soldiers and their elected representatives argued with Cromwell and the army's generals about who should have the right to vote.
  • 133 BCThe Gracchi and the Land of Rome — Tiberius Gracchus proposed giving public land held by the rich to poor citizens. Senators beat him to death with clubs. Ten years later his brother Gaius tried again — and was killed too.
