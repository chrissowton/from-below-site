Mozambique's Post-Election Protests

After an election that observers said was manipulated, an opposition candidate called Mozambicans onto the streets from hiding. Five months of protests left more than 300 dead before he and the new president shook hands.

When
21 October 2024 – 24 March 2025
Where
Maputo, Beira, Nampula and towns across Mozambique
Facts checked
2026-11-17

01

What is the background to this event?

FRELIMO, the movement that led Mozambique's independence struggle, had governed the country since 1975, and faced growing concern about authoritarianism and impunity.1

  • In the general election of 9 October 2024, FRELIMO's candidate, Daniel Chapo, was declared the winner with about 71% of the vote.1
  • Venâncio Mondlane, backed by the small PODEMOS party, claimed his observers' figures showed he had won 53%. EU observers reported irregularities during counting and the unjustified alteration of results. The Catholic bishops also questioned the result.1
  • Many young people faced poverty and unemployment; one gas project worth $20 billion had been delayed by the insurgency in Cabo Delgado.1
02

Who were the key people?

  • Venâncio Mondlane

    Opposition presidential candidate, who led the protests largely through livestreams, partly from hiding abroad.

  • Elvino Dias and Paulo Guambe

    Mondlane's lawyer and his party's spokesperson, shot dead in Maputo on 18 October 2024.

  • Protesters

    Many of them young, who blocked roads and joined strikes and marches across the country.

  • Daniel Chapo

    FRELIMO's candidate, sworn in as president in January 2025.

In their own words

03

What happened?

On 18 October 2024, Mondlane's lawyer, Elvino Dias, and the PODEMOS spokesperson, Paulo Guambe, were shot dead in their car in Maputo by unidentified attackers. Mondlane blamed the security forces.1

  • From 21 October, protests and strikes broke out in Maputo, Beira, Nampula and elsewhere. When Chapo's victory was announced on 25 October, riots spread across the country, and security forces used live ammunition.1
  • Mondlane went into hiding abroad, saying his life was in danger, and called for marches and strikes by livestream. By 6 November, Human Rights Watch had counted at least 30 deaths.1
  • On 23 December, the Constitutional Council confirmed FRELIMO's victory. The protests that followed left at least 125 people dead, including at least 33 prisoners killed during a mass escape from a prison.1
  • Mondlane returned on 9 January 2025. Chapo was sworn in on 15 January, and later sacked the police chief over his heavy-handed response to the protests.1
  • On 24 March 2025, Mondlane announced that he had met Chapo, and that the government had agreed to release political prisoners and compensate the injured and the families of the dead.1

Amnesty International called for investigations into the killing of more than 300 protesters by government forces. The government said 22 members of the security forces were killed.1

04

What happened next?

  • A handshake: legal experts described the meeting of March 2025 as "breaking the ice" and the beginning of the end of the protests.1
  • A new party: Mondlane set up a new party, ANAMALALA. Its name, which means "it's over" in the Makhuwa language, caused a dispute with the government.1
05

Why does it matter?

The protests were a major challenge to FRELIMO, which has governed since independence. They were led by a politician outside the established parties, organised largely through social media, and are counted among the Gen Z protests of 2024–25.14

  • Amnesty International called for investigations into the killing of more than 300 protesters; the government said 22 members of the security forces were killed.1
  • The protests ended after a meeting between Mondlane and the president in March 2025, not with a clear victory.1
  • Mondlane set up a new party, ANAMALALA — 'it's over' in Makhuwa.1

What do others say?How it was seen at the time, and where historians disagree

At the time

Mondlane called for a "revolution"; Chapo said FRELIMO was "an organised party that prepares its victories", and the attorney general accused Mondlane of inciting violence.1

Debates since

  • Protest or riot? Many protests were peaceful, but some turned to looting and attacks on property; the security forces were accused of killing unarmed protesters, some as young as 16.1
  • Violence: Amnesty International put the number of protesters killed at more than 300; the government said 22 members of the security forces were killed.1
How do we know?The evidence, and a source from the time
  • Reports by EU election observers, Human Rights Watch, Amnesty International and local monitors, and Mondlane's own livestreams.1

Observers' reports carry weight because they are independent, but government and opposition can read the same report in very different ways.

How is it remembered?Memorials, anniversaries and arguments over memory
  • The killing of Elvino Dias and Paulo Guambe by unidentified attackers, which began the crisis, was condemned by the EU, the African Union, the United States and Portugal.1
