The Women of Boeung Kak Lake

When Phnom Penh's largest lake was leased to a company linked to a ruling-party senator and filled with sand, thousands of families were evicted. Women led the resistance, and some went to prison for it.

When
2007 – 2016
Where
Boeung Kak, Phnom Penh, Cambodia
Facts checked
2026-11-16

See it on the map →

01

What is the background to this event?

Boeung Kak Lake was the largest lake in Phnom Penh, surrounded by homes and businesses.1

  • In 2007, the city leased the lake and surrounding land for 99 years to Shukaku Inc., a company owned by the wife of a senator from the ruling party.1
  • The lease was expected to affect over 4,000 families.1
  • From 2008, the lake was filled with sand for luxury development.1
02

Who were the key people?

  • Tep Vanny

    Leader of the women's campaign, imprisoned several times.

  • The Boeung Kak 13

    Thirteen women, aged 25 to 72, jailed in 2012.

  • Yorm Bopha

    Activist imprisoned for her campaigning.

03

What happened?

Residents protested against the filling of the lake and the evictions.1

  • By 2010, 90% of the lake had been filled, and thousands of people had been evicted.1
  • Women from the community, led by Tep Vanny, became the leaders of the resistance.1
  • After pressure from the community, the World Bank stopped new loans to Cambodia until the dispute was resolved.1
  • On 24 May 2012, thirteen women were arrested and sentenced to two and a half years in prison for occupying land.1
  • After an international campaign, they won their release.1
04

What happened next?

  • Land titles: some remaining residents won land titles, but many had already lost their homes.
  • Prison again: Tep Vanny was jailed again in 2016–18.
05

Why does it matter?

Boeung Kak shows how women became leaders in the fight for housing rights in Cambodia.

  • It shows the human cost of land grabs for development.
  • Some remaining residents won land titles, but many had already lost their homes.
  • Tep Vanny was jailed again in 2016–18, showing the risks the women took.

What do others say?How it was seen at the time, and where historians disagree

At the time

The government argued the development was in the public interest; residents called it a land grab.1

Debates since

  • Development for whom? Critics point to links between the company and the ruling party; supporters of the project point to new roads and buildings.1
  • Violence: the protesters were peaceful; police and security guards broke up protests and arrested women.1
How do we know?The evidence, and a source from the time
  • Reports by Amnesty International and Human Rights Watch.1
