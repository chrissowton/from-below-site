Hawo Tako and the Somali Youth League

Thirteen young men founded a club that refused to speak of clan and demanded a united, independent Somalia. In January 1948 its supporters filled the streets of Mogadishu — a day that ended in bloodshed, and made a young woman a national heroine.

01

What is the background to this event?

In the nineteenth century, the lands of the Somali people were divided between Britain, Italy, France and Ethiopia.

  • During the Second World War, Britain occupied Italian Somaliland and ruled it under military administration from 1941 to 1950.1
  • After the war, the four victorious powers had to decide the future of Italy's colonies. Many Italians in Somalia hoped Italy would return.

In their own words

03

What happened?

The Somali Youth Club was founded in 1943, with thirteen members. Members agreed never to mention their clan, in order to build national unity.1

  • It grew quickly, opened branches across the Somali territories and became the Somali Youth League (SYL).1
  • Its aims were to unite all Somali territories, spread modern education, develop a written Somali language, and oppose the return of Italian rule.1
  • In January 1948, a Four-Power Commission visited Mogadishu to hear local opinion. The League and pro-Italian groups both organised demonstrations.12
  • On 11 January 1948, the demonstrations turned into a riot. League supporters attacked Italians and their property. More than 50 Italians and 14 Somalis were killed.23
  • Among the Somali dead was Hawo Tako. Accounts of her death differ: in the national story she died fighting for independence; some sources say she was killed while trying to stop the killing of Italians.2

04

What happened next?

  • Trusteeship: in 1950, the United Nations returned southern Somalia to Italy as a trust territory for ten years, against the League's wishes.
  • Independence: in 1960, the former Italian and British territories united as the independent Somali Republic. The League formed the government.
  • Coup: the League governed until the military coup of Siad Barre in 1969.
05

What do others say?

At the time

Italians blamed the League and the British administration for the killings; the League blamed provocation by pro-Italian groups.3

Debates since

  • A massacre or an uprising? In Italy the day is remembered as a massacre of Italian civilians; in Somalia, as a stand against the return of colonial rule. Both things happened.23
  • Hawo Tako: her story has been used by different governments, including Siad Barre's, which founded a women's organisation in her memory in 1977.2

The evidence

07

How is it remembered?

  • Monuments in Mogadishu honour Hawo Tako and the "stone throwers" of 1948.
  • 15 May, the League's founding day, is celebrated as Somali Youth Day.
08

Why does it matter?

The League was Somalia's first political party and led the country to independence, with a vision of unity above clan.

  • Women such as Hawo Tako took part from the start.
  • The events of 1948 show how anti-colonial protest could turn into deadly violence against civilians.
