RAWA — the Revolutionary Association of the Women of Afghanistan

Founded by a 20-year-old student, RAWA has opposed every Afghan government since 1977. Its members ran secret schools, filmed abuses under the Taliban and paid a high price: its founder was assassinated in 1987.

When
1977 – present
Where
Kabul, Afghanistan, and Quetta, Pakistan

See it on the map →

01

What is the background to this event?

In the 1970s, Afghanistan was a poor, mostly rural country where women's rights were limited by law and custom.

  • In 1977, Meena Keshwar Kamal, a student at Kabul University, founded RAWA with other young women.1
  • RAWA called for a secular, democratic Afghanistan in which women could participate fully.1
02

Who were the key people?

  • Meena Keshwar Kamal

    Founder of RAWA, assassinated in Quetta in 1987.

  • RAWA members

    Women who worked secretly, often under false names, in Afghanistan and in refugee camps in Pakistan.

  • Afghan refugee women and girls

    Who attended RAWA's schools and courses.

03

What happened?

After the Soviet invasion in 1979, Meena campaigned against the occupation and the Soviet-backed government.1

  • In 1981 she launched a magazine, Payam-e-Zan (Woman's Message).1
  • RAWA set up schools for refugee children, hospitals and handicraft centres for refugee women in Pakistan.1
  • RAWA also opposed the mujahideen and Islamist groups fighting the Soviets.1
  • On 4 February 1987, Meena was assassinated in Quetta, Pakistan. RAWA blamed Afghan intelligence agents working with an Islamist group; the killers' identity remains disputed.1
  • Under the Taliban (1996–2001), RAWA ran secret schools, orphanages and nursing courses, and its members secretly filmed public punishments of women to show the world.1

04

What happened next?

  • After 2001: RAWA opposed the US-backed government as well, arguing it included warlords and fundamentalists.1
  • The Taliban return: since the Taliban regained power in 2021 and banned girls from secondary school and university, RAWA has again worked underground.
05

What do others say?

At the time

RAWA's opponents, from communists to Islamists, accused it of being a front for their enemies. RAWA insisted on its independence.1

Debates since

  • Tactics: some Afghan women's groups criticised RAWA's refusal to work with any government; supporters say this kept it honest.2
  • Images: RAWA objected when its photographs were used in US propaganda in 2001.1
  • Non-violence: RAWA has campaigned without arms, while its members faced violence from many sides.1
06

The evidence

  • Payam-e-Zan and RAWA's archive of photographs and video.1
  • Anne Brodsky's interviews with members.2
07

How is it remembered?

  • RAWA commemorates Meena each year on the anniversary of her death.
08

Why does it matter?

RAWA is one of the longest-lasting independent women's organisations in the Muslim world, and shows women organising for themselves under war and dictatorship.

  • It has insisted on independence from all governments and foreign powers.

What other events are like this?

  • 2022Woman, Life, Freedom — The death in custody of a young Kurdish woman arrested for 'improper' hijab sparked the largest protests in Iran in decades, led by women and girls under the slogan 'Woman, Life, Freedom'.
  • 1952Fatima Ahmed Ibrahim and the Sudanese Women's Union — A schoolgirl who led a strike for science lessons founded one of Africa's largest women's movements. In 1965 she became the first woman elected to Sudan's parliament.
  • 2011The Women to Drive Movement — For almost three decades, Saudi women defied the world's only ban on women driving — getting behind the wheel, filming themselves and petitioning the king. The ban was lifted in 2018, but many of the campaigners were in prison.
