The Fasci Siciliani

Sicily's peasants, sulphur miners and artisans formed hundreds of workers' leagues demanding fair contracts and land. The government crushed them with martial law and mass arrests.

When
1889 – 1894
Where
Piana degli Albanesi and Sicily, Italy
Facts checked
2026-11-16

See it on the map →

01

What is the background to this event?

From the late 1880s, Italy was in a deep economic recession.1

  • Sicilian peasants and sharecroppers faced unfair contracts, low wages, usury and high local taxes.1
  • Common land had been taken over by the powerful.1
  • On 20 January 1893, at Caltavuturo, authorities killed 13 peasants who had occupied land they said was theirs.1

In their own words

03

What happened?

From 1889, workers' leagues (fasci) spread across Sicily.1

  • They brought together farm workers, sharecroppers, artisans, intellectuals, industrial workers and sulphur miners — and many women.1
  • By the end of 1893, there were about 170 fasci, with by some estimates over 300,000 members.1
  • They demanded fair rents, new sharecropping contracts, higher wages, an end to usury, lower local taxes and the return of common land.1
  • In summer 1893, landowners and mine owners rejected their demands, and strikes spread across the island.1
  • In January 1894, Prime Minister Crispi declared a state of emergency.1
04

What happened next?

  • Repression: troops killed scores of protesters; thousands were jailed, and about 1,000 people were deported to penal islands without trial.1
  • Trials: in May 1894, the leaders were sentenced to between 12 and 18 years.1
  • Emigration: the defeat led to a rise in emigration from Sicily.1
05

Why does it matter?

The Fasci Siciliani were one of the first mass socialist movements in Italy, built by the poorest people of Sicily.

  • Despite the name, they had nothing to do with the later fascist movement.
  • The repression was severe: troops killed scores of protesters, and about 1,000 people were deported to penal islands without trial.1
  • The defeat led to a rise in emigration from Sicily.1

What do others say?How it was seen at the time, and where historians disagree

At the time

Landowners and the government saw an insurrection; supporters saw a fight for basic rights.1

Debates since

  • Modern or millenarian? Eric Hobsbawm saw the peasants treating socialism as a new religion of justice.13
  • Violence: strikes sometimes became riots that attacked tax offices and town halls; soldiers and police killed scores of protesters.1

Reading it

This drawing was made for a popular magazine read mainly in the north of Italy. Readers in Milan or Turin learned that Sicily was disorderly. What they did not see was the poverty that drove peasants and sulphur miners to organise.
