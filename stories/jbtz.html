The Trial of the Four and the Slovenian Spring

When the Yugoslav army arrested three journalists and a soldier for revealing its secrets — and tried them in Serbo-Croatian rather than Slovene — 100,000 people signed a petition and tens of thousands filled Ljubljana's main square.

When
May – July 1988
Where
Ljubljana, Slovenia
Facts checked

01

What is the background to this event?

In the late 1980s, Slovenia was the most liberal of Yugoslavia's republics. Its communist leader, Milan Kučan, allowed growing press freedom.1

  • The youth magazine Mladina broke taboos with satire and criticism of the Yugoslav People's Army (JNA).1
  • It called the defence minister a "merchant of death" for selling arms to famine-stricken Ethiopia.1
  • Many critical articles were written by the young defence writer Janez Janša.1

03

What happened?

In May and June 1988, the army arrested Janša, Borštner, Tasić and Zavrl for betraying military secrets.1

  • The Committee for the Defence of Human Rights was founded the same day as the arrest.1
  • Its petition for the accused gathered 100,000 signatures.1
  • On 22 June 1988, at least 40,000 people rallied in Congress Square, Ljubljana.1
  • The military court held the trial in Serbo-Croatian, although Slovenia's constitution required Slovene. This caused further anger.1
  • In July, the four were sentenced to between six months and four years in prison.1

04

What happened next?

  • Mild sentences: the four were handed to the Slovene authorities, who carried out the sentences as mildly as possible.1
  • Democracy: the Committee became the core of the democratic opposition, which won Slovenia's first free elections in 1990.
  • Independence: Slovenia declared independence in June 1991, followed by a ten-day war with the JNA.
05

What do others say?

At the time

The army said it was defending Yugoslavia; Slovenes saw an attack on free speech and on their republic.1

Debates since

  • Janša: his later career as a controversial right-wing prime minister has coloured how the trial is remembered.
  • Non-violence: the movement was entirely peaceful.

How is it remembered?

  • The trial is remembered as a founding moment of Slovenian democracy.
08

Why does it matter?

The trial united Slovenians across political lines in defence of free speech, their language and their rights.

  • It is seen as the beginning of the "Slovenian Spring".1

Questions to think about

  3. How did one trial lead to a democratic movement?
