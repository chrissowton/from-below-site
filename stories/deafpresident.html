Deaf President Now

The world's only university for deaf students had never had a deaf president. When the board chose a hearing candidate again, students shut down the campus — and won in a week.

When
6 – 13 March 1988
Where
Gallaudet University, Washington, D.C., United States
Facts checked

01

What is the background to this event?

Gallaudet University in Washington, D.C., was founded in 1864 as the world's first university for deaf and hard-of-hearing students.1

  • For more than 120 years, every president of Gallaudet had been hearing. Before 1980, no deaf person had held the post of president or chief academic officer.1
  • Deaf people faced widespread discrimination, and many hearing people assumed that deaf people could not lead.
  • American Sign Language (ASL) had only recently been recognised by linguists as a full language, and Deaf people increasingly saw themselves as a cultural and linguistic minority.

When the president resigned in 1987, deaf organisations campaigned for a deaf successor. The board narrowed the field to three candidates: Elisabeth Zinser, who was hearing, and two deaf candidates, I. King Jordan and Harvey Corson.1

03

What happened?

On 6 March 1988, the Board of Trustees chose Zinser.1 Angry students marched to the hotel where the board was meeting. The chair, Jane Bassett Spilman, reportedly told them that deaf people could not function in a hearing world.1

The next morning, students shut down the campus.1

  • They barricaded the gates with locks and buses, letting the air out of the tyres.
  • They set out four demands: Zinser's resignation and the appointment of a deaf president; Spilman's resignation; a board with a 51 per cent deaf majority; and no reprisals against the protesters.1
  • Students, staff, alumni and the wider Deaf community marched to the US Capitol, held rallies and gave interviews to the press. They burned effigies of Zinser and Spilman.1
  • The protest received national media coverage, and support poured in from across the country.

On 13 March, after a week of protest, the board gave in to all four demands. I. King Jordan was appointed Gallaudet's first deaf president.1

04

What happened next?

  • New leadership: Jordan served as president until 2006. The board was reconstituted with a deaf majority and a deaf chair, Philip Bravin.
  • National change: the protest raised awareness of disability rights and is widely seen as helping to build support for the Americans with Disabilities Act (ADA) of 1990.
  • Deaf culture: it gave confidence to Deaf communities in the United States and around the world.
05

What do others say?

At the time

Some board members and commentators argued that the most qualified candidate should be chosen regardless of hearing status. The students argued that a university for deaf people should be led by a deaf person, as a matter of equality and role models.

Commentators' debates

  • Who is "deaf enough"? Jordan became deaf at 21 and used both speech and sign; some in the Deaf community had preferred Corson, who was born deaf. Later controversies at Gallaudet, including protests in 2006, raised similar questions about identity.
  • Tactics: the shutdown and the burning of effigies were confrontational, but the protest was non-violent. Commentators credit its discipline and media strategy for its success.
  • Impact on the ADA: historians debate how much the protest directly influenced the passage of the ADA.

How is it remembered?

  • Gallaudet commemorates the protest every March.
  • A documentary about the protest, Deaf President Now!, was released in 2025.
08

Why does it matter?

Deaf President Now was a turning point for Deaf people and for the wider disability rights movement. In a week, students overturned a century of assumptions about who could lead.

  • It showed the power of a well-organised, disciplined protest with clear demands.
  • It challenged the idea that deafness is a deficiency rather than a difference.
  • It linked disability rights to civil rights and self-representation: "nothing about us without us".

Questions to think about

  • 1977Aoi Shiba no Kai and the Kawasaki Bus Struggle — A radical group of people with cerebral palsy refused to be pitied. They protested against the killing of disabled children — and occupied buses that refused to carry wheelchair users.
