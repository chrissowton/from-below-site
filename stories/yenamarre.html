Y'en a Marre — "Fed Up"

Angry about power cuts, a group of rappers and journalists launched a movement called 'Fed Up'. They registered hundreds of thousands of young voters and helped defeat President Abdoulaye Wade's bid for a third term.

When
2011 – 2012
Where
Dakar, Senegal
Facts checked
2026-11-16

See it on the map →

01

What is the background to this event?

In 2011, Senegal suffered long power cuts, high unemployment and rising prices. President Abdoulaye Wade, in power since 2000, planned to stand for a third term.1

  • Critics argued that a third term breached the constitution.1
  • Wade also proposed changes to the electoral system that critics said would help his son, Karim Wade, succeed him.1

In their own words

03

What happened?

Y'en a Marre was founded in January 2011 by rappers and journalists, at first in protest against power cuts.1

  • The movement called on young people to register to vote, under the slogan of a "New Type of Senegalese". It said it registered about 300,000 voters.1
  • Its rappers released protest songs, including "Faux! Pas Forcé".1
  • On 23 June 2011, mass protests forced Wade to withdraw his electoral reform.1
  • When the Constitutional Council allowed Wade to stand again in January 2012, protests broke out. On 16 February, three founders were arrested at a sit-in in Dakar's Obelisk Square.1
  • Several people were killed in clashes between protesters and police in early 2012; estimates range from about six to more than ten.2
  • In the second round in March 2012, Wade was defeated by Macky Sall, and accepted the result.1

04

What happened next?

  • Later campaigns: Y'en a Marre continued to campaign on governance and opposed Macky Sall's government in turn.
  • Influence: its founders advised movements in Burkina Faso (Le Balai Citoyen) and the DRC (Filimbi and LUCHA). In 2015, Y'en a Marre activists were arrested in Kinshasa.
  • 2024: when Sall postponed the 2024 election, Y'en a Marre joined the protests that forced the vote to go ahead.
05

What do others say?

At the time

Wade's supporters accused the movement of being partisan; Y'en a Marre insisted it was non-party.1

Debates since

  • Impact: some argue the movement was decisive in Wade's defeat; others stress the role of opposition parties.
  • Violence: the movement advocated non-violence, but some protests turned violent, and people were killed on both the police and protester sides of the clashes.2

The evidence

07

How is it remembered?

  • Y'en a Marre is still active, and its headquarters in Dakar is a meeting place for activists.
08

Why does it matter?

Y'en a Marre showed how hip-hop and youth culture could become a force for democracy.

  • It focused on voter registration and peaceful protest.
  • It inspired similar movements across Africa.

Questions to think about

  1. Why did power cuts become a political issue?
  2. How did music help to build the movement?
  3. Why did the movement focus on voter registration?
  4. Should social movements stay outside party politics?
