Africa's First Pride March

A Black gay activist, tried for treason by the apartheid state, led Africa's first pride march — and helped make South Africa the first country to protect gay rights in its constitution.

When
13 October 1990
Where
Johannesburg, South Africa

01

What is the background to this event?

Under apartheid, sex between men was illegal in South Africa, and gay and lesbian people faced police raids and discrimination. Black gay and lesbian people faced both racism and homophobia.

  • Simon Nkoli was born in Soweto in 1957. He became involved in anti-apartheid activism during the 1976 Soweto uprising.1
  • In 1984, he was arrested with 21 other anti-apartheid leaders and charged with treason, which carried a possible death sentence, in what became the Delmas Treason Trial.1
  • During the trial, he came out as gay to his co-defendants. His case attracted international support, especially among gay and lesbian groups — but the mainly white Gay Association of South Africa did not support him.1

In their own words

03

What happened?

After his acquittal in 1988, Nkoli co-founded the Gay and Lesbian Organisation of the Witwatersrand (GLOW), a multiracial organisation that held political rallies and social events.1

On 13 October 1990, GLOW organised the first pride march in South Africa — and on the African continent — in Johannesburg.2

  • About 800 people took part.2 Many wore paper bags over their heads to hide their identities, fearing they would lose their jobs or be attacked.
  • The crowd was addressed by Nkoli, Donné Rundle, Beverley Ditsie, Edwin Cameron and Hendrik Pretorius.2
  • Nkoli declared that the fight against apartheid and the fight for sexual freedom were inseparable: "I cannot be free as a black man if I am not free as a gay man."2

04

What happened next?

  • The constitution: anti-apartheid gay activists persuaded the African National Congress to support gay rights. In 1996, South Africa's constitution became the first in the world to prohibit discrimination on the grounds of sexual orientation.12
  • Law reform: the National Coalition for Gay and Lesbian Equality, in which Nkoli was active, successfully challenged the anti-sodomy laws, which the Constitutional Court struck down in 1998. Same-sex marriage was legalised in 2006.1
  • AIDS activism: Nkoli was one of the first Black South Africans to disclose that he was living with HIV. His death in 1998 inspired activists to found the Treatment Action Campaign, which won wider access to HIV treatment.1
05

What do others say?

At the time

Some anti-apartheid activists saw gay rights as a distraction from the struggle, while some white gay organisations avoided politics. Nkoli argued that the two struggles could not be separated.

Commentators' debates

  • Laws and lives: South Africa's laws are among the most progressive in the world, but many LGBTQ+ people, especially in townships, still face violence and discrimination.
  • Protest or party? In 2012, the Johannesburg Pride march was marred by a clash between activists and organisers, and the organising body split over whether pride should remain political.2
  • Across Africa: the South African experience is often contrasted with countries where same-sex relations remain criminalised.

07

How is it remembered?

  • Johannesburg Pride and Soweto Pride continue each year, and Nkoli is honoured as a founder of the movement.
  • Beverley Ditsie's documentary Simon and I (2002) tells the story of their activism.
08

Why does it matter?

The first pride march in Africa linked the struggles against racism and homophobia. It helped make South Africa a pioneer of gay and lesbian rights.

  • It shows the importance of solidarity between movements.
  • It challenges the claim that homosexuality is "un-African".
  • Yet hate crimes, including so-called "corrective rape" of lesbians, remain a serious problem, and pride marches today also protest against this violence.2

What other events are like this?

  • 1969The Stonewall Uprising — A routine police raid on a gay bar met resistance, and several nights of protest gave rise to the modern LGBTQ+ liberation movement.
  • 1978The First Sydney Mardi Gras — A night-time street party for gay rights ended with police violence and 53 arrests. A newspaper printed the names of those arrested — and the protest grew into one of the world's largest pride events.
  • 1976The Soweto Uprising — Thousands of Black schoolchildren marched against being taught in Afrikaans. Police opened fire, and the uprising spread across the country.
