The Squatters of 1946

Returning from war to find no homes, tens of thousands of families moved into empty army camps across Britain. In London, Communist activists led squatters into empty luxury flats.

When
1945 – 1946
Where
Army camps across Britain and empty flats in London

01

What is the background to this event?

By 1945, bombing had destroyed or damaged hundreds of thousands of homes in Britain, and almost no new houses had been built during the war.

  • Servicemen returned home to overcrowded housing, often living with parents or in a single room.
  • After the First World War, ex-servicemen had already squatted land and empty houses.1
  • In 1945, ex-servicemen calling themselves "Vigilantes" moved homeless families into empty houses in Brighton.1

In their own words

03

What happened?

In the summer of 1946, as military camps were emptied, families moved in. The movement spread rapidly across the country.1

  • By October 1946, Aneurin Bevan told parliament that 1,038 camps in England and Wales were occupied by 39,535 people.1
  • Squatters organised camp committees, shared cooking, and arranged water and electricity.
  • Many local councils accepted the squatters and began to provide services.
  • In August, Clementine Churchill defended them as respectable citizens who only wanted a home.1
  • In September 1946, in the "Great Sunday Squat", Communist Party members helped more than 100 families occupy a hotel and flats in central London, including Duchess of Bedford House in Kensington.1
  • The government declared the London occupations illegal. Leaders were arrested and charged with conspiracy, and the families left the flats.

04

What happened next?

  • Camps: many families stayed in the camps for years, and councils eventually rehoused them.
  • Council housing: the Labour government built more than a million homes, mostly council houses, by 1951.
  • Later squats: the 1946 movement inspired squatting campaigns in the 1960s and 1970s.
05

What do others say?

At the time

Many newspapers and politicians sympathised with the families in the camps, but the government feared that the London occupations threatened property rights and order.1

Debates since

  • Spontaneous or political? Historians argue that the camp squats were largely spontaneous, while the London squats were organised by Communists.2
  • Peaceful: the squats were almost entirely peaceful; some police officers even helped.1

How is it remembered?

  • The 1946 squatters are remembered in local histories of the camps and by housing campaigners today.
08

Why does it matter?

The squatters showed that ordinary people would take direct action when the state failed to house them.

  • It was one of the largest direct action movements in modern British history.
  • It put pressure on the government to speed up house building.
