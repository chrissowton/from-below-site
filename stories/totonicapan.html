Atanasio Tzul and the Totonicapán Uprising

When Spanish officials kept collecting a tribute that had been abolished, K'iche' Maya communities refused to pay, drove out the mayor and set up their own government.

When
July – August 1820
Where
Totonicapán, Guatemala
Facts checked
2026-11-16

See it on the map →

01

What is the background to this event?

Under Spanish rule, the colonial economy of Guatemala depended on the land and labour of Indigenous Maya people, who were forced to pay heavy tributes.1

  • In 1812, Spain's liberal Cádiz Constitution abolished the tribute and gave Indigenous Americans the vote.1
  • In 1814, King Ferdinand VII abolished the constitution and the tribute returned.1
  • In 1820, a military revolt in Spain restored the constitution. But colonial officials in Guatemala refused to apply it and kept collecting tribute.1
  • The K'iche' communities of Totonicapán learned of this and refused to pay.1
02

Who were the key people?

  • Atanasio Tzul

    A K'iche' leader (principal) of Totonicapán, who according to tradition was proclaimed king.

  • Lucas Aguilar

    A K'iche' leader who served alongside Tzul in the new government.

  • Prudencio Cózar

    The Spanish official (corregidor) who led a militia to crush the uprising.

  • The 48 cantons of Totonicapán

    The Maya communities whose collective organisation continues today.

03

What happened?

K'iche' towns in Totonicapán rose in opposition. Leaders organised protests in the central plaza, and the mayor fled for his life.1

  • Under Atanasio Tzul, an autonomous Indigenous government was established in Totonicapán. According to popular tradition, Tzul was declared king, with a crown and throne taken from a church statue.1
  • The Indigenous government lasted for about 20 days.1
  • The uprising was put down by a militia of about 1,000 ladino (non-Indigenous) men from nearby towns, led by the Spanish corregidor Prudencio Cózar.1
  • After small skirmishes, Tzul and the other leaders surrendered and were imprisoned.1

The following year, as Central America won independence from Spain, Tzul and the others were pardoned.1

04

What happened next?

  • Independence: Central America declared independence from Spain on 15 September 1821, but Indigenous people continued to face forced labour and loss of land under the new republics.
  • Memory: in some parts of Totonicapán, independence is celebrated not on 15 September but on 12 July, the day Tzul rose up.1
  • The 48 cantons: the Indigenous communal authorities of Totonicapán, the 48 Cantones, remain an important political force and led national protests in 2023.
05

What do others say?

At the time

Colonial officials saw the uprising as a threat to order. The K'iche' communities argued that they were only demanding what the law — the restored constitution — already granted them.

Historians' debates

  • A king? Historians debate whether Tzul was really crowned king or whether this is later legend; some see the title as a symbol of Indigenous self-government rather than monarchy.
  • Independence or local revolt? Some historians see it as part of the independence movement; others as a local tax revolt with its own Indigenous logic.
  • Violence: the uprising involved little bloodshed; it was ended by a superior militia force with few deaths.
06

The evidence

  • Colonial court records of the trial of the leaders.
  • Oral traditions in Totonicapán.
07

How is it remembered?

  • In 2021, Guatemala's president returned the "chair of Atanasio Tzul" to the 48 cantons of Totonicapán.
  • Tzul is honoured as a hero of Indigenous resistance in Guatemala.
08

Why does it matter?

The Totonicapán uprising is a reminder that Indigenous people were not passive in the age of independence. They used the new liberal ideas of the time to demand their rights and briefly governed themselves.

  • It challenges histories of independence that focus only on criollo elites.
  • It is a symbol for the Maya movement in Guatemala today.

Questions to think about

  1. Why did the K'iche' communities refuse to pay the tribute?
  2. How did Spanish liberal ideas help Indigenous people make their case?
  3. Why do some people in Totonicapán celebrate independence on a different date?
  4. Does it matter whether Tzul was really crowned king?

What other events are like this?

  • 1780The Rebellion of Túpac Amaru II — An Indigenous leader and his wife, Micaela Bastidas, led the largest rebellion in Spain's American empire, demanding an end to forced labour and abuse.
  • 1944Guatemala's 'Ten Years of Spring' — Students, teachers and workers brought down a dictator. A decade of democracy and land reform followed — until a US-backed coup ended it.
  • 1994The Zapatista Uprising — On the day the North American Free Trade Agreement came into force, masked Indigenous rebels seized towns in southern Mexico and declared 'Enough!'
