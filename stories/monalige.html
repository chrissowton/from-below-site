Acacio Mañé and the Struggle for Equatorial Guinea

Under Franco's Spain, campaigning for independence in Spanish Guinea was dangerous. Acacio Mañé, a Fang farmer, built a secret movement among teachers, catechists and farmers. In 1959 he was arrested and never seen again — but within a decade the colony was free.

01

What is the background to this event?

Spanish Guinea was a Spanish colony made up of the island of Fernando Po (now Bioko) and the mainland region of Río Muni.1

  • Spain was ruled by the dictator Francisco Franco.
  • Wealth was concentrated in the hands of colonial and elite planters.1
  • By the 1950s, independence movements were growing across Africa.

03

What happened?

Around 1950, Mañé helped to lead a secret organisation, the National Liberation Crusade of Equatorial Guinea.2

  • He spread the idea of independence among teachers, administrators, farmers and catechists, and kept contact with traditional leaders.2
  • The movement was later renamed MONALIGE, the National Liberation Movement of Equatorial Guinea, at the suggestion of Atanasio Ndongo.3
  • Its supporters included both Bubi and Fang people.2
  • On 28 November 1959, Mañé was arrested near the Catholic mission in Bata and put on a ship for Fernando Po. He never arrived; it is believed he was thrown into the sea.2
  • Another leader, Enrique Nvo, who founded the IPGE (Popular Idea of Equatorial Guinea) in exile, was also killed in 1959, allegedly by killers paid by the Spanish authorities.4
04

What happened next?

  • Exile: MONALIGE leaders, accused by Spain of being communists, went into exile in Gabon, while the IPGE was founded by exiles in Gabon and Cameroon. In 1962, Gabon's president Léon M'ba and the IPGE pressed for independence at the United Nations.34
  • Autonomy: Spain granted the colony autonomy in 1964, and many exiles returned.3
  • Independence: in March 1968, under pressure from nationalists and the UN, Spain agreed to independence. A constitution was approved by 63% of voters in August, and the country became independent on 12 October 1968.1
  • Dictatorship: the first president, Francisco Macías Nguema, set up a one-party state and a reign of terror. Up to a third of the population died or fled before he was overthrown in 1979.1
05

Why does it matter?

The story shows the dangers faced by independence campaigners under a colonial dictatorship. It also shows that independence alone did not guarantee freedom: the hopes of Mañé's generation were betrayed by the dictatorship that followed.

  • Leaders went into exile and pressed the case at the United Nations.34
  • Spain granted autonomy in 1964 and, under pressure, independence in 1968.3
  • Acacio Mañé is remembered as one of the first leaders of the independence movement.2

What do others say?How it was seen at the time, and where historians disagree

At the time

The Spanish authorities treated the nationalists as subversives and communists; the nationalists appealed to the United Nations for support.3

Debates since

  • Unknown fates: the circumstances of Mañé's and Nvo's deaths have never been fully established, and the sources rely partly on belief and allegation.24
  • Divided movement: MONALIGE, the IPGE and other groups disagreed over whether to unite with Cameroon and how quickly to seek independence.34

Equatorial Guinea, 12 October 1968

Spain's minister Manuel Fraga and the new president, Francisco Macías Nguema, sign the act of independence of Equatorial Guinea. The ceremony was the end of a long struggle in which campaigners like Acacio Mañé paid with their lives.

Reading it

Spain's minister signs the act of independence with the new president, Francisco Macías Nguema. The ceremony shows officials, not the campaigners like Acacio Mañé who paid with their lives. Spain wanted the moment recorded as a dignified handover. What followed under Macías was one of Africa's most brutal dictatorships.
