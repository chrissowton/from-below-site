The Chipko Movement

Village women in the Himalayas placed themselves between the loggers and the forest, and forced a ban on commercial felling.

When
1973 – 1974 (Reni: 26 March 1974)
Where
Reni village, Chamoli district, Uttarakhand, India
Facts checked
2026-11-15

01

What is the background to this event?

For the villages of the Garhwal Himalaya, then part of Uttar Pradesh, the forests were essential to everyday survival, providing fuel, fodder for animals, building materials, food and medicinal plants. Under a system inherited from colonial rule, however, the forests were controlled by the state Forest Department, which auctioned felling rights to contractors from outside the region.

Deforestation had serious consequences:

  • Landslides and soil erosion became more frequent; the devastating Alaknanda flood of 1970 was widely blamed on the loss of forest cover upstream.
  • Local people received little benefit from the timber trade, and were sometimes refused permission to cut a few trees for agricultural tools while outside companies were allowed to fell large areas.
  • Women, who carried out most of the work of gathering fuel and fodder, felt the effects most directly.

In 1964 the Gandhian activist Chandi Prasad Bhatt and others founded the Dasholi Gram Swarajya Sangh (DGSS), a cooperative that sought to create local employment from forest resources. Its experience of being denied trees that were then given to a sports goods company in 1973 became the immediate trigger for protest.

03

What happened?

The movement began in April 1973 at Mandal, near Gopeshwar, where around a hundred villagers and DGSS workers, beating drums and shouting slogans, confronted the employees of a sports goods company who had come to fell ash trees, forcing them to withdraw.1 Activists pledged that, if necessary, they would hug the trees to prevent them from being cut — the word chipko means "to cling" or "to embrace".

The decisive confrontation came at Reni, a village overlooking the Alaknanda River, where the government had auctioned some 2,500 trees.1 On the day the felling was due to begin, in March 1974, the men of the village and DGSS workers had been drawn away to the district headquarters by an announcement of compensation payments for land.12 Labourers and forest officials arrived by truck.

  • A young girl saw the workers heading for the forest and ran to tell Gaura Devi.2
  • Gaura Devi gathered around 27 women and girls, and they went to confront the loggers.1
  • The women blocked the path, faced down threats and abuse, and kept watch over the forest through the night. The loggers withdrew.2

According to one account of the confrontation, Gaura Devi told the men:

"This forest nurtures us like a mother; you will only be able to use your axes on it if you shoot me first."Gaura Devi at Reni, March 1974, as recounted by Sankalp India Foundation3

She later insisted that the action had not been planned in advance: "Our men were out of the village so we had to come forward and protect the trees."3

04

What happened next?

  • Spread of the movement: Chipko actions spread across the Himalayan districts during the 1970s; in 1977 women in one area tied sacred threads (rakhi) around trees marked for felling.
  • A national ban: in 1980 Prime Minister Indira Gandhi imposed a fifteen-year ban on commercial green felling in the Himalayan forests of Uttar Pradesh.
  • International recognition: in 1987 the movement received the Right Livelihood Award "for its dedication to the conservation, restoration and ecologically-sound use of India's natural resources".
  • Later struggles: activists went on to oppose limestone quarrying in the Doon Valley and the Tehri Dam. In 1982 the forests around Reni became part of the Nanda Devi National Park, which in turn restricted villagers' traditional access — a sign that conservation and local rights do not always coincide.
05

What do others say?

At the time

Forest officials and contractors argued that commercial felling was legal, managed and economically necessary, and that local people were being stirred up by outside activists.

Historians' debates

  • Environmental or economic? Ramachandra Guha argued that Chipko was first and foremost a peasant movement over control of local resources, rather than an environmental movement in the Western sense.
  • A women's movement? Vandana Shiva and others presented Chipko as an example of women's special relationship with nature. Critics argue that this romanticises rural women and obscures the role of men and of class and caste.
  • Leaders: there has been public disagreement over the respective roles of Chandi Prasad Bhatt and Sunderlal Bahuguna.
  • Outcomes: the 1980 ban protected forests but also restricted local villagers' own use of forest resources.

The evidence

  • Reports by the Dasholi Gram Swarajya Sangh and contemporary press coverage.

  • Photographs and films made by activists.
07

How is it remembered?

  • Reni and nearby villages marked the 30th anniversary in 2004 and the 50th in 2024.
  • The movement was marked by a Google Doodle on its 45th anniversary in 2018, and Gaura Devi is commemorated in Uttarakhand.
  • The movement is often cited in Indian school textbooks.
08

Why does it matter?

Chipko is often described as one of the first modern environmental movements in the Global South. For the women of Reni, protecting the forest was not an abstract cause but a question of survival, and their action showed the connection between ecology, livelihood and social justice decades before "climate justice" became a common phrase.

  • It achieved a concrete policy change: a ban on commercial felling across a large region.
  • It demonstrated the leadership of rural women, who are frequently ignored in both environmental and political history.
  • It inspired tree-protection and forest-rights movements in India and around the world, such as the Appiko movement in Karnataka.

Questions to think about

What other events are like this?

  • 1966The Wave Hill Walk-Off — Gurindji stockmen and their families walked off a British-owned cattle station. What began as a strike over pay became the foundation of the Aboriginal land rights movement.
  • 1929The Women's War (Ogu Umunwanyi) — Tens of thousands of Igbo and Ibibio women challenged British colonial rule, using their own traditions of collective protest.
  • 1930The Salt March — A 24-day march to the sea to make salt in defiance of British law set off a campaign of civil disobedience across India.
