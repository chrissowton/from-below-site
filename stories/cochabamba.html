The Cochabamba Water War

When Bolivia sold its city water system to a multinational consortium and bills soared, farmers, factory workers and residents rose up — and won.

When

Where
Cochabamba, Bolivia
Facts checked

01

What is the background to this event?

In the 1980s and 1990s, Bolivia, like many indebted countries, adopted free-market reforms promoted by the World Bank and the International Monetary Fund, including the privatisation of state companies.

In Cochabamba, Bolivia's fourth-largest city, water was scarce, and the public water company (SEMAPA) reached only part of the population. Many poorer neighbourhoods and farming communities relied on wells and on water systems they had built and managed themselves.

  • In September 1999 the government of President Hugo Banzer signed a 40-year concession, worth $2.5 billion, with the sole bidder, Aguas del Tunari — a consortium led by International Waters, a subsidiary of the US construction giant Bechtel, together with the British company United Utilities and Bolivian partners.1
  • The contract required the company to build a long-planned dam, which was favoured by the city's mayor, Manfred Reyes Villa.1
  • A new national water law, Law 2029, appeared to give the company a monopoly over water resources, including community systems. Many people feared that they would need a licence even to collect rainwater from their roofs.1

03

What happened?

After taking control of the water system, Aguas del Tunari raised water rates by an average of 35 per cent, to about $20 a month. For many customers who earned only around $100 a month, this was more than they spent on food.1 A manager for the consortium said simply that if people did not pay their water bills, their water would be turned off.1

A broad coalition, the Coordinadora for the Defence of Water and Life (La Coordinadora), was formed by peasant irrigators, factory workers led by Oscar Olivera, neighbourhood groups and others.1 The protests grew in stages:1

  • In January 2000, a general strike shut down Cochabamba for four days.
  • On 4 February 2000, thousands of marchers were met by police and soldiers; almost 200 demonstrators were arrested, and 70 protesters and 51 police officers were injured.
  • In March, the Coordinadora organised its own referendum: of almost 50,000 votes, 96 per cent demanded that the contract be cancelled. The government replied that "there is nothing to negotiate".
  • In April, demonstrators again occupied the city's central plaza. The leaders of the Coordinadora were arrested when they went to a meeting with the governor.

On 8 April 2000, President Banzer declared a "state of siege", suspending constitutional rights, allowing protest leaders to be detained without a warrant, and imposing a curfew.1 On the same day, an army captain was filmed firing a rifle into a crowd; the seventeen-year-old student Víctor Hugo Daza was shot in the face and killed.1

Anger erupted. The police told the consortium's executives that their safety could no longer be guaranteed, and they fled the city. On 10 April, the government signed an agreement with the Coordinadora: Aguas del Tunari would leave, Cochabamba's water system would be handed back to public control, detained protesters would be released and Law 2029 would be repealed.1

04

What happened next?

  • Back in public hands: SEMAPA returned to public control, with community representatives on its board. But it continued to face serious problems of funding, management and coverage.
  • The legal battle: Aguas del Tunari sought around $25 million in compensation from Bolivia through the World Bank's investment tribunal (ICSID). After an international campaign, the company dropped its claim in 2006 in return for a token payment.
  • A new politics: the Water War was followed by the Gas War of 2003, which forced President Gonzalo Sánchez de Lozada to resign, and in 2005 Evo Morales was elected Bolivia's first Indigenous president.
  • The dam: the Misicuni dam was eventually built by the state, and completed in 2017.
05

What do others say?

At the time

The government and the consortium argued that privatisation was the only way to finance the investment needed to extend water to the whole city, and that the protests were stirred up by political agitators and coca growers. Protesters argued that the contract was negotiated in secret, that the price rises were unaffordable and that water was a common good.

Commentators' debates

  • Was privatisation the problem? Some economists argue that the contract was badly designed — especially the requirement to build an expensive dam — rather than privatisation as such.
  • Did public control work? Researchers have pointed out that, years later, many of Cochabamba's poorest residents still lacked piped water, and that the public company struggled. Others stress the growth of community-run water systems.
  • Telling the story: some accounts have exaggerated details, such as claims that rainwater collection was banned outright; the law was ambiguous rather than explicit on this point.

07

How is it remembered?

  • The Water War is dramatised in Icíar Bollaín's film También la lluvia (Even the Rain, 2010).
  • It is celebrated each April in Cochabamba, and remains an important reference for movements against privatisation worldwide.
  • The phrase "water is life" (el agua es vida) became a slogan of water-justice campaigns across Latin America.
08

Why does it matter?

The Cochabamba Water War became a global symbol of resistance to the privatisation of essential services. Ordinary people — farmers, factory workers, students and residents — forced a government and one of the world's largest companies to back down.

  • It raised the question of whether water is a commodity or a human right — a question later addressed by the UN, which recognised the human right to water and sanitation in 2010.
  • It helped to launch a wave of social movements that transformed Bolivian politics.
  • It shows the power of broad coalitions that unite urban and rural, workers and peasants.
