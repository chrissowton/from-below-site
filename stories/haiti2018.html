"Where Did the PetroCaribe Money Go?": Haiti's Anti-Corruption Protests

A rise in fuel prices and a Senate report on missing oil money brought Haitians onto the streets. A cardboard sign asking where the money had gone became a national slogan.

When
July 2018 – 2019
Where
Port-au-Prince, Les Cayes, Jacmel, Hinche and towns across Haiti
Facts checked
2026-11-17

See it on the map →

01

What is the background to this event?

PetroCaribe was an oil agreement between Venezuela and Caribbean states, presented as a way to fund development in the region. Haiti received its first oil shipment under it in 2008.1

  • A Haitian Senate investigation, released in November 2017, found that significant corruption under the governments of René Préval and Michel Martelly (2008–2016) had been funded with Venezuelan loans through PetroCaribe.2
  • President Jovenel Moïse had come to power in a 2016 election in which only about 21% of voters took part.2

In their own words

03

What happened?

On 6 July 2018, the government announced increases in fuel prices of up to 51%. Protests and rioting followed.2

  • The government suspended the price rises, and on 14 July Prime Minister Jack Guy Lafontant resigned.2
  • In mid-August 2018, Gilbert Mirambeau Jr. posted a photograph of himself, blindfolded, holding a cardboard sign that asked in Haitian Creole where the PetroCaribe money had gone. The hashtag #PetroCaribeChallenge spread on social media, and Haitian media carried the message offline.2
  • Protests grew through the autumn, with clashes in Les Cayes, Jacmel and Saint-Marc. A week of protests in November 2018 left 10 people dead.2
  • In February 2019, after a court report on the Senate investigation, protests broke out again. The opposition, led by Jean-Charles Moïse, refused offers of dialogue and called a general strike to force the president to resign.2
  • The opposition also accused the government of involvement in a massacre in La Saline, a district of Port-au-Prince, and took its case to the UN Secretary-General.2
04

What happened next?

  • Deadlock: protests continued through 2019 and into 2021, when protesters argued that Moïse's term had ended. Between 2017 and 2021, with the government deadlocked, gangs gained power through links to politicians and business.2
  • Assassination: on 7 July 2021, President Moïse was assassinated. A group of foreign mercenaries was accused of the killing.2
  • Gangs: in 2022, gangs blockaded Haiti's largest fuel depot. In March 2024, acting Prime Minister Ariel Henry announced his resignation under pressure from protesters, gangs and foreign governments.2
05

Why does it matter?

The PetroCaribe protests showed how a single question — where did the money go? — could unite people against corruption. Their failure to bring accountability is part of the story of how Haiti's state collapsed.2

  • With the government deadlocked between 2017 and 2021, gangs gained power through links to politicians and business.2
  • President Moïse was assassinated on 7 July 2021.2
  • No one was held to account for the missing money, and its absence became part of how Haiti's state collapsed.2

At the time

President Moïse called for dialogue, arguing that the country's problems were social and economic as well as political; the opposition refused to talk until he resigned.2

Debates since

  • Who was the campaign for? Shearon Roberts, cited by Wikipedia, argues that the online campaign also signalled to the outside world that a push for a change of government was under way.2
  • Violence: deaths were counted protest by protest — for example, 10 in a week in November 2018 — and no overall official figure is given in our sources.2

Port-au-Prince, 9 September 2018

A protest demanding an account of how PetroCaribe funds were managed, taken a few weeks after Gilbert Mirambeau Jr.'s photograph launched the #PetroCaribeChallenge online. It shows the campaign moving from social media onto the streets.

Reading it

A protest demanding to know what happened to the PetroCaribe funds, weeks after one man's photograph started the online challenge. The question moved from social media to the street almost unchanged. In a poor country, oil money meant for development that seemed to vanish was an easy grievance for anyone to grasp.
