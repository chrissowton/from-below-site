The Makhnovshchina: Anarchist Ukraine

In the chaos of revolution and civil war, peasants in southern Ukraine tried to build a society without landlords, bosses or a state. They fought Germans, Whites and Reds — and were finally crushed by the Bolsheviks.

When
1917 – 1921
Where
Huliaipole and southern Ukraine

01

What is the background to this event?

In the late nineteenth century, the steppe of southern Ukraine was a grain-exporting region where land was concentrated in the hands of landowners and wealthy farmers, including many German Mennonite colonists.1

  • Poor peasants resisted land reforms that threatened their communes, and were hostile to landowners.1
  • In Huliaipole, a small town, young anarchist-communists formed a group in the early 1900s. One of them was Nestor Makhno, a peasant who spent nearly ten years in prison.1
  • After the February Revolution of 1917, Makhno returned to Huliaipole.1

In their own words

03

What happened?

In 1917, the anarchists of Huliaipole set up a peasants' union, took control of the town and established agricultural communes on land seized from landowners.1

  • When German and Austrian troops occupied Ukraine in 1918, Makhno's insurgents fought a guerrilla war against them.1
  • By 1919, the Makhnovists controlled much of Katerynoslav province. They held congresses of peasants, workers and insurgents and tried to create "free soviets" — councils without political parties.1
  • Their army helped defeat the White Army of General Denikin at the Battle of Peregonovka in September 1919, and controlled much of southern and eastern Ukraine.1

The war was brutal. The Makhnovists fought against antisemitic pogroms and executed the warlord Nykyfor Hryhoriv after his troops carried out pogroms. But insurgent units also committed atrocities, including the massacre of Mennonite colonists at Eichenfeld in October 1919, where insurgents executed the village's landowners and their adult sons.

In 1920, the Red Army turned on the Makhnovists. After a brief alliance against the Whites, the Bolsheviks attacked them again and defeated them by August 1921. Makhno fled abroad; many of his followers were killed.1

04

What happened next?

  • Defeat: Ukraine became part of the Soviet Union. Collectivisation and the famine of 1932–33 (the Holodomor) devastated the region's peasants.
  • Exile: Makhno lived in poverty in Paris, where he wrote his memoirs and helped develop anarchist ideas of organisation.
  • Legacy: the movement inspired anarchists worldwide, including in the Spanish Civil War.
05

What do others say?

At the time

The Bolsheviks portrayed Makhno as a bandit and a kulak leader. Anarchists saw him as a hero of the peasant revolution.

Historians' debates

  • Antisemitism: Makhno was accused of pogroms; historians generally find that he opposed antisemitism and punished pogromists, though some of his troops carried out attacks.
  • The Mennonites: Mennonite communities remember the Makhnovists as perpetrators of massacres; Sean Patterson has compared anarchist and Mennonite memories of the war.
  • Anarchism in practice: historians debate how far the movement really created free soviets, and how far it depended on Makhno's military leadership.

07

How is it remembered?

  • Huliaipole has a museum with exhibits about Makhno. The town has been heavily damaged in the Russian invasion of Ukraine since 2022.
  • The black flags of the Makhnovists remain symbols of anarchism.
08

Why does it matter?

The Makhnovshchina was one of the largest attempts to put anarchist ideas into practice. It shows that the Russian Revolution contained many different visions of the future.

  • It raises questions about whether a society without a state is possible in wartime.
  • It shows how revolutionary movements can also commit atrocities.

Questions to think about

  1. What did the Makhnovists mean by 'free soviets'?
  2. Why did the Bolsheviks turn against the Makhnovists?
  3. How should we judge a movement that fought pogroms but also committed massacres?
  4. Can a society without a state survive?

What other events are like this?

  • 1921The Kronstadt Rebellion — The sailors who had helped bring the Bolsheviks to power demanded free elections and free speech. The Red Army crossed the ice to crush them.
  • 1871The Paris Commune — After defeat in war, the working people of Paris took over their city and governed it for 72 days. The Commune ended in 'Bloody Week', when the army killed thousands — and Communards shot their hostages.
  • 1773Pugachev's Rebellion — A Cossack deserter claiming to be the murdered Tsar Peter III proclaimed the end of serfdom. Cossacks, serfs, factory workers and Bashkirs rose in the largest peasant war in Russian history.
