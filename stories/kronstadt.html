The Kronstadt Rebellion

The sailors who had helped bring the Bolsheviks to power demanded free elections and free speech. The Red Army crossed the ice to crush them.

When
1 – 18 March 1921
Where
Kronstadt, Kotlin Island, near Petrograd, Russia
Facts checked
2026-11-15

See it on the map →

01

What is the background to this event?

Kronstadt, a naval fortress on an island in the Gulf of Finland, was the base of the Baltic Fleet. Its sailors had been among the most radical supporters of the Russian Revolution of 1917.1

  • By early 1921, the Bolsheviks had won the civil war, but Russia was in ruins, with famine, disease and cold.1
  • Under "war communism", the state seized grain from peasants and controlled the economy. Peasant revolts broke out across the country.1
  • In January 1921, bread rations in the cities were cut by a third. Strikes and protests broke out in Petrograd, and the Cheka (secret police) made thousands of arrests.1
02

Who were the key people?

  • Stepan Petrichenko

    A sailor who chaired the rebels' Provisional Revolutionary Committee. He fled to Finland and died in a Soviet prison camp in 1947.

  • The sailors, soldiers and workers of Kronstadt

    About 26,000 sailors and soldiers and 50,000 civilians lived on the island.

  • Vladimir Lenin and Leon Trotsky

    The Bolshevik leaders. Trotsky signed the order to crush the rebellion.

  • Mikhail Tukhachevsky

    The Red Army commander who led the assault.

In their own words

03

What happened?

In late February 1921, sailors on the battleships Petropavlovsk and Sevastopol sent delegates to investigate the strikes in Petrograd. On 1 March, a mass meeting adopted the Petropavlovsk resolution, with fifteen demands.1 They included:

  • new elections to the soviets (councils) by secret ballot, with freedom to campaign;
  • freedom of speech and the press for workers, peasants, anarchists and left socialist parties;
  • the release of socialist political prisoners;
  • an end to the privileges of the Bolshevik Party, and freedom for peasants to work their land.1

The government called the rebels counter-revolutionaries backed by the Whites, and demanded unconditional surrender.1 The rebels hoped for negotiations and support from the rest of the country, which never came.1 They held Bolshevik officials prisoner but did not execute them; the authorities took rebels' relatives as hostages and shot 45 sailors at Oranienbaum.1

From 7 March, the Red Army attacked across the frozen sea. Cadets and soldiers advanced across the open ice under fire. Kronstadt fell on 18 March.1

  • The American consulate at Vyborg estimated 10,000 Red Army dead, wounded or missing.1
  • One report estimated 600 rebels killed, 1,000 wounded and 2,500 imprisoned. About 8,000 rebels fled across the ice to Finland.1
  • Hundreds of prisoners were shot after the battle, and others died in prison camps.1

04

What happened next?

  • The New Economic Policy: during the rebellion, the Bolsheviks' Tenth Party Congress replaced grain requisitioning with a tax and allowed some private trade — meeting some of the rebels' economic demands.1
  • Political repression: none of the political demands were met. Opposition parties were suppressed, and factions were banned within the Bolshevik Party itself.1
  • Exile: the refugees in Finland lived in bleak camps. Many who accepted Soviet promises of amnesty and returned were sent to prison camps.1
05

What do others say?

At the time

The Bolsheviks claimed that the rebellion was organised by White generals and foreign powers, and that a rebel Kronstadt would have opened the way to counter-revolution. The rebels and their supporters said they were fighting for the original promise of "all power to the soviets".

Historians' debates

  • White conspiracy? Historians such as Paul Avrich found no evidence that the Whites organised the rising, though émigré groups hoped to exploit it. Petrichenko and some other leaders later had contacts with White émigrés in Finland.1
  • Were the sailors the same? Trotsky later argued that the revolutionary sailors of 1917 had been replaced by peasant recruits. Research suggests that most of the sailors in 1921 were veterans of 1917.1
  • Necessity? Some historians argue that the Bolsheviks had to act before the ice melted and the island became impregnable; others see the repression as a turning point towards dictatorship.

07

How is it remembered?

  • In 1994, President Boris Yeltsin issued a decree rehabilitating the Kronstadt rebels and condemning the repression.
  • Kronstadt remains a powerful symbol in anarchist and left-wing debates about revolution and freedom.
08

Why does it matter?

The Kronstadt rebellion was a revolt from below against a revolutionary government, by the very people who had helped bring it to power. It became a symbol for socialists and anarchists who opposed one-party rule.

  • It raises questions about whether revolutions can protect freedom and democracy.
  • It led many left-wing supporters of the Soviet Union, such as the anarchist Emma Goldman, to break with it.1
  • It shows how governments can label protesters as foreign agents to justify repression.

09

What other events are like this?

  • 1871The Paris Commune — After defeat in war, the working people of Paris took over their city and governed it for 72 days. The Commune ended in 'Bloody Week', when the army killed thousands — and Communards shot their hostages.
  • 1962The Novocherkassk Strike and Massacre — When the price of meat and butter went up on the same day that their pay was cut, Soviet locomotive workers went on strike. Soldiers opened fire, and the state kept the massacre secret for thirty years.
  • 1956The Hungarian Revolution of 1956 — Students, workers and soldiers rose against Hungary's Soviet-backed dictatorship and pulled down Stalin's statue. For twelve days they won — until Soviet tanks returned.
