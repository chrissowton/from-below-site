The Gaelic League: Saving the Irish Language

By the 1890s, only a tiny fraction of Irish children were being raised speaking Irish. A new league set out to save the language — and grew to 600 branches and 50,000 members within ten years.

When
1893 – 1915
Where
Dublin and across Ireland
Facts checked

See it on the map →

01

What is the background to this event?

Irish had been the language of most people in Ireland. By the late 19th century, it seemed close to dying out as a spoken language.1

  • Census figures suggested that at least 45% of people born in Ireland in the first decade of the 19th century had been brought up speaking Irish; by 1891, just 3.5% of children were.1
  • Irish survived mainly among poor farmers and labourers in the west. In schools, children could be punished for speaking it.1
  • In November 1892, the scholar Douglas Hyde gave a lecture on "The Necessity for De-Anglicising Ireland".1

In their own words

03

What happened?

The Gaelic League, Conradh na Gaeilge, was founded in 1893, with Douglas Hyde as its president.1

  • Its first aim was to keep Irish alive where it was still spoken. It ran Irish classes and published in Irish.1
  • Hyde argued that the language belonged to everyone: "neither Protestant nor Catholic... neither a Unionist nor a Separatist." The League even attracted some unionists.1
  • Membership grew fast. Branches rose from 43 in 1897 to 600 in 1904, with 50,000 members.1
  • In 1903, the League opened the first Irish-language summer college, and in 1904 it won a bilingual programme for schools in Irish-speaking districts.1

04

What happened next?

  • Politics: despite Hyde's hopes, the League became bound up with nationalism. From 1915, members of its executive accepted the leadership of the Irish Republican Brotherhood.1
  • After independence: many members put their efforts into the new state's institutions, parties and schools.1
  • Today: the League still campaigns for Irish speakers' rights. In Northern Ireland it campaigned for an Irish Language Act; many of its proposals became law in 2022.1
05

What do others say?

At the time

Hyde argued that by Anglicising themselves, the Irish had thrown away their best claim to be a nation. Earlier, the nationalist leader Daniel O'Connell had said he did not regret the language's gradual abandonment.1

Debates since

  • Who is Irish? Critics argue that calling Gaelic 'the Irish language' implied that those who did not speak it were less Irish.1
  • Success or failure? The League made Irish a national cause, but it did not restore it as the everyday language of most people.1

How is it remembered?

  • Conradh na Gaeilge still exists, with more than 150 branches, and runs classes, a bookshop and programmes for schools.1
08

Why does it matter?

The Gaelic League turned a language in steep decline into a mass movement run by ordinary people in classes and branches across Ireland. It also shows how language revival can become tied to national politics.1

Questions to think about
