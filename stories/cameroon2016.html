Lawyers and Teachers: Cameroon's Anglophone Protests

English-speaking lawyers and teachers went on strike against the use of French in their courts and schools. The government banned their organisations and arrested their leaders, and within a year the protests had turned into an armed conflict.

When
October 2016 – October 2017
Where

Ongoing situationLast reviewed 17 November 2026

Wikipedia describes the armed conflict in the English-speaking regions as ongoing.1 Estimates of the death toll vary widely and are disputed; the war has forced hundreds of thousands of people from their homes.

Still unresolved:

  • Whether talks between the government and separatist groups resume
  • Whether schools in the two regions can reopen safely
  • Whether anyone is held to account for killings of civilians by either side

This is recent history. Accounts, casualty figures and outcomes may change as more evidence emerges.

01

What is the background to this event?

Cameroon is mostly French-speaking, but its Northwest and Southwest Regions were once the British-ruled Southern Cameroons and use English and the common law.1

  • In a UN plebiscite in February 1961, about 70% of voters in the Southern Cameroons chose union with Cameroon rather than Nigeria. The territory joined a federation as "West Cameroon", with its own prime minister.1
  • After a referendum in May 1972, a new constitution replaced the federation with a unitary state and gave more power to the president.1
  • Many English-speakers came to feel that their legal and education systems were being eroded. This grievance became known as the "Anglophone problem".21

In their own words

03

What happened?

On 6 October 2016, common law lawyers began a sit-down strike in the courts, protesting against the appointment of French-speaking judges in English-speaking areas.2

  • Lawyers marched in Bamenda, Buea and Limbe, calling for the common law to be used in the region's courts and for laws to be translated into English. Security forces used tear gas and were accused of assaulting protesters and lawyers.2
  • In November 2016, thousands of teachers joined the lawyers, protesting against the use of French in English-speaking schools. Schools across the two regions closed.2
  • On 23 November 2016, at least two people were reported killed and 100 protesters arrested in Bamenda. Within two weeks, more than 100 activists had reportedly been arrested; six deaths were reported.2
  • In his New Year message, President Biya said the protesters had raised real issues and called for dialogue, but warned that Cameroon was "one and indivisible".2
  • Talks broke down. The Consortium put forward a proposal for a federation; on 17 January 2017, the government banned the Consortium and the Southern Cameroons National Council. Agbor Balla and Fontem Neba were arrested, and the internet was cut off in the two regions.25
  • The leaders were tried before a military court on charges including terrorism and treason. On 31 August 2017, a presidential decree ordered their release.5
  • By then, many protesters were demanding independence. On 22 September 2017, thousands marched; about eight people were reportedly killed. On 1 October, separatists declared the independence of "Ambazonia", and soldiers were sent to the two regions. Amnesty International reported at least 17 people killed.2
04

What happened next?

  • War: the protests escalated into an armed conflict between the army and separatist groups. More than half a million people have been forced to flee their homes.1
  • Deaths: in September 2019, independent sources put the death toll at around 3,000, counting combatants and civilians. Federalist groups put it at 3,000–5,000, and separatists at 5,000–10,000.1
  • Schools: by July 2019, close to 6,000 schools had shut, affecting more than 600,000 children. Separatist gunmen attacked schools to enforce a boycott.1
  • Abuses on both sides: in February 2020, soldiers and armed Fulani killed at least 23 civilians at Ngarbuh; separatists have been accused of kidnappings and killings.1
05

What do others say?

At the time

Protesters argued that the English-speaking regions were being marginalised; the government argued that the protests threatened national unity.2

Debates since

  • Federalism or independence? The Consortium proposed a return to a federation; separatists demanded full independence.21
  • Violence: all death tolls are estimates, and they vary depending on who produced them: from around 3,000 (independent sources, 2019) to 10,000 (separatists).1

07

How is it remembered?

  • Separatists use the name "Ambazonia" for the English-speaking regions, and fly its blue-and-white striped flag.2
08

Why does it matter?

The Anglophone protests began with lawyers and teachers defending a language and a legal tradition. The way the government met them, with bans, arrests and internet shutdowns, helped turn a peaceful movement into a war.21

Open questions

  1. Why did lawyers and teachers lead these protests?
  2. Why do language and legal systems matter so much to people's sense of identity?
  3. How did the government's response change what protesters were asking for?

What other events are like this?

  • 1955Cameroon's Hidden War — When France banned Cameroon's main independence party, its supporters took to the forests. The war that followed killed tens of thousands — and France only acknowledged it in 2025.
  • 1952The Bengali Language Movement — Students in Dhaka defied a ban on protest to demand that Bengali be a national language of Pakistan. Police opened fire. The date is now International Mother Language Day.
  • 2015The Oromo Protests — A plan to expand Ethiopia's capital into the farmland around it set off years of protests among the Oromo, the country's largest people. Hundreds were killed — and in 2018 the prime minister resigned.
