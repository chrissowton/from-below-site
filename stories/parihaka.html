Peace & anti-war

Parihaka

On land taken by the colonial government, two Māori leaders built a thriving village and led a campaign of peaceful resistance: ploughing settlers' fields and rebuilding fences. In 1881, 1,600 troops marched in and were greeted by singing children.

When
1866 – 1881
Where
Parihaka, Taranaki, Aotearoa New Zealand

See it on the map →

01

What is the background to this event?

During and after the New Zealand Wars of the 1860s, the colonial government confiscated large areas of Māori land in Taranaki.1

  • In about 1866, the chiefs Te Whiti o Rongomai and Tohu Kākahi founded Parihaka on confiscated land.1
  • It grew to more than 2,000 people, attracting Māori who had lost their land, and impressed visitors with its farming and order.1
02

Who were the key people?

  • Te Whiti o Rongomai

    Prophet and leader of Parihaka; jailed without trial.

  • Tohu Kākahi

    Prophet and leader of Parihaka; jailed with Te Whiti.

  • The ploughmen and fencers

    Men of Parihaka arrested for peaceful protest.

  • John Bryce

    Native Minister who led the invasion of Parihaka.

03

What happened?

Parihaka became the centre of a campaign of non-violent resistance to the occupation of confiscated land.1

  • From 1879, men from Parihaka ploughed land that settlers had occupied, and later rebuilt fences across roads built through Māori land.
  • The protest was aimed at the government, not the settlers, but farmers threatened to shoot the ploughmen.1
  • Hundreds of protesters were arrested and many were held for months without trial, some in Dunedin jail far away in the South Island.1
  • At dawn on 5 November 1881, 1,600 troops and cavalry entered the village. They were met by hundreds of singing children offering them food.1
  • Te Whiti and Tohu were arrested and jailed for 16 months; 1,600 residents were expelled without food or shelter.1

04

What happened next?

  • Redress: between 2001 and 2006, the government apologised and paid redress to four Taranaki tribes for Parihaka and the confiscations.1
  • Apology: in June 2017, the Crown formally apologised to the Parihaka community, calling the events among the most shameful in the country's history.1
05

What do others say?

At the time

Settlers and politicians called the protesters dangerous fanatics; a few European visitors admired Parihaka.1

Debates since

  • Non-violence: historians compare Parihaka with Gandhi's later campaigns, though there is no proof of a direct link.2
  • Violence: the people of Parihaka did not use violence; the colonial state used armed force, imprisonment without trial and the destruction of the village.1
06

The evidence

  • Government records and newspaper reports from 1879–81.2
  • Parihaka's own oral traditions.
07

How is it remembered?

  • Parihaka holds monthly gatherings and an annual peace festival.
  • Dick Scott's book Ask That Mountain (1975) brought the story to a wide audience.
08

Why does it matter?

Parihaka was one of the world's earliest organised campaigns of non-violent resistance to colonialism.

  • It showed how peaceful protest could expose the violence of a colonial state.

Questions to think about

  1. Why did Te Whiti and Tohu choose non-violence?
  2. Why was ploughing such a powerful form of protest?
  3. Why did the government hold protesters without trial?
  4. Why did it take until 2017 for the Crown to apologise?
