The Carnation Revolution

Young officers overthrew Europe's oldest dictatorship with barely a shot fired. Then the people took to the streets with carnations — and kept going, occupying land, factories and homes.

When
25 April 1974 – November 1975
Where
Lisbon, Portugal
Facts checked
2026-11-16

See it on the map →

01

What is the background to this event?

From 1932, Portugal was ruled by the Estado Novo dictatorship of António de Oliveira Salazar, and after 1968 by Marcelo Caetano.1

  • The political police, the PIDE, persecuted opponents, who were often tortured, imprisoned or killed.1
  • From 1961, Portugal fought colonial wars against independence movements in Angola, Mozambique and Guinea-Bissau.1 By the 1970s, the wars consumed a huge share of the budget, and young men faced years of conscription.
  • Many junior officers, who had fought in Africa, came to oppose the wars and the regime.
02

Who were the key people?

  • The Armed Forces Movement (MFA)

    A group of junior officers, such as Captain Salgueiro Maia, who organised the coup.

  • Celeste Caeiro

    A restaurant worker who handed carnations to soldiers, giving the revolution its name.

  • Workers, tenants and farm labourers

    Who occupied factories, empty homes and estates during 1974–75.

  • Zeca Afonso

    The singer whose song 'Grândola, Vila Morena', broadcast on the radio, was a signal for the coup.

In their own words

03

What happened?

Shortly after midnight on 25 April 1974, a radio station played the banned song "Grândola, Vila Morena" — the signal for the coup. Officers of the Armed Forces Movement occupied key points in Lisbon and forced Caetano to surrender.

  • Despite appeals to stay at home, thousands of people poured into the streets. Celeste Caeiro offered carnations to soldiers, and others placed them in the muzzles of guns.1
  • Almost no shots were fired by the soldiers.1 However, PIDE agents fired on a crowd outside their headquarters, killing four people.
  • The coup was soon accompanied by an unexpected popular movement.1

In the following months — the "Revolutionary Process" — workers occupied factories and set up workers' committees; landless labourers occupied large estates in the Alentejo; tenants occupied empty homes; and neighbourhood committees sprang up. Banks and major industries were nationalised.

04

What happened next?

  • Decolonisation: by 1975, Guinea-Bissau, Cape Verde, Mozambique, São Tomé and Príncipe and Angola were independent.1 Over a million Portuguese settlers returned from Africa as retornados.1 In Angola and Mozambique, civil wars followed.
  • The "hot summer": in 1975, Portugal was deeply divided between radical left and conservative forces; offices of left-wing parties were burned in the north. On 25 November 1975, moderate officers ended the revolutionary period.
  • Democracy: a new constitution was adopted in 1976, and Portugal became a parliamentary democracy. It joined the European Community in 1986.
05

What do others say?

At the time

Conservatives feared Portugal would become communist; the radical left hoped for a socialist revolution. The moderate parties sought a Western-style democracy.

Historians' debates

  • Coup or revolution? Historians such as Raquel Varela emphasise the role of workers' committees and occupations; others focus on the officers and parties.
  • Decolonisation: some criticise the speed of decolonisation, which left Angola in civil war; others argue that the liberation movements had already won the right to independence.
  • Violence: the revolution in Portugal was mostly peaceful, but the "hot summer" of 1975 saw bombings and attacks by both left and right.

07

How is it remembered?

  • 25 April is Freedom Day, a national holiday in Portugal.1
  • The 50th anniversary in 2024 was marked by huge celebrations, with carnations everywhere.
  • The Aljube Museum in Lisbon, a former political prison, tells the story of resistance to the dictatorship.
08

Why does it matter?

The Carnation Revolution ended Europe's longest dictatorship and Portugal's colonial empire. It began a wave of democratisation across southern Europe and the world.1

  • It shows how the people can transform a military coup into a social revolution.
  • It was largely peaceful, though the decolonisation it brought was followed by bloody wars in Africa.

09

What other events are like this?

  • 1960The Mueda Massacre — Makonde farmers gathered peacefully to ask the Portuguese for better prices and independence. Soldiers opened fire. The massacre convinced many that only armed struggle could end colonial rule.
  • 1961The Baixa de Cassanje Revolt — Angolan farmers forced to grow cotton for a colonial company refused to work and burned their identity cards. Portugal answered with air raids — and within weeks Angola's war of independence had begun.
  • 1973The Athens Polytechnic Uprising — Students occupied their university and broadcast a call for 'Bread, Education, Freedom' against Greece's military dictatorship. Before dawn on the third day, a tank crashed through the gates.
