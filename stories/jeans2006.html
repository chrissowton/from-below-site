The Jeans Revolution

When Alexander Lukashenko claimed 83% of the vote, young Belarusians pitched tents in central Minsk and wore denim as a symbol of freedom. After a week, riot police cleared the camp — this colour revolution failed.

When
19 – 25 March 2006
Where
October Square, Minsk, Belarus
Facts checked

01

What is the background to this event?

By 2006, Alexander Lukashenko had ruled Belarus for twelve years. Term limits had been removed in a 2004 referendum that was not judged free and fair internationally.1

  • His government had been widely criticised for crushing dissent and restricting civil society.1
  • In September 2005, when police seized the banned white-red-white flags at a protest, a youth activist of the Zubr movement raised his denim shirt instead. Denim became a symbol of protest, and of Belarusians' wish not to be cut off from the West.2
  • Before the election, opposition activists were arrested. The candidate Alexander Kozulin was briefly arrested and beaten on 2 March 2006.3

In their own words

03

What happened?

On 19 March 2006, the official result gave Lukashenko about 83% of the vote. Observers from the OSCE said the election did not meet democratic standards.3

  • As soon as the polls closed, more than 10,000 people gathered on October Square in Minsk.23
  • Each evening the crowd was smaller. By 23 March, about 200 mostly young protesters remained in a tent camp on the square.2
  • Early on 24 March, riot police cleared the camp and arrested hundreds of people.3
  • On 25 March, a much larger crowd — about 45,000, according to one account — confronted the police. There were clashes, and the protesters were driven back. More than 100 people were arrested, including Kozulin.2

04

What happened next?

  • Prison: on 14 July 2006, Kozulin was sentenced to five and a half years in prison.2
  • Lukashenko stayed: the Jeans Revolution brought no radical change to Belarusian politics.2 Larger protests after the 2020 election were also crushed.
05

What do others say?

At the time

Lukashenko said the revolution "that so many people talked about" had failed, as it could only have done. The opposition said the election had been stolen.2

Debates since

  • Why it failed: possible reasons include the loyalty of the security forces, state control of the media and the arrests of opposition activists before the vote. Compare Ukraine, where troops pulled back and parts of the media were free.
  • Violence: state television reported that no one was hurt when the camp was cleared; the opposition reported beatings, and Kozulin was allegedly assaulted during his arrest.2

How is it remembered?

  • Zubr activists suggested wearing jeans on the 16th of each month to remember people who had disappeared in Belarus.2
08

Why does it matter?

The Jeans Revolution shows that the colour revolutions did not always succeed. Where security forces stayed loyal and the government controlled the media, peaceful protest could be contained.12

Questions to think about

  1. Why did the protests in Minsk fail when those in Kyiv succeeded?
  2. Why did denim become a symbol of protest?
  3. How did the government make it hard for the opposition to campaign?
  4. What can failed protests teach us?

What other events are like this?

  • 2020The Belarusian Protests of 2020 — When Alexander Lukashenko claimed 80 per cent of the vote after 26 years in power, Belarusians came out in the largest protests in their history. Three women led the challenge. Tens of thousands were arrested.
  • 2004The Orange Revolution — When a presidential run-off was rigged, hundreds of thousands of Ukrainians camped in the snow in central Kyiv for weeks. The Supreme Court annulled the result, and the election was held again.
  • 1988Kurapaty and the Belarusian Memory Movement — In 1988, a historian revealed mass graves of Stalin's victims in a forest near Minsk. The discovery sparked Belarus's democracy movement, and young people later camped through a winter to stop a road being built over the graves.
