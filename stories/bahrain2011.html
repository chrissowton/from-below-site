The Pearl Roundabout Uprising

Inspired by Tunisia and Egypt, Bahrainis camped at the Pearl Roundabout demanding political freedom and equality. A month later, troops from Saudi Arabia and the UAE crossed the causeway, and the monument itself was torn down.

When
February – March 2011
Where
Pearl Roundabout, Manama, Bahrain

See it on the map →

01

What is the background to this event?

Bahrain is ruled by the Al Khalifa monarchy, while about 70% of its citizens are Shia Muslims.1

  • Many Shia Bahrainis felt excluded from power and jobs.
  • An uprising in the 1990s had ended with reforms under King Hamad.1
  • In 2011, the Arab Spring in Tunisia and Egypt inspired a new wave of protest.1
02

03

What happened?

From 14 February 2011, protesters demanded greater political freedom and equality for the Shia majority.1

  • They camped at the Pearl Roundabout in Manama, which became the centre of the protests.1
  • On 17 February, a deadly night raid on the camp ("Bloody Thursday") led to calls for the end of the monarchy.1
  • On 14 March, 1,000 troops from Saudi Arabia and 500 from the UAE entered Bahrain under the Gulf Cooperation Council.1
  • The king declared martial law; the roundabout was cleared and its monument demolished.1

04

What happened next?

  • Repression: more than 2,900 people were arrested, and at least five died from torture in custody.1
  • Inquiry: the Bahrain Independent Commission of Inquiry found that security agencies had systematically abused detainees, in many cases amounting to torture.1
  • Continuing protest: protests and clashes continued until around 2014.1
05

What do others say?

At the time

The government accused Iran of stirring sectarian unrest; protesters said they wanted democracy and equal rights for all.1

Debates since

  • Sectarian or democratic? The opposition was mainly Shia but included Sunnis; the government framed it as a sectarian threat.1
  • Violence on both sides: the protests were largely non-violent civil disobedience, with some violent resistance; security forces killed protesters and tortured detainees, and police officers were also killed in later clashes.1

07

How is it remembered?

  • The demolished Pearl Monument remains a powerful symbol for Bahraini opposition supporters.
08

Why does it matter?

The Pearl Roundabout uprising shows how regional powers helped crush an Arab Spring movement.

What other events are like this?

  • 1954Bahrain's National Union Committee — After clashes between Sunni and Shia Bahrainis, reformers from both communities came together to demand an elected assembly, trade unions and an end to British control. Their leaders were exiled to a remote Atlantic island.
  • 2011Yemen's Revolution and Change Square — Young Yemenis built a tent city outside Sanaa University and demanded the end of 33 years of rule by Ali Abdullah Saleh. A journalist and mother of three, Tawakkol Karman, became the first Arab woman to win the Nobel Peace Prize.
  • 2010The Tunisian Revolution — A street vendor's desperate protest in a small town sparked a month of uprising that toppled a dictator of 23 years and began the Arab Spring.
