The Great Banana Strike

In the original 'banana republic', 40,000 workers walked out on the American fruit companies. After 69 days, they won higher pay and the right to a union.

When
April – July 1954
Where
Tela, San Pedro Sula and the north coast of Honduras

01

What is the background to this event?

From the early twentieth century, Honduras's economy was dominated by American banana companies — the United Fruit Company and the Standard Fruit Company. They owned vast plantations, railways and ports on the north coast and had enormous influence over the government.

  • Unions were banned, and workers lived in company towns under company control.
  • In 1954, President Juan Manuel Gálvez, a former United Fruit lawyer, surprised many by introducing pro-labour measures, including an eight-hour day and extra pay for holiday work.1
  • In neighbouring Guatemala, the Árbenz government's land reform was challenging United Fruit.

03

What happened?

In April 1954, banana workers employed by United Fruit began a wildcat strike in Tela, mainly over pay.1

  • In May, dockworkers in Puerto Cortés demanded double pay for holiday work, as the law required. When their leader was dismissed, they went on strike.1
  • The strike spread to all of United Fruit's workers — some 25,000 — and then to 15,000 workers of Standard Fruit.1
  • Industrial workers in San Pedro Sula also joined, making it a remarkable labour mobilisation.1
  • The strikers organised committees to keep order and distribute food; local businesses secretly gave money to support them.1

The US Secretary of State, John Foster Dulles, claimed that Guatemalan communists were behind the strike.1 But the United States, worried about events in Guatemala, also pressured the companies and the government to settle.1

After 69 days, the strike ended in July. Wages rose substantially, and the banana workers' union was recognised.1

04

What happened next?

  • Unions: the strike gave birth to the Honduran labour movement. Banana workers' unions became among the strongest in Central America.
  • Labour code: the government incorporated some of the workers' demands into reforms, and a labour code was adopted in 1959.1
  • The Guatemala coup: in June 1954, while the strike was still going on, the CIA-backed invasion of Guatemala was launched partly from Honduran territory.
05

What do others say?

At the time

The companies and the US government claimed the strike was communist-inspired. The strikers said it was about wages and dignity.

Historians' debates

  • Communist influence: scholars show that the Honduran Communist Party was as much a product of the strike as its cause, and that many groups, including the Liberal Party, supported it.1
  • Why did the companies give in? Some historians stress the strength of the workers; others US fears of communism spreading from Guatemala.1
  • The strike was largely peaceful, with strikers keeping discipline; historians contrast it with violent repression elsewhere in the region.

07

How is it remembered?

  • The strike is commemorated by Honduran unions as the birth of the country's labour movement.
  • The novelist Ramón Amaya Amador's Prisión verde (1950) depicted the lives of banana workers before the strike.
08

Why does it matter?

The 1954 strike was a watershed in Honduran history. Workers in the original "banana republic" stood up to some of the most powerful corporations in the world — and won.

  • It shows the power of united action across workplaces.
  • It raises questions about the power of multinational corporations in poorer countries.

Questions to think about

  1. Why did banana workers have so little power before 1954?
  2. Why do you think the strike spread so quickly?
  3. Why did the US government both denounce the strike and push for a settlement?
  4. How much power should multinational companies have in a country?

What other events are like this?

  • 1944Guatemala's 'Ten Years of Spring' — Students, teachers and workers brought down a dictator. A decade of democracy and land reform followed — until a US-backed coup ended it.
  • 1937The Butler Riots: Trinidad's Oilfield Strike — Oil workers led by the preacher 'Buzz' Butler struck against low wages and racism. The violence that followed killed police and strikers alike — and the island's labour movement was born.
  • 1907The Santa María School Massacre — Thousands of striking nitrate miners and their families were shot down in and around a school in Iquique — one of the worst massacres of workers in Latin American history.
