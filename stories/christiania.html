Freetown Christiania

In 1971, squatters climbed into an abandoned army barracks in the middle of Copenhagen and declared a free town. Half a century later, after battles with police and the state, its residents own the land.

When
1971 – present
Where
Christianshavn, Copenhagen, Denmark
Facts checked

See it on the map →

01

What is the background to this event?

Around 1970, Copenhagen had a serious shortage of affordable housing, especially for young people.

  • The army left the Bådsmandsstræde barracks in Christianshavn, an area of 7.7 hectares of buildings and old ramparts.1
  • Local residents broke through the fence to make a playground; squatters soon followed.
  • The provo and hippie movements were experimenting with new ways of living together.

In their own words

03

What happened?

On 26 September 1971, Jacob Ludvigsen declared Christiania open in his magazine, Hovedbladet.1

  • Hundreds of people moved in, repairing buildings and building their own homes.
  • Residents made decisions by consensus at common meetings, and wrote their own rules — including bans on hard drugs, weapons and cars.
  • In 1972, the government allowed Christiania to continue as a "social experiment".
  • Governments later tried to close it, but residents resisted with protests, theatre and legal challenges.
  • In 1989, the Danish parliament passed a law legalising Christiania.1

04

What happened next?

  • Ownership: after a long conflict with the state, residents agreed in 2011 to set up a fund to buy the land, and made the first payment in 2012.1
  • Pusher Street: the open cannabis trade drew gangs and violence. In 2024, residents and police together shut down much of the trade, and the street was dug up.1
  • Housing: Christiania has agreed to build some public housing on its land.
05

What do others say?

At the time

Critics saw a lawless drug haven on public land; supporters saw a creative social experiment and affordable housing.2

Debates since

  • Violence: Christiania has seen clashes with police during raids, including stones and fireworks thrown by residents in 2007, and police use of tear gas; gang shootings linked to the cannabis trade killed and injured people, including in 2005 and 2016.1
  • Normalisation: some residents fear that legal ownership has turned the free town into an ordinary neighbourhood.

How is it remembered?

  • Christiania is one of Copenhagen's best-known sights, with around half a million visitors a year.1
08

Why does it matter?

Christiania is one of the longest-lasting squats and self-governing communities in Europe.

  • It shows that people can build and run a neighbourhood themselves.
  • It also shows the problems of a free space: crime, conflict and the struggle to keep its ideals.

Questions to think about

  1. Why did people occupy an army barracks?
  2. How does a community govern itself without a mayor or police?
  3. Why did the open cannabis trade become a problem for the residents themselves?
  4. Did buying the land mean Christiania had won, or changed?

What other events are like this?

  • 1946The Squatters of 1946 — Returning from war to find no homes, tens of thousands of families moved into empty army camps across Britain. In London, Communist activists led squatters into empty luxury flats.
  • 2014Brazil's Homeless Workers' Movement (MTST) — In a country with millions of families in inadequate housing and millions of empty homes, the MTST occupies unused land and buildings to demand housing — sometimes in tent cities of thousands of families.
  • 1971Gardarem lo Larzac: Farmers against the Army — When the French army announced it would take their land for a military camp, 103 sheep farmers swore an oath not to sell. Ten years of non-violent resistance followed — and they won.
