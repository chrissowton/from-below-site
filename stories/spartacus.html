Spartacus and the Slave Revolt

Seventy gladiators broke out of a training school armed with kitchen knives. Within two years, an army of some 120,000 enslaved people had defeated Rome's legions again and again.

When
73 – 71 BCE
Where
Capua and southern Italy
Facts checked
2026-11-16

See it on the map →

01

What is the background to this event?

By the first century BCE, the Roman Republic had conquered most of the Mediterranean world, and its wars had brought hundreds of thousands of captives to Italy as slaves. Enslaved people worked as household servants and craftsmen, but vast numbers laboured in mines and on the great agricultural estates of southern Italy and Sicily, often in brutal conditions.1

  • Enslaved people had already risen in two great revolts in Sicily, in 135 and 104 BCE — the First and Second Servile Wars — which had taken years to suppress.1
  • Gladiatorial games were a popular entertainment. In training schools (ludi) across Italy, prisoners of war and condemned criminals were taught to fight, often to the death, for the crowd's pleasure.1
  • Rome's best armies were fighting abroad in the 70s BCE, against rebels in Spain and against King Mithridates in the east.

In their own words

03

What happened?

In 73 BCE, around 200 gladiators at the training school of Lentulus Batiatus in Capua plotted to escape. When their plot was betrayed, about 70 of them seized knives and spits from the kitchen, fought their way out and captured wagons carrying gladiators' weapons.12 They chose as their leaders Spartacus and two Gauls, Crixus and Oenomaus. The Greek writer Plutarch described Spartacus as:

"…a Thracian of nomadic stock, possessed not only of great courage and strength, but also in sagacity and culture superior to his fortune, and more Hellenic than Thracian."Plutarch, Life of Crassus, trans. Bernadotte Perrin (1916)3

The rebels took refuge on Mount Vesuvius. When a Roman force under the praetor Gaius Claudius Glaber besieged them, they made ropes and ladders from wild vines, climbed down the cliffs on the far side of the mountain, and attacked and destroyed Glaber's army from behind.1 Enslaved herdsmen and farm workers flocked to join them:12

  • Over the next two years, the rebels defeated a series of Roman armies, including both consuls of 72 BCE.
  • The rebel army grew to some 120,000 men, women and children. It raided estates and towns, sometimes in separate bands under different leaders. Crixus and his followers split from the main army and were destroyed.
  • The rebels marched north towards the Alps, where they could have left Italy, but then turned back south. The ancient sources disagree about why.

In 72 BCE the Senate gave command to Marcus Licinius Crassus, with eight legions. According to Plutarch, Crassus restored discipline by reviving the ancient punishment of decimation — executing one in every ten men of units that had fled.23 Spartacus tried to cross to Sicily with the help of Cilician pirates, but they took his payment and abandoned him.2 Trapped in the toe of Italy, the rebels broke through Crassus's lines but were finally defeated in 71 BCE, near the river Silarus in Lucania. Spartacus died fighting; his body was never found.12

About 6,000 captured rebels were crucified along the Appian Way, the road from Capua to Rome.12

04

What happened next?

  • Political rewards: Pompey, returning from Spain, destroyed a band of fleeing rebels and claimed credit for ending the war, to Crassus's fury. The two men used their armies and their prestige to win the consulship of 70 BCE — a step on the road to the end of the Republic.
  • No more great revolts: the Third Servile War was the last great slave war in Roman history. Slavery itself continued for centuries.
  • Changing slavery? Some historians suggest that, over the following centuries, Roman landowners relied somewhat less on large gangs of enslaved workers and more on tenant farmers, though the reasons were many.
05

What do others say?

The sources

No account of the revolt was written by an eyewitness, or by an enslaved or formerly enslaved person. The main surviving accounts were written by Plutarch and Appian more than a century later, using earlier histories (including Sallust's) that survive only in fragments.2

Historians' debates

  • Did Spartacus want to end slavery? No ancient writer claims that he aimed to abolish slavery. Plutarch suggests the rebels simply wanted to escape Italy to freedom; Appian and Florus present it as a war in which they meant to take Rome.
  • Why turn back from the Alps? Historians have suggested that the rebels feared the mountains, wanted to keep raiding Italy, or were divided among themselves.
  • Hero or bandit? Roman writers sometimes admired Spartacus's courage while condemning the rebels' violence. Modern writers have often idealised him; historians such as Barry Strauss try to see him in his own time.
06

The evidence

  • Plutarch's Life of Crassus and Appian's Civil Wars, together with shorter accounts by Florus, Orosius and others, and fragments of Sallust's Histories.
  • A wall-painting from Pompeii showing a horseman labelled "Spartaks", which some scholars think may refer to him.
  • Archaeological evidence of slavery and gladiatorial schools in Campania.

07

How is it remembered?

  • In the eighteenth century, the French writer Guillaume Raynal called for a "black Spartacus" to free the enslaved of the Caribbean — a role many later saw in Toussaint Louverture.
  • Karl Marx, in a letter of 1861, called Spartacus one of the finest figures in ancient history; in 1916 Rosa Luxemburg and Karl Liebknecht named their revolutionary Spartacus League after him.
  • Howard Fast's novel Spartacus (1951) became Stanley Kubrick's film (1960); Aram Khachaturian's ballet Spartacus (1956) and many television series have followed.
08

Why does it matter?

The revolt of Spartacus is the most famous rebellion of enslaved people in the ancient world. People whom Roman society treated as property organised themselves, defeated professional armies and held out for two years against the most powerful state in the Mediterranean.

  • It shows the courage and capacity for organisation of people at the very bottom of Roman society.
  • Spartacus became a universal symbol of resistance to oppression — for abolitionists, socialists and revolutionaries, and in novels, films and ballet.
  • His story raises questions about how we can know the history of people who left no records of their own.

Questions to think about

  1. Why do you think so many enslaved people joined the revolt so quickly?
  2. How can we know what Spartacus wanted, when all our sources were written by Romans long after his death?
  3. Why did Rome punish the captured rebels in such a public and brutal way?
  4. Why do you think Spartacus has been such a powerful symbol for later movements?

What other events are like this?

  • 1791The Haitian Revolution — The only revolution in history in which enslaved people overthrew their enslavers and founded an independent state.
  • 1605Palmares: The Free Kingdom in the Hills — For almost a century, people who had escaped slavery built a free society in the hills of north-east Brazil and resisted repeated attempts to destroy it.
  • 869The Zanj Rebellion — Enslaved East Africans forced to dig salt-crusted marshland in southern Iraq rose against the Abbasid Caliphate, built their own state and held out for fourteen years.
