Civil rights & anti-racism

The Mahad Satyagraha

B. R. Ambedkar led thousands of Dalits to drink from a public water tank that caste Hindus had forbidden them to touch — a founding moment of the struggle against untouchability.

When
20 March 1927
Where
Mahad, Maharashtra, India
Facts checked

01

What is the background to this event?

Under the Hindu caste system, millions of people were treated as "untouchable". Today they call themselves Dalits ("broken" or "oppressed"). They were segregated from caste Hindus and subjected to severe discrimination:1

  • Dalits were banned from using public wells, water tanks and roads used by caste Hindus, and were excluded from temples and many schools.
  • They were restricted to the most menial and stigmatised work, and could be beaten or punished for "polluting" caste Hindus.

In August 1923, the Bombay Legislative Council passed a resolution, moved by the social reformer S. K. Bole, declaring that the "depressed classes" should be allowed to use public places built and maintained by the government, including water sources. In January 1924, the municipality of Mahad, a town in the Konkan region, resolved to apply this to its public tank, the Chavdar Tale. But caste Hindus prevented Dalits from using it, and the resolution was never put into practice.1

Bhimrao Ramji Ambedkar, born into the Mahar caste, had overcome enormous obstacles to study at Columbia University and the London School of Economics. In 1924 he founded the Bahishkrit Hitakarini Sabha (Society for the Welfare of the Excluded) to organise Dalits.

02

Who were the key people?

  • B. R. Ambedkar

    A lawyer, economist and leader of the Dalit movement, who led the satyagraha. He later chaired the committee that drafted India's constitution.

  • Surendranath Tipnis

    The president of Mahad municipality, who declared its public places open to untouchables and invited Ambedkar to Mahad.

  • A. V. Chitre and G. N. Sahasrabuddhe

    Caste Hindu supporters of the campaign. Sahasrabuddhe, a Brahmin, burned the Manusmriti at Ambedkar's side in December 1927.

  • The satyagrahis

    Thousands of Dalit men and women who travelled to Mahad from across the region, many contributing their own money.

03

What happened?

In 1927, Ambedkar decided to launch a satyagraha (non-violent resistance) to assert the right of untouchables to use the tank. Mahad was chosen because it had a core of support from a few caste Hindu reformers, and because its municipality had already opened the tank in principle.1 The Bahishkrit Hitakarini Sabha appealed for funds to bring supporters to the town.

On 19–20 March 1927, a conference of thousands of Dalits was held at Mahad. After the meeting, on 20 March, they marched in procession to the Chavdar Tale:1

  • Ambedkar bent down and drank water from the tank, and thousands of untouchables followed him.
  • Ambedkar also addressed Dalit women, urging them to change the way they dressed, which marked their caste; immediately afterwards, many women decided to drape their saris in the style of upper-caste women.

Ambedkar made clear that the protest was about dignity, not water. In a speech at the conference, he said that they were going to the tank "to assert that we too are human beings like others".2

The response was violent. A rumour spread that the Dalits planned to enter a Hindu temple, and caste Hindus attacked the delegates, injuring many.1 Some caste Hindus argued that the untouchables had polluted the tank. To "purify" it, Brahmins recited mantras while 108 pots of a mixture of cow urine and cow dung were emptied into the water, and the tank was then declared fit again for upper-caste use.1

04

What happened next?

  • Burning the Manusmriti: Ambedkar called a second conference at Mahad for December 1927. Caste Hindus obtained a court order claiming the tank was private property, so the satyagraha was postponed; but on 25 December 1927, Ambedkar's supporters publicly burned the Manusmriti, an ancient Hindu law book that justified caste discrimination.
  • Victory in court: after a ten-year legal battle, the Bombay High Court ruled in 1937 that untouchables had the right to use the tank.
  • Temple entry: in 1930 Ambedkar led a campaign for Dalit entry to the Kalaram temple in Nashik.
  • The constitution: as chair of the drafting committee of India's constitution, adopted in 1950, Ambedkar helped to abolish untouchability in law and to guarantee equal access to public wells, tanks and places.
05

Why does it matter?

The Mahad Satyagraha is often described as the first great collective revolt of Dalits against untouchability. By drinking from a public tank, thousands of people who had been told they were unclean asserted their equal humanity — and made clear that they would not wait for others to grant them their rights.

  • It marked the emergence of an independent, organised Dalit movement under Dalit leadership.
  • It showed how an ordinary act — drinking water — could become a powerful act of resistance.
  • The struggle continues: discrimination over access to water, temples and public spaces is still reported in India today.

What do others say?How it was seen at the time, and where historians disagree

At the time

Orthodox caste Hindus regarded the satyagraha as an attack on religion and tradition, and many nationalist politicians were uneasy about a campaign that targeted Hindu society rather than British rule. Ambedkar's supporters saw it as a struggle for basic civil rights.

Historians' debates

  • Ambedkar and Gandhi: historians compare Ambedkar's approach, which demanded rights and challenged Hindu scriptures directly, with Gandhi's campaign to reform Hindu attitudes to untouchability from within. The two men later clashed bitterly over separate electorates for Dalits.
  • Who led? Historians such as Anand Teltumbde emphasise the role of ordinary Dalit activists and the support of a few caste Hindu reformers, as well as Ambedkar's leadership.
  • Water or dignity? Ambedkar himself stressed that the struggle was not about water but about equality; historians see it as the beginning of his journey towards rejecting Hinduism — he converted to Buddhism in 1956.
How do we know?The evidence, and a source from the time
  • Ambedkar's speeches and writings, and his newspaper Bahishkrit Bharat, founded in 1927.
  • Reports in Marathi and English newspapers, and police records.
  • The records of the court case over the tank, decided in 1937.
  • Leaflets such as the 1927 appeal for funds from the Bahishkrit Hitakarini Sabha.

How is it remembered?Memorials, anniversaries and arguments over memory
  • 20 March is observed in India as Social Empowerment Day, and thousands visit the Chavdar Tale each year.
  • 25 December is marked by many Dalits as Manusmriti Dahan Din (Manusmriti Burning Day).
  • A memorial and a statue of Ambedkar stand beside the tank at Mahad, known in Ambedkarite tradition as a krantibhoomi — a land of revolution.
