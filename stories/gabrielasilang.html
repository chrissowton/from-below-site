Gabriela Silang and the Ilocano Revolt

When Britain captured Manila, Diego Silang rose against Spanish rule in the north. After he was assassinated, his widow Gabriela took command — and became a symbol of Filipino women's resistance.

When
1762 – 1763
Where
Vigan and Abra, Ilocos, Philippines
Facts checked
2026-11-16

See it on the map →

01

What is the background to this event?

The Philippines had been a Spanish colony since the sixteenth century. In the Ilocos region of northern Luzon, people paid tribute and were forced to work on public projects (polo), and the Church held great power.

  • Diego Silang, a courier who carried letters between Ilocos and Manila, saw colonial injustice at first hand.1
  • In October 1762, during the Seven Years' War, British forces captured Manila.1
  • The British occupation weakened Spanish authority and inspired uprisings in the north.1
02

Who were the key people?

  • Gabriela Silang

    An Ilocano-Itneg woman who led the revolt after her husband's death. She was executed in September 1763.

  • Diego Silang

    The leader of the revolt, assassinated in May 1763.

  • Nicolás Cariño

    Gabriela's uncle, who joined the revolt.

  • Miguel Vicos and Pedro Becbec

    Former allies who killed Diego Silang for a Spanish reward.

03

What happened?

Diego Silang demanded that Spanish officials in Ilocos be replaced by native-born officials and that the tribute be abolished.1 His forces took control of Vigan, and he allied with the British, who named him governor of Ilocos on their behalf.2

  • Gabriela became one of his closest advisers and his aide during skirmishes with Spanish troops.2
  • The Spanish offered a reward for Diego's death. On 28 May 1763, two former allies, Miguel Vicos and Pedro Becbec, shot him in Vigan.2
  • Gabriela fled to Tayum in Abra, appointed generals and took command of the rebel forces. Her image as "La Generala" on horseback dates from this time.2
  • On 10 September 1763, she led an attack on Vigan but was driven back.2

She was captured in Abra and, on 20 September 1763, hanged in Vigan, with many of her followers.2

04

What happened next?

  • Spanish rule restored: Britain returned Manila to Spain in 1764 under the Treaty of Paris.
  • Later revolts: uprisings continued in the Ilocos, such as the Basi Revolt of 1807, until the Philippine Revolution of 1896.
  • A symbol: in the twentieth century, Gabriela Silang became a national hero. The women's organisation GABRIELA, founded in 1984 during the Marcos dictatorship, is named after her.
05

Why does it matter?

Gabriela Silang is remembered as the first Filipino woman to lead a revolt against colonial rule. Her story challenges the idea that colonised peoples, and women, were passive.

  • It shows how global wars between empires created openings for local resistance.
  • It raises questions about alliances with one colonial power against another.
  • Uprisings in the Ilocos continued, from the Basi Revolt of 1807 to the Philippine Revolution of 1896.

What do others say?How it was seen at the time, and where historians disagree

At the time

The Spanish saw the Silangs as traitors who allied with an enemy power. Their followers saw them as fighting against tribute and abuse.

Historians' debates

  • Independence or reform? Historians debate whether Diego Silang sought independence or reform within the colonial system.
  • The British alliance: some question whether allying with Britain was a form of national resistance or simply a change of masters.
  • Violence: the revolt involved fighting on both sides; the Spanish executions of Gabriela and her followers were meant as a warning.
How do we know?The evidence, and a source from the time
  • Spanish colonial records and chronicles, such as that of Joaquín Martínez de Zúñiga (1805).
  • Ilocano oral traditions.
How is it remembered?Memorials, anniversaries and arguments over memory
  • A monument to Gabriela Silang stands in Makati, Metro Manila.
  • The Philippine Navy and the women's organisation GABRIELA bear her name.
