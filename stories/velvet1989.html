The Velvet Revolution

Riot police beat students marching in Prague. Within days, half a million people filled Wenceslas Square jingling their keys, a two-hour general strike stopped the country, and forty-one years of one-party rule ended without a shot.

When
17 November – 29 December 1989
Where
Národní Street and Wenceslas Square, Prague, Czech Republic

01

What is the background to this event?

The Communist Party had ruled Czechoslovakia since 1948.1

  • After the Prague Spring of 1968 was crushed, dissidents such as the signers of Charter 77 were persecuted.1
  • In 1989, communist governments were falling across Eastern Europe.1

In their own words

03

What happened?

On 17 November 1989 (International Students' Day), riot police blocked and attacked a student march on Národní Street in Prague.1

  • A false rumour that a student had been killed spread outrage.1
  • Protests grew from 200,000 to an estimated 500,000 people in Prague.1
  • Civic Forum in Prague and Public Against Violence in Bratislava led the opposition.1
  • Crowds jingled their keys, telling the Communists it was time to go home.1
  • On 27 November, a two-hour general strike was held across the country.1
  • On 28 November, the Communist Party gave up its monopoly on power.1

04

What happened next?

  • New president: Václav Havel became president on 29 December 1989.1
  • Elections: free elections followed in June 1990.1
  • Separation: on 31 December 1992, Czechoslovakia split peacefully into two countries.1
05

What do others say?

At the time

The government blamed troublemakers; protesters demanded dialogue and free elections.1

Debates since

  • A negotiated end? Some argue that the Communist Party chose to negotiate because regimes were collapsing around it.1
  • Violence: the protests were peaceful; police beat students on 17 November.1

How is it remembered?

  • 17 November is a national holiday in the Czech Republic and Slovakia.
08

Why does it matter?

The Velvet Revolution showed how quickly a regime could collapse once people stopped being afraid.

09

What other events are like this?

  • 1989Leipzig's Monday Demonstrations and the Fall of the Wall — Prayers for peace in a Leipzig church became a weekly march. On 9 October, 70,000 people walked the city's ring road past armed police chanting 'We are the people' — and no shots were fired. A month later the Berlin Wall opened.
  • 1968Prague 1968: Resisting the Tanks — When half a million Warsaw Pact troops invaded to crush 'socialism with a human face', Czechs and Slovaks resisted without weapons — removing road signs, broadcasting from secret radios and arguing with soldiers in the streets.
  • 1988The Candle Demonstration — Thousands of Slovak Catholics stood silently in a square holding candles to ask for religious freedom. The communist police turned water cannon and batons on them — and the protest became a first step towards the Velvet Revolution.
