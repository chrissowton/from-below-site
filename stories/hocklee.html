Workers & unions

The Hock Lee Bus Strike

Bus workers sacked for joining a union went on strike, and Chinese-school students came to support them. A night of rioting left four people dead and became a defining moment in Singapore's history.

When
April – May 1955
Where

01

What is the background to this event?

In the 1950s, Singapore was a British colony moving towards self-government. In 1955, a new constitution led to elections, and David Marshall of the Labour Front became Chief Minister.1

  • The easing of emergency restrictions led to the formation of many new trade unions.1
  • Many workers faced low pay and poor conditions, and students in Chinese-language schools resented colonial education policies they saw as discriminatory.1
  • In 1955 alone, 31,000 workers took part in 129 strikes between March and June.1

In their own words

03

What happened?

In February 1955, the Hock Lee Amalgamated Bus Company sacked 229 workers for belonging to the Singapore Bus Workers' Union. The workers went on strike.1

  • Strikers picketed the bus depot on Alexandra Road, and students from Chinese schools joined them, bringing food and singing.
  • Police tried to break the picket lines with fire hoses.
  • Despite attempts at mediation, on 12 May 1955 the confrontation escalated into a riot.1

Four people died and 31 were injured.1 They included two police officers, an American journalist, Gene Symonds, who was beaten by rioters, and a 16-year-old student, Chong Lon Chong, who was shot.

04

What happened next?

  • Settlement: the dispute was settled with the reinstatement of many workers.
  • Crackdowns: the colonial and later Singapore governments used the riots to justify detention without trial of union leaders and left-wing politicians. Fong Swee Suan and Lim Chin Siong were detained in 1956 and again in 1963.
  • Political change: the riots shaped the struggle between left and right within the People's Action Party, which came to power in 1959 under Lee Kuan Yew.
05

What do others say?

At the time

The government and the company blamed communist agitators. The unions and students saw themselves as defending workers' rights.

Historians' debates

  • Communist plot or workers' struggle? Conventional accounts present the riot as communist subversion. Newer historians focus on the social and economic grievances of workers and students.1
  • The violence: rioters killed a journalist and police officers; police shooting killed a student. Historians debate who started the violence.
  • The role of outside powers: research shows that US diplomats pressed Britain to act against the labour and student movements.1

07

How is it remembered?

  • The riots feature prominently in Singapore's official history of the struggle against communism, and are increasingly debated by younger historians.
08

Why does it matter?

The Hock Lee strike is one of the most debated events in Singapore's history. For some it was communist subversion; for others, a struggle by workers and students for rights under colonial rule.

  • It shows how labour movements were caught up in the politics of decolonisation and the Cold War.
  • It raises questions about how official histories are written.

What other events are like this?

  • 1947The All-Malaya Hartal — Malay radicals, Chinese and Indian groups united behind a 'People's Constitution' for an independent, democratic Malaya with equal citizenship for all. On one day, they shut down the country.
  • 1925The Canton–Hong Kong Strike — After British police shot protesters in Shanghai, a quarter of a million workers left Hong Kong and boycotted British goods. The colony became a ghost town.
  • 1976The Grunwick Strike — South Asian women workers walked out of a photo-processing factory in north London and became the centre of one of the biggest industrial disputes of the 1970s.
