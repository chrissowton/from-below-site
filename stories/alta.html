Land & environment

The Alta Controversy

Sámi activists and environmentalists chained themselves together in the Arctic winter to stop a dam on the Alta river. They lost the dam — but won recognition for the Sámi people.

When
1978 – 1982
Where
Alta river, Finnmark, Norway (Sápmi)

See it on the map →

01

What is the background to this event?

The Sámi are the Indigenous people of Sápmi, the far north of Norway, Sweden, Finland and Russia's Kola Peninsula. For centuries they lived by reindeer herding, fishing and hunting. From the nineteenth century, the Norwegian state pursued a policy of "Norwegianisation": Sámi children were taught only in Norwegian, Sámi land rights were ignored, and Sámi culture was treated as backward.

In 1968, the Norwegian Water Resources and Electricity Board published a plan to dam the Alta–Kautokeino river in Finnmark for hydroelectric power:1

  • The original plan would have flooded the Sámi village of Masi. After political resistance, a smaller project was proposed that would cause less displacement and less disruption to reindeer migration and wildlife.1
  • The river and its valley were still central to Sámi reindeer herding, fishing and culture, and the Alta is one of Europe's finest salmon rivers.
  • From 1973, a local committee documented the damage the project would do, and the municipal council of Alta opposed it.1

In 1978 the Norwegian parliament, the Storting, approved the development.

02

Who were the key people?

  • The Sámi hunger strikers

    Young Sámi activists, including Jorunn Eikjok and Niillas Aslaksen Somby, who went on hunger strike outside the Storting in Oslo in 1979.

  • Folkeaksjonen

    The People's Action against the Development of the Alta–Kautokeino River, founded on 12 July 1978, which at its peak had some 20,000 members.

  • The Sámi women who occupied the prime minister's office

    A group of Sámi women who occupied the office of Prime Minister Gro Harlem Brundtland in February 1981.

  • Gro Harlem Brundtland

    Norway's environment minister and later prime minister, who supported the development.

  • The local people of Alta and Kautokeino

    Sámi and non-Sámi residents, reindeer herders and fishermen who opposed the dam.

03

What happened?

Opponents of the dam organised on several fronts. Folkeaksjonen, founded on 12 July 1978, brought together environmentalists and Sámi activists.1 In the summer of 1979, protesters set up a camp at Stilla, at the site where construction was to begin.1

The protests escalated through 1979–81:1

  • In the autumn of 1979, as construction was about to start, protesters sat down at the construction site at Stilla and chained themselves together to block the road.
  • In October 1979, a group of young Sámi set up a lavvu (a traditional Sámi tent) outside the Storting in Oslo and went on hunger strike, demanding that construction be halted. Thousands of people visited them in solidarity.
  • The prime minister, Odvar Nordli, promised a review of the decision — but the Storting confirmed it.
  • When work started again in January 1981, more than a thousand protesters chained themselves at the site. The police responded with large forces: at one point 10 per cent of all Norway's police officers were stationed in Alta, housed on a cruise ship. The protesters were forcibly removed. Declassified documents later showed that the government had planned to use military forces to support the police.

In February 1981, Sámi activists began a second hunger strike, and a group of Sámi women occupied the prime minister's office in Oslo. The central Sámi organisations broke off all cooperation with the Norwegian government, and two Sámi women travelled to Rome to petition the Pope.1 In 1982, Norway's Supreme Court ruled in favour of the government. Organised opposition ended, and the dam was completed in 1987.1

04

What happened next?

  • A Sámi Rights Committee: in 1980, in response to the protests, the government set up a committee to examine Sámi rights to land and water and Sámi political representation.
  • The constitution: in 1988, Norway amended its constitution to recognise its duty to enable the Sámi to safeguard and develop their language, culture and way of life.
  • A Sámi Parliament: the Sámi Act of 1987 created the Sámediggi, the Sámi Parliament, which opened in Karasjok in 1989.
  • International law: in 1990, Norway became the first country to ratify the International Labour Organization's Convention 169 on the rights of Indigenous and Tribal Peoples.
  • New struggles: in 2023, young Sámi activists again occupied government offices in Oslo, protesting against wind turbines built on reindeer grazing land at Fosen.
05

What do others say?

At the time

Supporters of the dam argued that it would provide clean energy and jobs, and that a democratically elected parliament had approved it. Opponents argued that the rights of the Sámi, a minority, could not simply be overruled by a majority vote, and that the environmental damage was too great.

Historians' debates

  • Defeat or victory? Most historians agree that, though the dam was built, the movement won important long-term gains for Sámi rights.
  • Environment and Indigenous rights: historians discuss how environmentalists and Sámi activists, with different priorities, worked together — and where they disagreed.
  • Tactics: the movement was overwhelmingly non-violent; a failed attempt by two activists to blow up a bridge in 1982, in which Niillas Somby was badly injured, was widely condemned and remains debated.

The evidence

  • Photographs and film of the protests, including images in the National Archives of Norway.
  • Government documents, some declassified decades later, on the planning of the police operation.
  • Testimonies and memoirs of Sámi activists and members of Folkeaksjonen.
07

How is it remembered?

  • A memorial at Gargia honours those who fought against the damming of the Alta river.
  • The protests are remembered in Sámi music, art and literature as a moment of cultural and political awakening, and anniversaries are marked in Alta and Oslo.
  • The Sámi Parliament in Karasjok stands as a lasting outcome of the struggle.
08

Why does it matter?

The Alta controversy was a turning point for the Sámi people and for Indigenous rights in Europe. Although the protesters lost the battle over the dam, they forced Norway to confront its treatment of the Sámi and to recognise them as an Indigenous people with collective rights.

  • It revived Sámi pride in their language and culture, and helped to reverse the policy of Norwegianisation.
  • It brought together Indigenous rights and environmental protection in a way that inspired later movements.
  • It led directly to the Sámi Parliament and to constitutional and international recognition of Sámi rights.

Questions to think about

  1. Should a parliamentary majority be able to override the rights of an Indigenous minority?
  2. How did the Alta protests combine environmental and Indigenous concerns?
  3. Can a protest movement that 'loses' still achieve its deeper aims?
  4. Why do you think a hunger strike outside parliament was such a powerful tactic?
