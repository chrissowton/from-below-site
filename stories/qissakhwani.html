The Qissa Khwani Bazaar Massacre

Pashtun members of a non-violent movement, the 'Servants of God', stood their ground as British troops opened fire in a Peshawar bazaar. Soldiers who refused to shoot were jailed.

When
23 April 1930
Where
Qissa Khwani Bazaar, Peshawar, Pakistan

See it on the map →

01

What is the background to this event?

In 1929, Abdul Ghaffar Khan founded the Khudai Khidmatgar ("Servants of God"), a movement of Pashtun Muslims committed to ending British rule through non-violence.1

  • The North-West Frontier Province was governed under harsh laws such as the Frontier Crimes Regulation.1
  • In 1930, the Indian independence movement launched a campaign of civil disobedience.
  • On 23 April 1930, Ghaffar Khan was arrested after a speech urging resistance to British rule.1
02

Who were the key people?

  • Abdul Ghaffar Khan (Bacha Khan)

    Founder of the Khudai Khidmatgar, known as the 'Frontier Gandhi'.

  • The Khudai Khidmatgar

    Non-violent Pashtun activists, known as the 'Red Shirts'.

  • Chandra Singh Garhwali

    Soldier who led the refusal of the Garhwal Rifles to fire.

In their own words

03

What happened?

After other leaders were arrested, a large crowd gathered in the Qissa Khwani (Storytellers') Bazaar in Peshawar.1

  • As troops moved in, stones were thrown, and a British Indian Army dispatch rider was killed and his body burned.1
  • Armoured cars drove into the crowd at speed, killing several people.1
  • According to accounts, the crowd offered to disperse if they could collect their dead and wounded and the troops withdrew; the troops refused.1
  • Soldiers then opened fire with machine guns on the unarmed crowd. Many Khudai Khidmatgar members stood their ground without fighting back.1
  • The official death toll was 20; Indian and Pakistani sources put it at up to 400.1

04

What happened next?

  • Mutiny: two platoons of the Royal Garhwal Rifles refused to go into Peshawar; their leaders were jailed for up to eight years.1
  • Growth: the shooting sparked protests across India and turned the Khudai Khidmatgar into a mass movement.1
05

What do others say?

At the time

The British authorities claimed the troops faced a violent mob; nationalists described a massacre of peaceful protesters.1

Debates since

  • Non-violence: historian Mukulika Banerjee shows how the movement built a culture of disciplined non-violence.2
  • Violence: some in the crowd threw stones, and a soldier was killed; troops killed between 20 and several hundred unarmed people.1
06

The evidence

  • Official reports and nationalist inquiries.1
  • Oral histories of Khudai Khidmatgar veterans.2
07

How is it remembered?

  • The massacre is commemorated in Peshawar each year.
08

Why does it matter?

Qissa Khwani is one of the most striking examples of non-violent discipline under fire.

  • It challenged stereotypes of Pashtuns as warlike.2

Questions to think about

  1. Why did Ghaffar Khan choose non-violence?
  2. How did the crowd respond to the shooting?
  3. Why did the Garhwali soldiers refuse to fire?
  4. Why is the Khudai Khidmatgar less well known than Gandhi's movement?

What other events are like this?

  • 1930The Salt March — A 24-day march to the sea to make salt in defiance of British law set off a campaign of civil disobedience across India.
  • 1881Parihaka — On land taken by the colonial government, two Māori leaders built a thriving village and led a campaign of peaceful resistance: ploughing settlers' fields and rebuilding fences. In 1881, 1,600 troops marched in and were greeted by singing children.
  • 1857The Indian Rebellion of 1857 — Indian soldiers in the East India Company's army mutinied at Meerut and marched on Delhi. Their revolt spread into a great rebellion of soldiers, peasants and princes across northern India.
