Disability rights

The Section 504 Sit-in

More than a hundred disabled people occupied a federal building for 26 days until the government signed the first major civil rights regulations for disabled Americans.

When
5 – 30 April 1977
Where
Federal Building, 50 United Nations Plaza, San Francisco, USA
Facts checked
2026-11-16

01

What is the background to this event?

For most of American history, disabled people were treated as objects of charity or medical care rather than as citizens with rights. They were routinely excluded from schools, workplaces, public transport and public buildings, and many were confined to institutions.

In 1973 Congress passed the Rehabilitation Act. Its Section 504 contained a single, far-reaching sentence:

"No otherwise qualified handicapped individual in the United States shall solely on the basis of his handicap, be excluded from the participation, be denied the benefits of, or be subjected to discrimination under any program or activity receiving Federal financial assistance."Rehabilitation Act of 1973, Section 504

But a law needs regulations to define what it means and how it will be enforced, and for four years the government did not issue them:

  • President Nixon had vetoed earlier versions of the Act in 1972 and 1973; disabled activists led by Judith Heumann had staged a sit-in on Madison Avenue in New York in protest.
  • Institutions that would have to comply, such as hospitals and universities, lobbied against regulations that might be costly.
  • In 1977 the new Secretary of Health, Education and Welfare (HEW), Joseph Califano, ordered a review and appeared ready to weaken the draft regulations.

The American Coalition of Citizens with Disabilities set a deadline of 4 April 1977 for the regulations to be signed, and warned of protests if they were not.

02

Who were the key people?

  • Judith Heumann

    A wheelchair user who had sued New York City's education board in 1970 to become a teacher, and one of the leaders of the occupation. She later served in the Clinton and Obama administrations.

  • Kitty Cone

    A disability rights organiser who played a central role in planning and running the occupation.

  • Brad Lomax and Chuck Jackson

    Lomax, a member of the Black Panther Party with multiple sclerosis, took part in the occupation with his attendant Jackson; their involvement brought the Panthers' support.

  • Ed Roberts

    A pioneer of the independent living movement and director of California's Department of Rehabilitation, who visited the occupation and testified in support.

  • Joseph Califano

    Secretary of Health, Education and Welfare, whose signature was needed for the regulations.

  • The occupiers

    More than a hundred disabled people — wheelchair users, blind and Deaf people, people with intellectual disabilities and others — together with interpreters, attendants and allies.

In their own words

03

What happened?

On 5 April 1977, the day after the deadline passed, disabled people demonstrated at HEW offices in several cities. In San Francisco, more than 500 people attended a rally at Civic Center Plaza, and then approximately 150 disabled people and their allies entered the Federal Building at 50 United Nations Plaza and refused to leave.2 Judith Heumann urged the crowd to tell the regional director that "the government cannot steal our civil rights!"2

In other cities, protests ended within a day or two. In San Francisco, the occupation lasted 26 days, sustained by an extraordinary coalition:2

  • The Black Panther Party provided hot meals every day, free of charge. One Panther explained: "We support you because you're asking America to change, to treat you like human beings, like you belong."2
  • Glide Memorial Church, the Gay Men's Butterfly Brigade, the United Farm Workers, the Gray Panthers and the International Association of Machinists also gave support, as did Mayor George Moscone.2
  • When the authorities restricted access to the building, Deaf occupiers used sign language through the fourth-floor windows to communicate with interpreters on the street below.2

Inside the building, members of Congress held a public hearing at which dozens of disabled people testified. When an HEW official revealed that Califano was considering changes, including allowing "separate but equal" provision, Ed Roberts condemned the idea.2 A delegation travelled to Washington, with the help of the Machinists' union, to lobby Congress and hold candlelight vigils outside Califano's home, where they sang "Sign 504" to the tune of "We Shall Overcome".2

On 28 April 1977, Joseph Califano signed the regulations without the proposed changes. The occupiers left the building on 30 April.2

04

What happened next?

  • Enforcement: the Section 504 regulations required federally funded institutions — schools, universities, hospitals, transport systems — to become accessible and to stop discriminating against disabled people.
  • A national movement: many of the activists went on to campaign for further rights, culminating in the Americans with Disabilities Act of 1990, which extended protection to the private sector.
  • Influence abroad: the American disability rights movement influenced campaigns elsewhere, including the UK's Disability Discrimination Act 1995 and the UN Convention on the Rights of Persons with Disabilities (2006).
05

Why does it matter?

The 504 sit-in is a landmark of the disability rights movement and one of the longest non-violent occupations of a federal building in US history. It transformed how disability was understood: not as an individual misfortune but as a question of civil rights and social barriers.

  • It won regulations that changed the daily lives of millions of disabled people.
  • It showed the power of solidarity between movements — disabled, Black, gay, labour and religious activists working together.
  • It helped popularise the "social model" of disability, which locates the problem in inaccessible environments rather than in disabled people themselves.

What do others say?How it was seen at the time, and where historians disagree

At the time

Califano and HEW officials argued that the regulations needed careful review because of their potential cost to schools, hospitals and local governments, and some institutions warned that full accessibility would be unaffordable. Some officials also favoured separate facilities for disabled people rather than full integration.

Historians' debates

  • Whose story? Early accounts focused on white leaders such as Heumann; more recent work has highlighted the role of Black disabled activists such as Brad Lomax and the support of the Black Panther Party.
  • Implementation: historians note that signing the regulations was not the end; enforcement was slow, and many institutions resisted for years.
  • Regional differences: why the San Francisco occupation succeeded where others ended quickly is often explained by the Bay Area's strong network of independent living centres and social movements.

This one sentence was passed into law in 1973 — but it could not be enforced until the government issued regulations. Disabled activists occupied federal offices in 1977 to force it to do so. The words of the law are what they were fighting for.

Reading it

One sentence, passed into law in 1973, that could not be enforced until the government issued regulations. Disabled activists occupied federal offices in 1977 to force it to act. A law can promise rights on paper; it took people in wheelchairs refusing to leave a building to make these words count.

How is it remembered?Memorials, anniversaries and arguments over memory
  • The documentary Crip Camp (2020), produced by Barack and Michelle Obama's company, brought the story to a wide audience and was nominated for an Academy Award.
  • Judith Heumann's death in 2023 prompted widespread tributes.
  • Anniversaries of the sit-in are marked by disability rights organisations across the United States.
