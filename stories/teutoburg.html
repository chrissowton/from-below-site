Arminius and the Teutoburg Forest

A Germanic officer trained by Rome led an alliance of peoples that destroyed three Roman legions in a forest ambush. Rome never again tried to conquer the lands beyond the Rhine.

When
September 9 CE
Where
Kalkriese, Lower Saxony, Germany
Facts checked
2026-11-16

See it on the map →

01

What is the background to this event?

Under Augustus, Rome was trying to turn the lands east of the Rhine into a province.1

  • The Roman governor, Publius Quinctilius Varus, imposed taxes and Roman law.
  • Arminius was a chieftain of the Cherusci who had Roman citizenship and a Roman military education, and served as an officer of Varus's auxiliaries.1
02

Who were the key people?

  • Arminius

    Cherusci chieftain and Roman officer who led the alliance.

  • Publius Quinctilius Varus

    Roman governor who died in the battle.

  • Thusnelda

    Arminius's wife, captured by the Romans in 15 CE.

  • Germanicus

    Roman general who led the revenge campaigns.

In their own words

03

What happened?

In September 9 CE, Arminius led Varus's army into an ambush by an alliance of Germanic peoples.1

  • Three legions, about 15,000 soldiers, plus around 4,500 auxiliaries, were attacked over several days in forest and marsh.1
  • Arminius's knowledge of Roman tactics let him anticipate their responses.1
  • Almost the whole army was destroyed, and Varus killed himself.
  • The battle was probably fought near Kalkriese, where archaeologists have found thousands of Roman objects.1
04

What happened next?

  • Revenge: Tiberius and Germanicus led campaigns of retaliation; Germanicus captured Thusnelda in 15 CE.1
  • The border: the Rhine became the empire's frontier, and Rome made no major incursion into Germania again until the Marcomannic Wars under Marcus Aurelius (161–180 CE).1
  • Arminius: according to Tacitus, he was killed by his own relatives in about 21 CE.
05

Why does it matter?

The battle stopped Roman expansion in northern Europe, and shows how colonised people could turn an empire's training against it.

  • It also shows how a story of resistance can later be used for nationalism and war.
  • The Rhine became the empire's frontier, and Rome made no major incursion into Germania again for some 150 years.1
  • Its memory was later bent to new purposes: the Hermannsdenkmal near Detmold, completed in 1875, became a symbol of German nationalism.1

What do others say?How it was seen at the time, and where historians disagree

At the time

Roman writers called it the "Varian disaster", blaming Varus; Tacitus later called Arminius the liberator of Germany.1

Debates since

  • Turning point? Some historians argue that Rome stopped at the Rhine mainly because supplying armies further east was too hard.1
  • Violence on both sides: Roman sources say captured officers were sacrificed and skulls were nailed to trees; Germanicus's campaigns in turn burned villages and killed many people.1
  • Nationalism: "Hermann" became a symbol of German nationalism, and later of the Nazis.13
How do we know?The evidence, and a source from the time
  • Accounts by Velleius Paterculus, Tacitus and Cassius Dio.
  • Excavations at Kalkriese since 1987.3

Primary source: Memorial stone

What is it?
Memorial stone
Made by, and when
Set up by Publius Caelius, Xanten, early 1st century CE

A memorial to a Roman centurion killed in the battle, put up by his brother at the legionary base of Vetera (Xanten). Its inscription says that his bones 'may be' buried there: the dead of Varus's army lay unburied in the forest for years.

Reading it

A brother put up this memorial, and its wording is careful: the centurion's bones 'may be' buried here. The dead of Varus's army lay unburied in the forest for years, so no one could be sure. It gives a glimpse of the battle's human cost from the Roman side — and a reminder that almost nothing survives from the Germanic side at all.

How is it remembered?Memorials, anniversaries and arguments over memory
  • The Hermannsdenkmal near Detmold was completed in 1875 as a symbol of German nationalism.1
  • The Museum und Park Kalkriese now presents the battle without nationalist myth.
