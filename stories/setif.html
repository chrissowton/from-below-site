The Sétif and Guelma Massacres

On the day the Second World War ended in Europe, Algerians marched for independence in Sétif. The colonial response killed thousands and set the course towards war.

When
8 May – June 1945
Where
Sétif, Guelma and Kherrata, Algeria
Facts checked
2026-11-16

See it on the map →

01

What is the background to this event?

France had conquered Algeria from 1830 onwards and made it legally part of France. Around a million European settlers (colons or pieds-noirs) held most of the best land and political power, while the nine-tenths of the population who were Muslim Algerians were treated as subjects rather than full citizens.

During the Second World War, the defeat of France in 1940 and the Allied landings in North Africa in 1942 shook French authority. Algerians served in large numbers in the Free French army that fought in Italy and France. Nationalist movements grew rapidly:

  • In 1943 Ferhat Abbas published the Manifesto of the Algerian People, which called for an Algerian constitution and state associated with France.1
  • The lack of a French response led to the creation of the Friends of the Manifesto and of Liberty (AML), which brought together Abbas's supporters, the more radical Algerian People's Party (PPA) of Messali Hadj, and others.1
  • In the Constantine region, drought and famine added to the tension. In Guelma, there were about 4,000 settlers and 16,500 Muslim Algerians.1

In April 1945 the French authorities deported Messali Hadj, and nationalists prepared to use the celebrations of victory in Europe to press their demands.

In their own words

03

What happened?

On the morning of 8 May 1945 — VE Day — around 5,000 people marched through Sétif. The march had been authorised as a celebration of the Allied victory, but marchers carried banners calling for the release of Messali Hadj and for independence, and the green and white Algerian nationalist flag, which had been banned.1 When police tried to seize the flag, shots were fired, and the young scout Bouzid Saâl was killed.13

Violence spread rapidly:1

  • Algerians rioted in Sétif and attacked European settlers in the town and the surrounding countryside, including around Kherrata. Over the following days, 102 Europeans were killed, according to the official French report.
  • That evening in Guelma, a smaller, peaceful march was violently repressed by police.
  • The attacks on settlers lasted until 12 May.

The French response was overwhelming and collective:1

  • On instructions from Paris, the army — including the Foreign Legion and Moroccan and Senegalese troops — carried out summary executions in a ratissage ("raking-over") of rural communities.
  • Villages that were hard to reach were bombed by French aircraft between 9 and 19 May, and the cruiser Duguay-Trouin shelled the coast near Kherrata.
  • In Guelma, settler militias organised by the sub-prefect André Achiari arrested and executed hundreds of Algerians; bodies were later burned in lime kilns at Héliopolis to hide the evidence.

The number of Algerians killed remains disputed. The French government's Tubert report, written shortly afterwards, gave a figure of 1,020; Radio Cairo claimed 45,000, a figure still used by the Algerian government. Historians' estimates vary widely, from around 3,000 to 30,000; most of the victims were men.14

General Duval himself warned his superiors in a secret report:

"I have secured you peace for ten years. If France does nothing, it will all happen again, only next time it will be worse and may well be irreparable."General Raymond Duval, 1945, as translated in English sources1
04

What happened next?

  • Repression: thousands of Algerians were arrested, including Ferhat Abbas, and dozens were sentenced to death. Nationalist organisations were banned.
  • Silence in France: the events were largely censored in France, and for decades they were little known outside Algeria.
  • Towards war: many young nationalists concluded that peaceful protest was useless. Some of those who were radicalised in 1945 went on to found the National Liberation Front (FLN), which launched the war of independence on 1 November 1954 — almost ten years after Duval's warning.
  • Independence: after a war in which hundreds of thousands died, Algeria became independent in 1962.
05

What do others say?

At the time

French authorities presented the events as an insurrection and a massacre of Europeans, which justified the repression. The term "Sétif massacre" was first used by French propaganda to refer to the European victims. Algerian nationalists described a planned colonial massacre of civilians.

Historians' debates

  • How many died? Estimates range from the French official 1,020 to the Algerian official 45,000. Historian Jean-Louis Planche estimated 20,000–30,000; others consider this too high, but argue that the figure of 15,000–20,000 given by Ferhat Abbas is plausible.
  • Planned uprising? Some historians argue that elements of the PPA planned an insurrection; others argue that the violence was largely spontaneous, and that the colonial authorities had prepared to use force.
  • Massacre, or something worse? Some Algerian voices have called the events a genocide; most historians use the term "massacre", while recognising their scale and collective character.
  • Algerian memory: historians note that the Algerian state long emphasised 1 November 1954 rather than May 1945, partly because 1945 involved nationalist currents that the FLN later opposed.
06

The evidence

  • The report of the Tubert commission of inquiry (1945), and French military and administrative archives, some of which were opened only recently.
  • General Duval's report to his superiors.
  • Testimonies of Algerian survivors and of settlers, collected by historians and film-makers.
07

How is it remembered?

  • In February 2005, France's ambassador to Algeria, Hubert Colin de Verdière, called the events an "inexcusable tragedy" — the most explicit French acknowledgement until then.
  • Since 2020, 8 May has been an official national day of remembrance in Algeria.
  • Memorials stand in Sétif, Guelma and Kherrata, and the events are depicted in Rachid Bouchareb's film Hors-la-loi (Outside the Law, 2010) and in Kateb Yacine's writing, including his novel Nedjma (1956).
08

Why does it matter?

The massacres of May 1945 were a turning point in the history of Algeria and of French colonialism. At the very moment when Europe was celebrating its liberation from fascism, colonised people who demanded the same freedom were met with mass killing.

  • They convinced a generation of Algerian nationalists that independence would have to be won by force.
  • They expose the contradiction between the ideals of the Allied victory and the realities of colonial rule.
  • The struggle over their memory remains central to relations between France and Algeria.

What other events are like this?

  • 1919Egypt's 1919 Revolution — When Britain exiled the nationalist leader Saad Zaghlul, Egyptians of every class and religion rose up in strikes and demonstrations — and women marched in public for the first time.
  • 1952The Mau Mau Uprising — An armed rebellion over land and freedom was met with mass detention and torture. Decades later, the British government paid compensation to survivors.
  • 1907The Santa María School Massacre — Thousands of striking nitrate miners and their families were shot down in and around a school in Iquique — one of the worst massacres of workers in Latin American history.
