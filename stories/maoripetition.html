Language & culture

The Māori Language Petition

Young Māori activists delivered a petition of more than 30,000 signatures calling for te reo Māori to be taught in schools — the start of the language's revival.

When
14 September 1972
Where

01

What is the background to this event?

Te reo Māori, the language of the Māori people, was spoken by almost all Māori in the nineteenth century. During the twentieth century it declined sharply:

  • Schools discouraged the use of Māori, and many children were punished for speaking it, so that many parents chose not to pass it on.
  • After the Second World War, large numbers of Māori moved from rural communities to towns and cities, where English dominated daily life.
  • By the early 1970s, the proportion of Māori who were fluent speakers had fallen dramatically, and most fluent speakers were older people. A survey led by Richard Benton in the 1970s warned that the language was in danger of dying out.

A new generation of young, mostly urban and university-educated Māori began to organise. Ngā Tamatoa ("The Young Warriors"), formed in Auckland around 1970, was influenced by international movements such as the Black Panthers and the American Indian Movement, and campaigned on land, the Treaty of Waitangi and language. At Victoria University in Wellington, the Te Reo Māori Society also campaigned for the language.

In their own words

03

What happened?

In 1972 Ngā Tamatoa and the Te Reo Māori Society organised a petition calling for Māori language and culture to be offered in New Zealand schools. Volunteers collected signatures across the country.2

On 14 September 1972, Hana Te Hemara, Lee Smith, Rawiri Paratene and Syd Jackson presented the petition, with more than 30,000 signatures, to Parliament in Wellington.23 The petition challenged politicians to make saving te reo Māori a priority.

  • The day of the presentation was declared Māori Language Day, and is still marked on 14 September each year.34
  • In 1975 the day was expanded into Māori Language Week (Te Wiki o te Reo Māori).3

Titewhai Harawira later explained the activists' motivation:

"We were determined to rescue our language because we felt and we believed, and we believe today, that a people without its language is a people that die."Titewhai Harawira, speaking in 20092
04

What happened next?

  • Community revival: Māori communities created their own language programmes: Te Ataarangi adult classes from 1979, and Te Kōhanga Reo, Māori-language "language nests" for pre-school children, from 1982. Māori-medium schools, Kura Kaupapa Māori, followed from 1985.
  • The Treaty claim: in 1986 the Waitangi Tribunal found that te reo Māori was a taonga (treasure) that the Crown was obliged to protect under the Treaty of Waitangi.
  • Official status: the Māori Language Act 1987 made te reo Māori an official language of New Zealand and established the Māori Language Commission.
  • Broadcasting: Māori-language radio stations followed, and Māori Television began broadcasting in 2004.
05

Why does it matter?

The 1972 petition is widely seen as the beginning of the modern revival of te reo Māori. A small group of young activists turned concern about the language's decline into a national campaign, and their work led to new institutions, laws and a profound change in how New Zealanders see the language.

  • It is one of the most influential Indigenous language revival movements in the world.
  • It linked language to wider questions of Māori rights and the Treaty of Waitangi.
  • Te reo Māori is now heard in everyday public life — in place names, greetings, the media and government.

What do others say?How it was seen at the time, and where historians disagree

At the time

Many politicians and educators considered that English was the language of opportunity and that teaching Māori in schools was impractical or unnecessary. Some older Māori, who had been taught that their language held their children back, were also uncertain. Ngā Tamatoa's confrontational style was criticised by some established Māori leaders.

Historians' debates

  • Who led the revival? Historians stress that the 1972 petition was one part of a wider movement, including community initiatives such as Te Ataarangi and Kōhanga Reo, led by elders and families as well as young activists.
  • How successful? Te reo is far more visible today, but the number of highly fluent speakers remains limited, and debates continue about how best to secure the language's future.
  • Contemporary politics: in 2024, government moves to reduce the use of Māori names and language in some public agencies prompted large protests, showing that the place of te reo remains politically contested.

The petition calling for te reo Māori to be taught in schools, presented to Parliament on 14 September 1972. Its signatures were gathered largely by young activists. The date of its presentation is now marked each year in Māori Language Week.

How is it remembered?Memorials, anniversaries and arguments over memory
  • Te Wiki o te Reo Māori (Māori Language Week) is celebrated nationally every September.
  • On the 50th anniversary in September 2022, thousands of people marched to Parliament in Wellington to commemorate the petition.
  • The anniversary is also marked with the "Māori Language Moment", when people across the country speak te reo Māori at the same time.
