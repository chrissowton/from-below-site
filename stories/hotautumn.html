Italy's Hot Autumn

Migrant workers from southern Italy, students and factory workers came together in a wave of strikes that shook Italy's car plants and tyre factories — and won a charter of workers' rights.

When
1968 – 1970
Where
Turin, Milan and the industrial north of Italy
Facts checked

01

What is the background to this event?

Italy's "economic miracle" of the 1950s and 1960s turned it into an industrial power. Millions of workers moved from the poor south to the factories of the north.1

  • In Turin, workers from the south faced exhausting assembly-line work at FIAT, crowded housing and discrimination.1
  • By the late 1960s, near-full employment in the north gave workers more bargaining power.1
  • The student movement of 1968 had occupied universities and created assemblies where decisions were made collectively.1

03

What happened?

From the summer of 1968, workers began striking for flat-rate pay rises. The student movement turned towards industrial struggles, especially in the car factories of Turin in 1969.1

  • FIAT's Turin plants became the focus of continual wildcat strikes. Workers, supported by New Left students, demanded wage increases and the same conditions as white-collar workers.1
  • On 3 July 1969, a strike over high rents led to a battle with police on Corso Traiano in Turin.
  • In the autumn of 1969, as national contracts were renegotiated, millions of workers struck. There were over 440 hours of strikes in the region in 1969–70.1
  • Workers created factory councils and assemblies, and used new tactics such as rotating strikes and internal marches through the factories.

Workers won a 40-hour week and substantial pay rises.1

04

What happened next?

  • The Workers' Statute: in May 1970, Italy's parliament passed the Workers' Statute, guaranteeing freedom of union activity and protection against unfair dismissal.
  • The strategy of tension: on 12 December 1969, a bomb in Piazza Fontana in Milan killed 17 people. It was planted by neo-fascists, but the police at first blamed anarchists; one, Giuseppe Pinelli, died falling from a police station window.
  • The Years of Lead: the 1970s saw political violence by both far-right groups and far-left groups such as the Red Brigades; in total, hundreds of people were killed.
05

What do others say?

At the time

Employers and conservatives warned of chaos and revolution. Workers and the left saw the struggles as a fight for dignity.

Historians' debates

  • Unions or rank and file? Historians debate how far the movement was led by the unions and how far by workers' own assemblies and New Left groups.
  • From Hot Autumn to Years of Lead: some historians see a link between the radicalism of 1969 and the armed groups of the 1970s; others stress that the vast majority of workers rejected terrorism, and that far-right bombings began the violence.

How is it remembered?

  • The phrase "autunno caldo" is still used in Italy for any period of major strikes.1
08

Why does it matter?

The Hot Autumn was one of the greatest workers' movements in post-war Europe. It transformed Italian workplaces and labour law.

  • It shows how migrants and students can revitalise labour movements.
  • It also shows how a period of hope can be followed by violence and reaction.

What other events are like this?

  • 1912The Bread and Roses Strike — More than twenty thousand migrant textile workers, speaking dozens of languages, shut down the mills of Lawrence and won.
  • 1980The Gdańsk Shipyard Strike and Solidarity — A strike over the sacking of a crane operator grew into the first independent trade union in the Soviet bloc, with ten million members.
  • 1969The Cordobazo — Car workers, power workers and students rose against Argentina's military dictatorship and took control of much of the city for two days. The army retook it — but the dictatorship never recovered.
