The Malê Revolt

During Ramadan, enslaved and freed African Muslims rose up in Salvador — the most significant urban revolt by enslaved people in the history of the Americas.

When
24–25 January 1835
Where
Salvador, Bahia, Brazil
Facts checked
2026-11-16

01

What is the background to this event?

In the early nineteenth century, Salvador, the capital of the province of Bahia, was one of the largest cities in the Americas and a major centre of the Atlantic slave trade. A large proportion of its population was African-born, both enslaved and freed. Many worked in the streets as porters, sellers and craftspeople, and enslaved "earning slaves" (escravos de ganho) handed part of their wages to their owners.

Many of the Africans brought to Bahia in this period came from the Bight of Benin, where wars associated with the collapse of the Oyo Empire and the expansion of the Sokoto Caliphate had produced large numbers of captives. Among them were many Muslims:

  • In Bahia, Muslims were called malês, from the Yoruba word imale. By 1835, most were Nagô (Yoruba), but there were also Hausa and others.1
  • Malês met in rented rooms and freedmen's houses to pray, study the Qur'an and learn to read and write Arabic on wooden slates.1
  • Bahia had already seen a series of revolts by enslaved Africans since 1807, and the authorities were constantly afraid of an uprising like the revolution in Haiti.
02

Who were the key people?

  • Pacífico Licutan

    An elderly, enslaved Nagô tobacco roller and respected Muslim teacher. He was in jail when the revolt began, and the rebels' first target was to free him.

  • Ahuna

    An enslaved Nagô man living in Salvador, believed to be one of the leaders of the conspiracy.

  • Manoel Calafate

    A freed Nagô man in whose house rebels gathered on the night of the revolt. He took part in the fighting and appears to have been killed.

  • Elesbão do Carmo (Dandará)

    A freed Hausa trader who spread Islam among enslaved people on the plantations of the Recôncavo.

  • Sabina da Cruz and Guilhermina Rosa de Souza

    Two freedwomen who learned of the planned revolt. Guilhermina's warning to a white neighbour led to its discovery.

  • The rebels

    Around 600 enslaved and freed Africans, mostly Muslims, who fought through the streets of Salvador.

In their own words

03

What happened?

The revolt was planned for the early hours of Sunday 25 January 1835, the feast of Our Lady of Guidance, during the month of Ramadan.13 The plan was discovered only hours before it was due to begin. On the evening of Saturday 24 January, Sabina da Cruz went looking for her husband, Vitório Sule, after a quarrel, and found him with organisers of the revolt. When they told her that the next day they would be masters of the land, she reportedly replied that they would be "masters of the whiplash, but not of the land".1 A freedwoman, Guilhermina, passed news of the plan to her white neighbour, and it reached the authorities. By 11 p.m. the provincial president had reinforced the palace guard, alerted the barracks and ordered boats to watch the bay.1

At around 1 a.m. justices of the peace raided the house where rebels had gathered. The rebels burst out and the fighting began early:13

  • The rebels, many wearing white garments and protective amulets containing passages from the Qur'an, first attacked the jail to free Pacífico Licutan, but were driven back.
  • They fought their way through the city towards the barracks and to the neighbourhood of Vitória, where other Muslims joined them.
  • Finally they made for Água de Meninos, hoping to reach the plantations of the Recôncavo, but were defeated by cavalry and infantry by the early morning.

The historian João José Reis, who studied the police and court records in detail, estimates that around 600 people took part, and that some 70 rebels were killed, against fewer than ten on the government side.3 The amulets the rebels wore were described by police as "war garments".1

04

What happened next?

  • Punishment: rebels were tried in the following months. Four men were executed by firing squad, and many others were sentenced to flogging, prison or forced labour.3
  • Deportation: hundreds of freed Africans suspected of involvement were deported to West Africa. Some settled in Lagos and Ouidah, and the Brazilian ("Agudá") communities of the Bight of Benin and the Tabom people of Ghana partly descend from returnees of this period.1
  • New laws: new laws imposed the death penalty without appeal on enslaved people who killed their owners or overseers, and restricted the movement of Africans in Bahia.1
  • Islam continued: although Islam was persecuted, African Muslim communities persisted in Brazil into the twentieth century.
05

Why does it matter?

The Malê Revolt was the largest urban rising of enslaved people in the Americas. It shows how enslaved and freed Africans used their own religious, linguistic and organisational resources — here, Islam, literacy in Arabic and networks of mutual support — to plan resistance against slavery.

  • It challenges the idea that enslaved people in the Americas had lost their African cultures.
  • It reminds us that Islam has a long history in the Americas, brought by enslaved Africans.
  • The fear it caused shaped slave law and the treatment of Africans in Brazil for decades.

What do others say?How it was seen at the time, and where historians disagree

At the time

The authorities and the Bahian press presented the revolt as a fanatical religious plot and a threat to the whole white and Brazilian-born population. Their records — police interrogations and trial transcripts — are the main sources, and were produced under pressure and sometimes torture.

Historians' debates

  • Holy war or slave revolt? Some historians have described it as a jihad linked to the Islamic revolutions in West Africa; João José Reis argues that, while Islam provided leadership and organisation, it was above all a revolt against slavery and racial oppression.
  • Ethnicity: historians debate how far the revolt was a Nagô (Yoruba) movement, and why so few Brazilian-born Black people joined it.
  • What were the aims? Some testimony suggests the rebels planned to kill or enslave whites and mixed-race Brazilians; historians question how reliable this evidence is.
How do we know?The evidence, and a source from the time
  • Police and court records of the investigation and trials, held in the Arquivo Público do Estado da Bahia.
  • Arabic writings and amulets seized from the rebels, some of which survive.
  • Reports in the Bahian press and the correspondence of foreign consuls.

Reading it

On the eve of the revolt, Sabina da Cruz found her husband with the organisers, who told her they would be masters of the land the next day. Her retort survives only because it was recorded in the investigation, and through a friend word of the plan reached the authorities. Her voice comes to us through the very process that crushed the revolt.

How is it remembered?Memorials, anniversaries and arguments over memory
  • The revolt is commemorated by Afro-Brazilian organisations in Salvador and in popular music and carnival groups (blocos afro).
  • The Brazilian-descended communities of Lagos, Ouidah and Accra preserve memories of their return from Brazil.
  • It has inspired novels, including Ana Maria Gonçalves's Um defeito de cor (2006).
