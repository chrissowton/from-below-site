Ethiopia 1974: 'Land to the Tiller'

Students demanded land for the peasants; taxi drivers, teachers, workers, Muslims and soldiers joined them. The emperor fell — but the revolution was seized by a military junta that turned its terror on the very students who had begun it.

When
1965 – 1975
Where
Addis Ababa and across Ethiopia

01

What is the background to this event?

Ethiopia under Emperor Haile Selassie was one of the oldest monarchies in the world. Most Ethiopians were peasants, many working land owned by nobles and the Church, and paying heavy rents.

  • A failed coup in 1960 weakened the monarchy's authority.2
  • Between 1963 and 1970, impoverished peasants rose in several provinces.2
  • From 1965, university students in Addis Ababa marched with the slogans "Land to the Tiller" and "Is poverty a crime?".1 They also demanded equality for Ethiopia's many nationalities.
  • In 1972–74, a terrible famine struck Wollo province. The government's neglect and attempts to hide it caused outrage when it became known.2

In their own words

03

What happened?

The revolution began in January 1974, when soldiers in Negele mutinied over pay and conditions.2 Protests spread rapidly:2

  • Taxi drivers struck against fuel price rises; teachers struck against education reforms; workers held the first general strike in Ethiopia's history.
  • Students, teachers, junior officers and workers demanded human rights, land reform, price controls, free schooling and the release of political prisoners.
  • On 20 April 1974, more than 100,000 Muslims rallied in Addis Ababa for religious equality.2

A committee of soldiers, the Derg, gradually took control. On 12 September 1974, they deposed Haile Selassie, who died in custody in 1975.2 In November 1974, they executed about 60 former officials without trial.

In March 1975, the Derg nationalised all rural land, meeting the students' old demand of "Land to the Tiller".1 Tens of thousands of students and teachers were sent to the countryside to explain the reforms and organise peasant associations.

04

What happened next?

  • Divisions: the student movement split into rival Marxist organisations, notably the EPRP and MEISON. The EPRP opposed military rule.1
  • Terror: in 1976–78, the EPRP carried out an assassination campaign against Derg officials and supporters — the "White Terror". The Derg responded with the "Red Terror" (Qey Shibir), killing tens of thousands of young people; estimates range very widely, from about 10,000 to much higher figures.2
  • Civil war and famine: wars in Eritrea and Tigray and the famine of 1983–85 followed. The Derg fell in 1991. In 2006, Mengistu was convicted of genocide in absentia.2
05

What do others say?

At the time

Supporters of the old order saw the revolution as the destruction of a historic monarchy. Students and workers saw it as the end of feudalism.

Historians' debates

  • Whose revolution? Historians such as Bahru Zewde emphasise that the students set the agenda, but that the military seized power.
  • Violence: the EPRP's assassinations and the Derg's mass killings are both condemned; historians agree that the Derg's Red Terror was on a far larger scale.2
  • The students' legacy: some argue that the students' Marxism and focus on nationalities contributed to later authoritarianism and ethnic conflict; others defend their idealism.1

How is it remembered?

  • The Red Terror Martyrs' Memorial Museum in Addis Ababa, opened in 2010, commemorates the victims.
08

Why does it matter?

The Ethiopian revolution began from below — with students, taxi drivers, teachers, Muslims and peasants — and achieved a sweeping land reform. But it also shows how revolutions can be captured by armed groups and turn on their own supporters.

  • The student movement's ideas about nationalities still shape Ethiopian politics.1
  • It raises difficult questions about revolutionary violence on all sides.

Questions to think about

  1. Why do you think 'Land to the Tiller' was such a powerful slogan?
  2. Why did so many different groups join the protests in 1974?
  3. How did a popular revolution end in military dictatorship?
  4. Should violence by revolutionary groups and by states be judged in the same way?

What other events are like this?

  • 1964Sudan's October Revolution — When police killed a student at a university debate about the war in the south, Sudanese professionals, workers and students launched a general strike that brought down a military dictatorship in days.
  • 1973The Thai Student Uprising of 14 October 1973 — When thirteen activists were arrested for handing out leaflets calling for a constitution, half a million people filled the streets of Bangkok and brought down a military dictatorship.
  • 1921The Kronstadt Rebellion — The sailors who had helped bring the Bolsheviks to power demanded free elections and free speech. The Red Army crossed the ice to crush them.
