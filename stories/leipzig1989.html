Leipzig's Monday Demonstrations and the Fall of the Wall

Prayers for peace in a Leipzig church became a weekly march. On 9 October, 70,000 people walked the city's ring road past armed police chanting 'We are the people' — and no shots were fired. A month later the Berlin Wall opened.

When
4 September – 9 November 1989
Where
St Nicholas Church and the Ring, Leipzig, Germany

See it on the map →

01

What is the background to this event?

East Germany (the GDR) was a one-party state, watched over by the Stasi secret police.

  • From 1982, St Nicholas Church in Leipzig held weekly prayers for peace.1
  • In 1989, thousands of East Germans fled to the West through Hungary, which had opened its border with Austria.2
02

Who were the key people?

  • Katrin Hattenhauer and Gesine Oltmanns

    Who unfurled the first banners on 4 September.

  • Pastors Christoph Wonneberger and Christian Führer

    Who led the prayers for peace.

  • Kurt Masur

    Conductor who appealed for calm on 9 October.

  • The people of Leipzig

    Who marched every Monday.

03

What happened?

On 4 September 1989, after the Monday prayers, Katrin Hattenhauer and Gesine Oltmanns unfurled a banner reading "For an open country with free people".1

  • The marches grew each Monday.1
  • On 9 October, the state gathered 8,000 police and armed units; people feared a massacre like Tiananmen Square.1
  • A message from six prominent citizens, including Kurt Masur, urged both sides to stay calm.1
  • About 70,000 people marched, chanting "We are the people!", and the security forces did not intervene.1
  • The marches grew to 120,000 and then 320,000. On 9 November, the Berlin Wall was opened.12

04

What happened next?

  • The end of the GDR: the Stasi was dissolved, and free elections were held in March 1990.2
  • Reunification: Germany was reunified in October 1990.2
05

What do others say?

At the time

The government called the protesters enemies of the state; the protesters insisted that they were the people.1

Debates since

  • Who brought down the Wall? Historians weigh the role of the marchers, the refugees, the Soviet leader Mikhail Gorbachev and divisions in the ruling party.2
  • Violence: the protesters were peaceful; earlier in October, police beat and arrested demonstrators across the GDR.1
06

The evidence

  • Photographs, Stasi files and the recorded appeal of 9 October.1
07

How is it remembered?

  • Leipzig holds a Festival of Lights each 9 October to remember the march.
08

Why does it matter?

Leipzig showed that mass non-violent protest could bring down a powerful police state.

Questions to think about

  1. Why did protests grow out of a church?
  2. Why did the security forces not shoot on 9 October?
  3. Why was the chant 'We are the people' so powerful?
  4. Did East Germans get the change they wanted?
09

What other events are like this?

  • 1989The Velvet Revolution — Riot police beat students marching in Prague. Within days, half a million people filled Wenceslas Square jingling their keys, a two-hour general strike stopped the country, and forty-one years of one-party rule ended without a shot.
  • 1989The Baltic Way — Around two million people joined hands in a human chain 675 kilometres long, across Estonia, Latvia and Lithuania, to demand freedom from the Soviet Union.
  • 1989Timișoara and the Romanian Revolution — It began with parishioners protecting their pastor from eviction in Timișoara. Within ten days, the most repressive regime in Eastern Europe had fallen — the only one in 1989 to fall in blood.
