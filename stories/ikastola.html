The Ikastolas: Basque Schools under Franco

When Franco's dictatorship banned the Basque language, teachers and parents ran secret classes in their homes. The schools they founded — the ikastolas — kept Basque alive.

When
1940s – 1970s
Where
San Sebastián (Donostia) and the Basque Country, Spain

See it on the map →

01

What is the background to this event?

Basque (Euskara) is one of the oldest languages in Europe, unrelated to any other living language. Spoken on both sides of the western Pyrenees, it had been in decline for centuries as Spanish and French spread.

  • The first Basque-medium schools, the ikastolas, were founded in the early twentieth century; the first official ikastola opened in 1914.1
  • After the Republican defeat in the Spanish Civil War in 1939, Francisco Franco's dictatorship made the public use of Basque illegal, and all education had to be in Spanish.1
  • Basque names were banned, Basque books were destroyed, and people could be fined or punished for speaking Basque in public. The language survived mainly in families and villages.
02

Who were the key people?

  • Elbira Zipitria

    A teacher who ran clandestine Basque-language classes in her home in San Sebastián from the 1940s, training a generation of teachers. She is regarded as the founder of the modern ikastola movement.

  • The andereños

    The women teachers (literally 'young ladies') who taught in the clandestine and early ikastolas, often without pay.

  • The parents

    Who organised, financed and ran the ikastolas as cooperatives.

  • Parish priests

    Some of whom sheltered ikastolas in church buildings, giving them a measure of protection.

03

What happened?

Although Basque was banned from schools, a few ikastolas continued to teach clandestinely.1 In San Sebastián, the teacher Elbira Zipitria began teaching small groups of children in Basque in her own home in the 1940s. She trained young women as teachers, and her methods spread.

From the late 1950s and 1960s, the movement grew rapidly:1

  • Parents in towns and villages across the Basque Country organised ikastolas, often in private homes, church buildings or rented flats, and financed them themselves.
  • The schools operated at the edge of the law. Because thousands of children were studying outside authorised schools, they could not go on to higher education. In 1969 the Catholic Church arranged for the ikastolas to operate under the protection of parishes.1
  • Popular festivals were organised to raise money for the schools, a tradition that continues today.

By the time Franco died in 1975, tens of thousands of children were being educated in Basque.1

04

What happened next?

  • Autonomy: after the return of democracy, the Basque Autonomous Community was created in 1979, with Basque and Spanish as co-official languages.
  • Basque in public schools: the Basque Law of Normalisation of 1982 created bilingual education models. Today most children in the Basque Autonomous Community are taught mainly in Basque.
  • The ikastolas today: in 1993 the ikastolas had to choose between joining the public school system or remaining private cooperatives; both public and cooperative ikastolas exist today. In the French Basque Country, the Seaska network was founded in 1969.
  • Revival: the number of Basque speakers has grown, especially among young people, though Basque remains a minority language in daily use in many areas.
05

What do others say?

At the time

The Franco regime regarded Basque as a threat to the unity of Spain and associated it with separatism. Ikastola supporters saw their work as cultural survival and a basic right.

Historians' and commentators' debates

  • Language and politics: the Basque language movement developed alongside Basque nationalism, including the armed group ETA, which killed more than 800 people between 1968 and 2010. Some critics have accused the ikastolas of promoting nationalist politics; their supporters reject this, stressing that the movement was overwhelmingly peaceful and culturally focused, and many Basque speakers opposed ETA's violence.
  • Who speaks Basque? Researchers debate the gap between the large number of people who can speak Basque, thanks to schools, and the smaller number who use it in daily life.
  • Women's work: historians such as Jacqueline Urla have highlighted the central but often overlooked role of the women teachers, the andereños.

The evidence

07

How is it remembered?

  • Elbira Zipitria is honoured in San Sebastián, where a street, an education centre and a statue bear her name.
  • Annual festivals such as Kilometroak, Herri Urrats, Nafarroa Oinez and Araba Euskaraz raise money for the ikastolas and celebrate the language.
08

Why does it matter?

The ikastola movement is one of the great stories of language survival under dictatorship. Teachers and parents, at personal risk, kept a banned language alive by teaching it to children — and laid the foundations for its revival after democracy returned.

  • It shows how ordinary people, especially women teachers, resisted cultural repression.
  • It created an education system that has produced hundreds of thousands of new Basque speakers.
  • It is a model for other minority-language schools, such as Diwan in Brittany.
