Anti-colonial & freedom

The Mau Mau Uprising

An armed rebellion over land and freedom was met with mass detention and torture. Decades later, the British government paid compensation to survivors.

When
1952 – 1960
Where
Central Kenya

See it on the map →

01

What is the background to this event?

In the early twentieth century, British settlers took over large areas of fertile land in the central highlands of Kenya — the "White Highlands" — much of it the home of the Kikuyu people. Africans were pushed into crowded "reserves" or became squatters and labourers on settler farms.

By the late 1940s grievances were acute:

  • Land shortage and overcrowding in the reserves, and the eviction of squatters from settler farms.
  • Low wages, poor housing and racial discrimination in Nairobi, where thousands of young Kikuyu had moved in search of work.
  • Africans had almost no political representation; peaceful campaigns by the Kenya African Union, led by Jomo Kenyatta, had made little progress.

A more militant movement grew, binding its members with oaths. Its fighters called themselves the Kenya Land and Freedom Army; the British called it "Mau Mau".

02

Who were the key people?

  • Dedan Kimathi

    A former teacher who became the leading commander of the forest fighters in the Aberdare mountains. Captured in October 1956 and hanged in February 1957.

  • Jomo Kenyatta

    Leader of the Kenya African Union, convicted in 1953 of managing Mau Mau in a widely criticised trial. He denied involvement, and later became Kenya's first Prime Minister and President.

  • Field Marshal Muthoni wa Kirima

    One of the few women to reach senior rank among the forest fighters.

  • Sir Evelyn Baring

    Governor of Kenya, who declared the State of Emergency in October 1952.

  • The detainees

    Tens of thousands of Kikuyu, Embu and Meru men and women held without trial in camps, many of whom were tortured.

  • The loyalists

    Africans, including members of the Home Guard, who sided with the colonial government — many of them killed by Mau Mau.

03

What happened?

After a series of attacks on loyalist chiefs and settlers, the Governor, Sir Evelyn Baring, declared a State of Emergency on 20 October 1952. In Operation Jock Scott, around 180 alleged leaders were arrested, including Jomo Kenyatta, who was later convicted in a trial at Kapenguria.1

Fighters retreated to the forests of the Aberdares and Mount Kenya, from where they attacked loyalists, police posts and settler farms. The colonial response was massive:12

  • In Operation Anvil, from 24 April 1954, around 25,000 security forces sealed off Nairobi and screened its African population; 20,000 suspects were sent to detention camps and 30,000 more deported to the reserves.
  • Most of the Kikuyu population of the reserves — more than a million people — was forced to move into some 800 fortified "villages", surrounded by barbed wire.
  • Tens of thousands of people were detained without trial in a network of camps, where many were forced into labour and subjected to beatings and torture to make them renounce their oaths.
  • The RAF bombed the forests, and 1,090 people were hanged after trials under emergency laws.

The violence also divided Kikuyu communities. In March 1953 Mau Mau fighters killed dozens of loyalists and their families at Lari, and security forces and loyalists carried out brutal reprisals.1 By the end of the Emergency, official figures recorded more than 11,000 Mau Mau fighters killed, compared with 32 European civilians; many historians believe the true number of African deaths was far higher.2

Dedan Kimathi was captured on 21 October 1956, which effectively ended the military campaign.1 In March 1959, eleven detainees were beaten to death by guards at Hola camp. The scandal provoked outrage in Britain and helped bring the Emergency to an end in January 1960.2

04

What happened next?

  • Independence: although the rebellion was defeated militarily, Britain accelerated political reform. Kenyatta was released in 1961, and Kenya became independent on 12 December 1963 with Kenyatta as Prime Minister.
  • Silence: the independent Kenyan government did not celebrate Mau Mau, and the movement remained officially banned in Kenya until 2003.
  • Hidden files: in 2011 it emerged that the British government had secretly kept thousands of colonial files, the "migrated archives", which documented abuses.
  • Compensation: in 2013 the British government settled a case brought by Kenyan victims of torture, paying £19.9 million to 5,228 claimants. The Foreign Secretary, William Hague, expressed "sincere regret" that Kenyans had been subjected to torture and ill-treatment.
05

What do others say?

At the time

The colonial government and much of the British press portrayed Mau Mau as a primitive, savage cult, emphasising its oaths and the killing of settlers. This image dominated British views for decades. Some Kikuyu, especially loyalists and Christians, also opposed Mau Mau as a threat to their communities.

Historians' debates

  • Nationalist movement or civil war? Some historians see Mau Mau as a national liberation struggle; others stress that it was also a civil war within Kikuyu society. Kenyatta himself later called it a civil war.
  • The scale of abuses: Caroline Elkins's Pulitzer Prize-winning Imperial Reckoning (2005) argued that the detention system amounted to a "British gulag"; some historians criticised her estimates of deaths as too high, though the release of the migrated archives supported much of her account of abuse.
  • Mau Mau's own violence: historians such as David Anderson have examined both the killings by Mau Mau and the far greater violence of the colonial state.
06

The evidence

  • Colonial government files, including the "migrated archives" released after the 2011 court case.
  • Trial records of those hanged, studied by David Anderson.
  • Testimonies of former detainees, including memoirs such as J.M. Kariuki's "Mau Mau" Detainee (1963).
  • Oral histories collected by historians, and evidence given in the 2011–13 court case.
07

How is it remembered?

  • A statue of Dedan Kimathi was unveiled in Nairobi in 2007.
  • In 2015 a memorial funded by the British government as part of the 2013 settlement was unveiled in Nairobi's Uhuru Park.
  • 20 October, once Kenyatta Day, has since 2010 been Mashujaa (Heroes') Day, honouring those who fought for independence.
  • The remains of Dedan Kimathi, buried in an unmarked grave at Kamiti prison, have still not been recovered.
08

Why does it matter?

The Mau Mau uprising was one of the most violent episodes of decolonisation in the British Empire. For decades it was presented in Britain as a savage, irrational revolt; the work of historians and the testimony of survivors have revealed a struggle over land and freedom, and a system of detention and torture that the British state long concealed.

  • It accelerated the end of colonial rule in Kenya.
  • The 2013 settlement was a rare acknowledgement by a former colonial power of abuses committed during decolonisation.
  • It raises hard questions about violence, memory and justice that are still debated in both Kenya and Britain.
