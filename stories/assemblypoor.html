The Assembly of the Poor

Villagers whose fishing was ruined by a World Bank–funded dam joined with farmers and workers from across Thailand. Their Assembly of the Poor camped outside Government House to demand a say in 'development'.

When
1995 – present
Where
Pak Mun Dam, Ubon Ratchathani, Thailand
Facts checked
2026-11-16

See it on the map →

01

What is the background to this event?

From the 1950s, Thailand followed a development plan backed by the World Bank.1

  • Thailand became one of Asia's fastest-growing economies, but wealth was shared unevenly.1
  • Rural communities gave up land, rivers and forests for development, but saw few gains.1
  • The Pak Mun Dam, completed in 1994 near the mouth of the Mun River, damaged fisheries that thousands of villagers depended on.

03

What happened?

The Assembly of the Poor grew out of villagers' anger at the Pak Mun Dam.1

  • It was founded on Human Rights Day, 10 December 1995, at a conference at Thammasat University.1
  • It became the first rural political voice since the suppression of the Peasants' Federation in the 1970s.1
  • Its members issued the "Mun River Declaration", and marched through Bangkok.1
  • In 1997, thousands of members camped outside Government House in Bangkok for 99 days, winning promises of compensation.

04

What happened next?

  • The dam: after years of protest, the government agreed to open the dam's gates for part of each year to help fish migrate.
  • A model: the Assembly became a model for rural movements across Southeast Asia.2
05

What do others say?

At the time

Critics accused the Assembly of threatening national unity; members argued that development should benefit those who paid its costs.1

Debates since

  • Success? Bruce Missingham shows how local struggles became a national movement, but many of its demands were not met.2
  • Violence: the Assembly used peaceful protest camps and marches; police removed protesters at times.2
06

The evidence

  • The Mun River Declaration and the Assembly's petitions.1
07

How is it remembered?

  • The struggle over the Pak Mun Dam continues to inspire environmental movements in the Mekong region.
08

Why does it matter?

The Assembly of the Poor brought together people harmed by "development" to demand a voice in decisions about their land and rivers.1

Questions to think about

  1. Who gains and who loses from big dams?
  2. Why did villagers from different regions unite?
  3. Why did they protest outside Government House?
  4. What does 'development' mean, and who decides?
09

What other events are like this?

  • 1989Narmada Bachao Andolan — Save the Narmada — A movement of Adivasi villagers, farmers and activists challenged one of the world's largest dam projects, forcing the World Bank to withdraw and asking who pays the price of 'development'.
  • 1974The Chipko Movement — Village women in the Himalayas placed themselves between the loggers and the forest, and forced a ban on commercial felling.
  • 1973The Thai Student Uprising of 14 October 1973 — When thirteen activists were arrested for handing out leaflets calling for a constitution, half a million people filled the streets of Bangkok and brought down a military dictatorship.
