The Ixil Genocide Trial

Three decades after the army massacred Maya villages, survivors from the Ixil region testified against Guatemala's former dictator. For the first time, a former Latin American head of state was convicted of genocide in his own country — though the verdict was soon overturned.

When
19 March – 10 May 2013
Where
Guatemala City and the Ixil region, Guatemala

01

What is the background to this event?

During Guatemala's civil war, an estimated 200,000 people were killed, including at least 40,000 victims of forced disappearance.1

  • The UN-sponsored Commission for Historical Clarification found that 83% of the victims were Maya, and that government forces carried out 92% of civilian executions.1
  • Under General Efraín Ríos Montt, who ruled in 1982–83, the army treated the Ixil Maya as enemies. It acknowledged destroying 440 Maya villages between 1981 and 1983 alone.12

In their own words

03

What happened?

The trial of Ríos Montt began on 19 March 2013. It was the first time a former Latin American head of state had been tried for genocide in his own country.2

  • The charges listed the killing of 1,771 Ixil Maya, including children.2
  • Survivors from the Ixil region testified to the court.4
  • On 10 May 2013, the court convicted Ríos Montt of genocide and crimes against humanity and sentenced him to 80 years in prison. Judge Barrios said the Ixil had been treated as public enemies of the state and as an inferior race.2
04

What happened next?

  • Overturned: on 20 May 2013, Guatemala's Constitutional Court overturned the conviction, saying Ríos Montt had not been allowed an effective defence during part of the proceedings.2
  • Unfinished: a retrial began in 2015 but was held in private; Ríos Montt died in April 2018 before it was completed.2
05

What do others say?

At the time

Announcing the verdict, Judge Barrios declared that the defendant was 'responsible for masterminding the crime of genocide'; days later the Constitutional Court ruled that his right to a defence had not been respected.2

Debates since

  • Justice at home: unlike Nuremberg, the trial was held in the country where the crimes took place, by its own courts — and showed both the possibilities and the limits of that.2
06

The evidence

  • The reports of Guatemala's truth commissions, survivors' testimony, and forensic exhumations of mass graves.1

How is it remembered?

  • Hundreds of filmed interviews with survivors of the Guatemalan genocide have been recorded for the USC Shoah Foundation.2
08

Why does it matter?

Although it was overturned, the verdict recorded in a national court what the survivors had said for decades: that the killing of the Ixil was genocide.2

Open questions

  1. Why did it take thirty years to bring the case to court?
  2. What did it mean for the survivors to testify in their own country?
  3. Does a verdict still matter if it is later overturned?

What other events are like this?

  • 2016The Victims Who Brought Hissène Habré to Trial — A former prisoner gathered the testimonies of hundreds of survivors of Hissène Habré's secret police. After 25 years of campaigning, Habré became the first former head of state convicted by another country's courts for crimes against humanity.
  • 1977The Mothers of the Plaza de Mayo — The mothers of Argentina's 'disappeared' walked in silent circles in front of the presidential palace, in open defiance of a military dictatorship.
  • 1945Nuremberg: Witnesses Against the Nazi Leaders — The victorious Allies put the surviving leaders of Nazi Germany on trial for crimes against humanity. The prosecutors relied mostly on the Nazis' own documents — but a few survivors, like the French resistance fighter Marie-Claude Vaillant-Couturier, spoke for those who could not.
