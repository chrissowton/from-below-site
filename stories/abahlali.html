Abahlali baseMjondolo — the Shack Dwellers' Movement

Residents of a Durban shack settlement blocked a highway to demand the land and homes they had been promised. Their movement grew to tens of thousands — and has faced arrests, attacks and the killing of its leaders.

When
2005 – present
Where
Kennedy Road and other settlements, Durban, South Africa
Facts checked
2026-11-16

01

What is the background to this event?

After apartheid ended in 1994, millions of South Africans still lived in informal shack settlements, often without water, electricity or toilets.

  • In 2001, the eThekwini (Durban) municipality began a programme to clear informal settlements.1
  • Residents of the Kennedy Road settlement had been promised land nearby for housing.1

In their own words

03

What happened?

In early 2005, 750 people from the Kennedy Road settlement blocked the N2 highway for four hours with a burning barricade. Fourteen people were arrested.1

  • Out of these protests grew Abahlali baseMjondolo ("the residents of the shacks").1
  • It opposed demolitions and forced removals, occupied land, and made its own water and electricity connections.1
  • It boycotted elections under the slogan "No Land! No House! No Vote!"1
  • In May 2008, when xenophobic attacks swept South Africa, it opposed them and sheltered people who were displaced.1
  • In October 2009, it won a case in the Constitutional Court that declared the KwaZulu-Natal Slums Act unconstitutional.1

Repression

On 26 September 2009, a youth meeting in Kennedy Road was attacked; two people were killed in the conflict that followed, and many members' homes were destroyed.1 Since 2013, more than twenty of the movement's leaders and members have been killed. The movement blames the governing ANC; the ANC denies involvement.1

04

What happened next?

  • Growth: by 2022, the movement claimed more than 115,000 members in 81 branches in four provinces.1
  • Courts: it has used the courts to challenge evictions and police violence.1
  • International attention: the killings of its members have been raised by Amnesty International and at the UN Human Rights Council.13
05

Why does it matter?

Abahlali shows that the struggle for land and housing did not end with apartheid, and that poor people can organise democratically for themselves.

  • It has linked housing to dignity and democracy.
  • The violence against it raises questions about democracy in South Africa.
  • By 2022 the movement claimed more than 115,000 members in 81 branches across four provinces.1

What do others say?How it was seen at the time, and where historians disagree

At the time

Local officials accused the movement of being manipulated by outsiders and of illegal occupations; the movement insisted it was run democratically by its members.1

Debates since

  • Violence: the 2009 Kennedy Road events are disputed. Movement members were charged after the clashes but were later acquitted; the movement says it was the victim of an attack by ANC supporters.14
  • Tactics: road blockades and self-connections to electricity are seen by critics as illegal and dangerous, and by supporters as necessary.

Reading it

Members protest outside the Constitutional Court in 2009, when they challenged the KwaZulu-Natal Slums Act; the court ruled in their favour that October. A movement of the poor using the highest court in the land shows its two strategies at once: the street and the law.
