Rongelap: Leaving Home to Escape the Fallout

Showered with fallout from a US hydrogen bomb, then told their island was safe, the people of Rongelap finally decided to leave — and asked Greenpeace to carry them away.

When
1954 – 1985
Where
Rongelap Atoll, Marshall Islands
Facts checked
2026-11-16

See it on the map →

01

What is the background to this event?

After the Second World War, the United States governed the Marshall Islands as a UN Trust Territory. Between 1946 and 1958 it carried out 67 nuclear tests there, at Bikini and Enewetak atolls.

  • On 1 March 1954, the "Castle Bravo" hydrogen bomb exploded at Bikini with two and a half times the expected force, spreading radioactive fallout across a wide area.1
  • Fallout fell like snow on Rongelap, about 180 kilometres away. The 64 people living there were not evacuated for two days.1
  • Many suffered burns, hair loss and nausea. Later, many developed thyroid tumours, and children died of leukaemia.1
02

Who were the key people?

  • John Anjain

    The magistrate of Rongelap, whose son died of leukaemia. He appealed for international help.

  • Jeton Anjain

    A Marshall Islands senator who asked Greenpeace to help relocate his people.

  • The people of Rongelap

    About 350 people who chose to leave their ancestral home in 1985.

  • The crew of the Rainbow Warrior

    The Greenpeace ship that carried the islanders and their homes to Mejato.

In their own words

03

What happened?

In 1957, the US government declared Rongelap "clean and safe" and returned the islanders, telling them to eat canned food and avoid the northern islets.1 A US scientist noted that their return would provide valuable data on radiation in human beings.1

Evidence of continuing contamination mounted.1 The islanders felt they were being used as experimental subjects.

  • In 1984, Senator Jeton Anjain asked Greenpeace to help move his people.1
  • In May 1985, in "Operation Exodus", the Rainbow Warrior made three trips, carrying about 350 people and 100 tonnes of building materials to Mejato island in Kwajalein Atoll, about 180 kilometres away.1
  • The islanders dismantled their houses and took them with them.

Weeks later, on 10 July 1985, French secret agents sank the Rainbow Warrior in Auckland harbour, New Zealand, before it could sail to protest at Moruroa, killing the photographer Fernando Pereira.

04

What happened next?

  • Life in exile: life on Mejato and the crowded island of Ebeye was hard, with joblessness and overcrowding.1
  • Recognition: in 1991, the people of Rongelap and Jeton Anjain received the Right Livelihood Award for their struggle.1
  • Compensation: in 1996, the US signed a $45 million resettlement agreement. Some islanders have returned, but many remain wary.1
  • Continuing claims: the Marshall Islands has continued to press the US for further compensation, arguing that earlier settlements were inadequate.
05

What do others say?

At the time

The US government maintained that the fallout was an accident and that Rongelap was safe after 1957. Islanders and their supporters believed the US had knowingly exposed them and then studied them.

Debates since

  • Was it deliberate? Declassified documents show the US monitored the islanders closely; researchers debate whether the exposure was negligence or worse.
  • Is it safe to return? Scientists disagree about the levels of residual radiation and the safety of local foods.
  • Compensation: the US says it has paid fully; the Marshall Islands says the damage was far greater.1
06

The evidence

  • Declassified US government records and medical studies.
  • Testimonies of Rongelap islanders to the US Congress and the Nuclear Claims Tribunal.
  • Films such as Bikini – mon amour, which recorded the Greenpeace evacuation.1
07

How is it remembered?

  • The Marshall Islands marks 1 March as Nuclear Victims' Remembrance Day.
  • The sinking of the Rainbow Warrior is commemorated in New Zealand.
08

Why does it matter?

Rongelap is one of the starkest examples of how nuclear testing harmed Pacific peoples. Its people took their fate into their own hands by leaving their homeland.

  • It raises questions about informed consent and the use of people as research subjects.
  • It links the struggle against nuclear weapons with environmental justice.
  • Rising seas now threaten the Marshall Islands again.

Questions to think about

  1. Why do you think the people of Rongelap decided to leave their island?
  2. Why did they ask an environmental organisation rather than their government for help?
  3. Is it ever right to use people as research subjects without their consent?
  4. What do powerful countries owe to people harmed by their weapons?

What other events are like this?

  • 1995Against the Bomb: French Polynesia and Moruroa — For thirty years, France tested nuclear weapons on Polynesian atolls. When testing resumed in 1995, Tahitians rose in protest — and Pacific peoples demanded a nuclear-free ocean.
  • 1976Protect Kahoʻolawe: Reclaiming a Sacred Island — Native Hawaiian activists landed on an island the US Navy had been bombing for decades. Their campaign stopped the bombing and won the island back for the Hawaiian people.
  • 1956Minamata: The Struggle for Justice — A chemical company poisoned a fishing community with mercury for decades. Its victims fought for recognition and justice — and changed environmental law in Japan and the world.
