The Hut Tax War

When the British imposed a tax on every house in the new Sierra Leone Protectorate, the Temne leader Bai Bureh led a guerrilla war against them, while a separate Mende rising killed traders and missionaries. Both were crushed.

When
February – November 1898
Where
Port Loko and the Sierra Leone hinterland

See it on the map →

01

What is the background to this event?

In 1896, Britain declared a protectorate over the interior of Sierra Leone, beyond the coastal colony of Freetown.1

  • Governor Frederic Cardew introduced a tax on every dwelling from 1 January 1898: 10 shillings a year for larger houses and 5 shillings for smaller ones.1
  • Chiefs saw the tax as an attack on their authority and on their ownership of the land. Twenty-four chiefs petitioned against it.1
  • The tax was collected by the Frontier Police, who were often accused of abuses.1
02

Who were the key people?

  • Bai Bureh

    A Temne chief and experienced war leader, who led the resistance in the north.

  • Frederic Cardew

    Governor of Sierra Leone, who imposed the tax.

  • Nyagua and Kpana Lewis

    Mende chiefs exiled with Bai Bureh after the war.

  • Momoh Jah

    A leader of the Mende rising in the south.

  • Sir David Chalmers

    The commissioner who investigated the causes of the war.

03

What happened?

In February 1898, the British tried to arrest Bai Bureh, on a rumour that he planned to resist the tax. He took up arms.1

  • Bai Bureh's forces fought a skilful guerrilla war in the north, ambushing British columns from stockades in the forest.1
  • The British used scorched-earth tactics, burning villages and crops.1
  • In April 1898, a separate rising broke out among the Mende in the south. The rebels killed missionaries, Krio traders from Freetown, and others associated with the colonial government — possibly around 1,000 people.2
  • British forces lost 67 soldiers killed and 184 wounded, along with about 90 porters. African losses were much heavier, but were not counted.1
  • Bai Bureh surrendered on 11 November 1898.1

Many Mende rebels were tried, and 96 were hanged.2

04

What happened next?

  • Exile: Bai Bureh, Nyagua and Kpana Lewis were exiled to the Gold Coast (now Ghana). Bai Bureh was allowed to return in 1905, and died in 1908.3
  • The Chalmers Report blamed the tax and the conduct of the Frontier Police, but the British government kept the tax.1
  • Krio and the interior: the killing of Krio traders deepened divisions between Freetown and the interior.2
05

What do others say?

At the time

The British press presented the war as a savage uprising. Commissioner Chalmers blamed the tax and the police; Cardew and the Colonial Office rejected his findings.1

Debates since

  • Two wars: historians distinguish Bai Bureh's war against British troops from the Mende rising, which targeted civilians.2
  • Numbers: the number of civilians killed by Mende rebels and of Africans killed by British forces is uncertain.2
  • Hero: Bai Bureh is celebrated as an anti-colonial hero; some historians stress his own political aims as a chief.

07

How is it remembered?

  • Bai Bureh is a national hero in Sierra Leone, and appears on banknotes.
  • Objects linked to him are displayed in the Sierra Leone National Museum.
08

Why does it matter?

The Hut Tax War was one of the most serious challenges to British rule in West Africa in the 1890s.

  • It shows how colonial taxes were used to force people into the cash economy.
  • It also shows how resistance could include violence against civilians.
